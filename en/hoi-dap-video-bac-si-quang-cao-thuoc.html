Is that doctor selling medicine in a Facebook video real or fake?

Answered in a minute, with the warning signs and what to do right now.

Short answer

Very likely a deepfake

Many of these videos are deepfakes: scammers take real doctors' lectures or interviews and make that face say a new sales pitch. Vietnamese rules ban using doctors' names, images or medical facilities to advertise food products, so a supplement ad fronted by a doctor is wrong from the start.

How the trap works

Steps of the trap

  1. 1Fake doctor video
  2. 2Fake news page, thank-you comments
  3. 3A “pharmacist” calls you back

Red flags

Red flags

  • Lips out of syncMouth closed while sound continues.
  • Promises a full cure, no more medicineReal doctors almost never promise that.
  • Buy today or miss outMedicine isn't sold on a countdown.
  • Leads to a phone number or chatNot to the hospital's own website.

Do this

Do this

  1. Find the official pageSearch the doctor plus hospital; look for a denial.
  2. Look up the product registrationIn Vietnam: Drug Administration for medicine, Food Safety Authority for supplements.
  3. Do a reverse image searchIt often finds the original video on another topic.
  4. Ask your own doctorAny change to medication goes through your treating doctor.

More questions

People also ask

My parents believe the ad. How do I talk to them without a fight?

Try three steps: ask instead of judging (where did you see this?); look it up together instead of for them; and give them a way out by saying the video is so convincing that doctors have had to issue denials.

We already bought it. Now what?

Don't stop prescribed medicine. Show the product to the treating doctor before using it. Supplements aren't medicine and can't replace treatment.

How do I report a fake ad?

Tap the three dots on the ad, choose report ad, and pick scam. Every report helps get it removed sooner.
