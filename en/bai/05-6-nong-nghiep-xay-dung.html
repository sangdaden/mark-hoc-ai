Crop-spraying drones, self-driving tractors, site scans

Spraying drones follow the field, self-driving tractors use stereo cameras, and LiDAR scans buildings under construction.

Watch on

Drones over the rice fields

Many Vietnamese farmers now hire drones to spray their rice instead of carrying a backpack sprayer. To fly just above the crop without hitting trees or power lines, a drone needs eyes too.

FARMING

Following the field

One popular drone model carries radar at the front and back to measure height and detect obstacles, plus two pairs of stereo cameras to see the terrain. When the field's edge rises, the drone climbs so the spray covers evenly.

THE NUMBERS

A hectare in minutes

With a 6-meter spray width at 6 meters per second, a drone covers one hectare in about four and a half minutes, not counting turns. Spraying by hand usually takes hours.

TRACTORS

Self-driving with stereo cameras

John Deere's self-driving tractor, launched in 2022, uses six pairs of stereo cameras to see all around. The company chose stereo cameras because they judge distance cheaply, the same two-eye principle from part 3.

CONSTRUCTION

Scanning the site

On building sites, LiDAR scans the structure under construction into a point cloud and overlays it on the 3D design. A column out of line or a wall in the wrong place shows up in red, so it can be fixed before the next floor is poured.

THE NUMBERS

Measuring a sand pile

To measure a pile of sand, a drone scans it and adds up the height of every small square. The channel tested a pile 3 meters high and 10 meters wide and got 79 cubic meters, less than 1 percent off.

Try it now

3D scan with your phone

Install a free 3D scanning app such as Scaniverse and walk once around a potted plant. Phones with LiDAR scan faster, but phones without it can still build a model from photos.
