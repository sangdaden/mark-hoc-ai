Part 1

The question behind the experiment

Can a 4-year-old's ability to wait predict their future? The current answer: a little, far less than the legend says, and it depends largely on circumstances and on how children are taught to wait.

Key concepts

The hot and cool systems

hot · cool systemMischel and Metcalfe, 1999

How good waiters do it

self-distraction · attentionMischel, 1970s

Out of sight

When the treat was covered, children waited much longer than when it sat right in front of them.

Think of something else

Singing, counting fingers, playing with their feet; some children even fell asleep on the table.

If… then…

"If I feel like eating it, then I'll look out the window." A ready plan means no decision has to be made in the middle of a craving.

This is Mischel's main finding: the ability to wait depends on attention strategies, and strategies can be taught.

Culture and era change the results too

culture · cohortCarlson 2018 · Yanaoka 2022

Children today wait longer

Comparing samples from the 1960s to the 2000s, later children waited longer than earlier ones, despite worries about the "screen age".

Japan waits for food, the US for gifts

Japanese children are used to waiting until the whole family can eat; American children are used to waiting before opening presents. Each is better at waiting for what they are used to waiting for.

The 20-second threshold

In the 2018 replication, most of the difference was whether a child could wait 20 seconds at all; waiting longer than that added almost nothing.

Evidence: how far can we trust it

10 common claims about the experiment, checked against current research. Tap each row to see why.

3 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDCovering the reward and self-distraction help children wait longer

Mischel's series of experiments in the 1970s gave consistent results and has been replicated many times. This is the most solid part, and it was the original purpose of the experiment.

SOLIDWaiting time is linked to later academic results

The replication by Watts, Duncan and Quan (2018) still found a link, but a small one: 0.24, dropping to 0.08 after accounting for family background, home environment and early cognitive ability.

SOLIDChildhood self-control is linked to adult health and finances

The Dunedin study (Moffitt and colleagues, 2011) followed about 1,000 people from birth to age 32. But it measured self-control in many ways over many years, not with a single treat test.

CAUTIONWaiting for the treat predicts SAT scores (correlation 0.42–0.57)

Shoda, Mischel and Peake (1990) managed to reach only a small share of the children; the highest correlations came from about 35 children, almost all from the same social class. Samples like that easily inflate the numbers.

CAUTIONTrust in adults decides whether children wait

Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin (2013) had only 28 children, but the effect was very large (3 minutes versus 12). Later studies on trust and social groups point the same way, with smaller effects.

CAUTIONChildren today wait better than children in the 1960s

Carlson and colleagues (2018) compared different samples at different times, so the cause is hard to isolate. An interesting result, but not yet certain.

CAUTIONGood waiters as children have different brains as adults

Casey and colleagues (2011) scanned the brains of about 26 people from the original sample, grouped by how they did on self-control tests over many years. A very small sample for a conclusion about the brain.

CAUTIONCulture decides what children are good at waiting for

Yanaoka and colleagues (2022) compared Japanese and American children: one study, two countries, a clear result. Plausible, but it needs many more cultures.

CRITICISEDThe marshmallow test predicts lifelong success

The popular reading in self-help books, TED talks and adverts. Larger data show that a single test at age 4 says very little, and most of that comes from family background. Mischel also pushed back against this reading many times.

CRITICISEDChildren who eat right away lack innate willpower

Waiting time changes with how the reward is presented, which strategies are suggested, trust in adults and culture. It is behaviour that depends on circumstances, not a fixed trait.

Strengths
  • Simple, anyone can rerun it
  • Shows self-control is about strategy and can be taught
  • Rechecked many times, in the open
Weaknesses
  • Small original sample, only well-off Stanford families
  • One measurement at age 4 has been read into far too much
  • Ignores good reasons not to wait

A good experiment about how children hold themselves back, turned into a prophecy. Keep the waiting tricks, drop the prophecy.

More: why the legend spread faster than the replications

The story of "one marshmallow predicts the future" has everything it needs to spread: cute children, a simple choice, and a lesson about willpower that suits a culture that prizes individual effort. It appeared in Daniel Goleman's books, on TED and in many skills-training programmes.

The 2018 replication does not say the original experiment was wrong. It says the link is real but small, and most of the predictive power comes from family circumstances that sit behind both the waiting and the good grades. It is a familiar example of psychology's "replication crisis": small sample, pretty result, spreads fast; large sample, modest result, rarely mentioned.

Mischel, in his own 2014 book, stressed that self-control is a skill that can be learned, and that the test result is no child's destiny.

Part 6

Lessons to apply

A 4-year-old's tricks still work for adults: change how you see the temptation, don't white-knuckle it.

Out of sight, easier to waitHide the temptation before you need willpower.
Make it a "cloud"Describe the temptation by its shape, not its taste.
Keep your word with childrenChildren only wait when they believe waiting is worth it.
Self-control is not something you either have or lack; it is something you learn.A paraphrase of the spirit of The Marshmallow Test (Mischel, 2014), not a direct quote.
