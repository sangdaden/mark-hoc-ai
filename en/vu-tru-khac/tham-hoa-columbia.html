Part 1

The question

Seventeen years after Challenger, the same organisation had a new safety office and a whole book of lessons. Why did it lose a shuttle to the same old mistake: a repeated defect treated as normal, and engineers once again asked to prove there was danger?

Part 2

What happened

Timeline from chapter 3 and section 6.3 of the CAIB report. Times are US Eastern.

16 Jan 2003, 10:39 · LaunchAt 81.7 s a piece of foam separates from the left bipod ramp of the External Tank. At 81.9 s it strikes the left wing's leading edge near panel 8, at a relative speed of about 545 mph (877 km/h).
17 Jan · Flight Day 2The photo team spots the strike on launch film. The log says the vehicle "may have sustained damage from a debris strike".
18–19 Jan · Boeing's estimateDebris about 20 × 20 × 2 inches at about 750 feet per second. The Board later called the estimates "remarkably accurate".
21–22 Jan · Three imagery requests, then cancellationEngineer Rodney Rocha writes: "Can we petition (beg) for outside agency assistance?" On the morning of 22 Jan the request to the military is cancelled at 8:30.
23 Jan · E-mail to commander and pilot"There is absolutely no concern for entry." The crew is sent a video clip so they can answer press questions.
1 Feb, 8:44 · Entry interface270 seconds later the on-board recorder logs the first anomaly. At 8:54 Mission Control notices. Around 9:00 the orbiter breaks apart over Texas.

Sources: CAIB report, chapter 3, pp. 49, 54, 60, 65 (launch time, 81.7 and 81.9 s, panel 8, 545 mph, re-entry times); section 6.3 (17 Jan log, Boeing's estimate, Rocha's e-mail, cancellation at 8:30, 23 Jan e-mail). Chapter 3 also gives a 625–840 ft/s range from image analysis.

Schedule

The 19 February 2004 date for the station's Node 2 "influenced many of decisions". During the flight, managers worried about the strike's effect on the launch schedule (CAIB, section 6.2, F6.2-7).

A shrinking budget

The Shuttle programme lost about 40 percent of its budget's purchasing power. "Faster, better, cheaper" was the 1990s slogan (CAIB, chapter 5, pp. 103–104).

Safety without independence

Section 7.4 is titled "A Broken Safety Culture". The safety office "does not have the independence and authority" (CAIB, chapter 7, pp. 184, 192).
Part 4

Fact check: what's true, what's myth

13 things often said about Columbia, checked against the original record. Tap a row for the reasoning.

5 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDFoam from the left bipod ramp holed the left wing's leading edge

The Board's finding on the physical cause (Board Statement, chapter 3), backed by launch film, sensor data inside the wing and the foam-impact test.

SOLIDNASA had known about falling foam for years

Foam shedding on 65 of 79 imaged flights; seven losses from the left bipod ramp; a design requirement banning debris (CAIB, section 6.1).

SOLIDEngineers asked for pictures of the wing and were stopped

Three separate requests, none through the formal channel. The request to the military was cancelled on the morning of 22 Jan, 90 minutes after it was made (CAIB, section 6.3).

SOLIDThere was schedule pressure

The Node 2 date of 19 Feb 2004 "influenced many of decisions", and during the flight managers worried about the strike's effect on the launch schedule (CAIB, section 6.2, F6.2-7).

SOLIDColumbia repeated Challenger's organisational causes

Chapter 8: the causes of the institutional failure behind Challenger "have not been fixed"; dangerous aspects of NASA's 1986 culture "remained unchanged".

CAUTIONIf they had known, the crew would certainly have been saved

The study for the Board called the rescue "challenging but feasible", provided the damage was recognised before Flight Day 7. It was done after the accident, and rushing Atlantis to launch carried its own risk. "Possible" is right; "certain" is not.

CAUTIONA PowerPoint slide killed seven people

The slide is the most memorable symptom. The Board gave it a sidebar in chapter 7, but concluded that culture and structure "had as much to do with this accident" as the foam. Remove the slide but keep the question "is there a requirement?" and the outcome might well have been the same.

CAUTIONBudget cuts caused the accident

A loss of about 40 percent in purchasing power is real context. But the Board says the roots "can also be traced, in part," to the post-Cold War policy environment, and does not call it a direct cause (CAIB, chapter 5, pp. 99, 104).

CAUTIONThe heat tiles broke

The hole was in the reinforced carbon-carbon (RCC) leading edge, panel 8, not in the tiles on the orbiter's belly. Both are part of the thermal protection system, so this is only half right (CAIB, chapter 3).

CAUTIONThe crew died instantly, knowing nothing

The crew survival report (NASA, 2008): about 40 seconds passed between loss of control and cabin depressurisation, and "part of this short timeframe was undoubtedly employed in recognizing that a problem existed". After depressurisation the crew were incapacitated "within seconds". The accident "was not survivable".

FALSENASA could not have done anything to save the crew

The most repeated line. The Board wrote that had managers understood the threat, repair and rescue plans "would most likely have been developed, and a rescue would have been conceivable" (CAIB, section 6.4). The truer line: you cannot save what you refuse to look at.

FALSELight foam can't break a wing

That was also many engineers' feeling in 2003: a 17 Jan e-mail said the debris "didn't look like a big enough piece to pose any serious threat". The 7 July 2003 test fired 1.67 lb of foam at panel 8 and made a hole about 16 inches across (CAIB, section 6.3; CAIB test photo).

FALSENASA knew the orbiter was doomed and hid it from the crew

Managers believed there was no dangerous damage, so the e-mail to the crew said "absolutely no concern for entry" (CAIB, section 6.3). In 2013 Wayne Hale recalled a colleague saying "if it has been damaged, it's probably better not to know", but said "There was never any debate about what to tell the crew" (AP, 2013, secondary). The real failure was refusing to look, not hiding.

The disaster of an organisation that already had the answer in 1986, but kept it in a report instead of turning it into structure.

More: after Columbia

The Board made 29 recommendations, 15 of them required before flights resumed, including the ability to inspect and repair the thermal protection system in orbit and an independent technical authority. The return-to-flight mission, STS-114, launched on 26 July 2005. Launch imagery again showed a piece of foam coming off the External Tank, and an astronaut went outside to pull two protruding gap fillers from between the tiles.

This time, NASA looked. The Shuttle programme ended in 2011 after 135 flights and two losses: Challenger and Columbia.

Sources: CRS RS21606 (2003); NASA, STS-114 page; NASA (135 flights, 1981–2011).

Part 6

Work lesson: why organisations repeat their mistakes

For every risk report, every slide for the boss, every "known issue" on the backlog.

Count "familiar" defects as a trendThe Board found NASA "failed to adequately perform trend analysis on foam losses", almost the sentence written about O-rings in 1986.
Ask: how far outside our data are we?A model calibrated on 3-cubic-inch pieces says nothing about a 1,920-cubic-inch one.
Give worried people a channel around the hierarchyRocha's e-mail went unsent for fear of jumping the chain of command. A good channel doesn't require courage.
Ask regularly: is the old lesson still alive?Pick your team's last big incident. What changed in the structure, not just in the documents?

Sources: CAIB report, F6.1-10 (trend analysis), section 6.3 (Crater, Rocha's e-mail). The 1986 line: "little or no trend analysis", Rogers Commission report, chapter 7.

If these persistent, systemic flaws are not resolved, the scene is set for another accident.Columbia Accident Investigation Board report, chapter 8, p. 195 (2003).
