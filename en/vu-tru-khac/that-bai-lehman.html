Part 1

The question of the case

A 158-year-old bank: how could it collapse in a single weekend? The answer is not in that weekend but in the years before it: borrowing too much, holding too much real estate, and making quarter-end numbers look better than reality.

2006–2007Speeding upBorrowed money poured into commercial real estate and leveraged loans, just as the market started to turn.
→
late 2007 – mid 2008Dressing the booksEvery quarter-end, tens of billions of dollars of assets briefly left the balance sheet. Leverage looked lower than it was.
→
Sep 2008Loss of trustTwo loss-making quarters, no buyer, no rescue money. Bankruptcy filed in the early hours of 15 Sep.

Why: five layers of causes

Strategy layer: borrowing a lot to bet big

leverage · countercyclical growth10-K 2007; Valukas, vol. 1

Accounting layer: Repo 105, assets that vanish on closing day

Repo 105 · quarter-end window dressingValukas, vol. 3

Motive layer: one number the market was watching

net leverage targetValukas, vol. 3

Organisation layer: limits raised, the messenger sidelined

risk limits · whistleblower · auditorValukas, vols. 1 and 3

Limits raised

In the second half of 2007 Lehman persistently breached its own "risk appetite" limits. By year-end the breaches were gone, but by raising the limits, not by cutting risk.

Risk left uncounted

From about May to August 2007, a $2.3 billion bridge equity position in the Archstone deal was left out of the risk usage calculation.

The whistleblower letter

In May 2008 senior vice president Matthew Lee wrote a letter alleging accounting improprieties. On 12 June he met auditor Ernst & Young; according to Valukas, he raised Repo 105. In June he lost his job in a round of layoffs.

Source: Valukas report, vol. 1 (risk limits, Archstone $2.3 billion) and vol. 3 (Matthew Lee, E&Y), via Risk.net, Seton Hall Law (Denbeaux), The Economist 12 Mar 2010 and Public Integrity (secondary sources).

Valukas concluded there were colorable claims against CEO Richard Fuld and three chief financial officers over misleading 2007 10-K and Q1 and Q2 2008 10-Q reports, and against auditor E&Y for failing to question the disclosures. He found no colorable claims over the risky business decisions themselves: US law lets managers make business mistakes, but not misreport them.

Source: Valukas House testimony of 20 Apr 2010; Valukas report, vol. 1, executive summary.

Part 4

Fact-check: what is true and what is myth

13 things often said about Lehman, checked against the Valukas report, Lehman's SEC filings, the bankruptcy filing and the FCIC. Tap each line for the reasoning.

3 SOLID
5 CAUTION
5 FALSE
SOLID"The largest bankruptcy in US history"

The Chapter 11 petition of 15 Sep 2008 listed $639 billion of assets and $613 billion of debts (as of 31 May 2008). The previous record was WorldCom (AP, 15 Sep 2008, secondary source).

SOLID"A 158-year-old bank"

2007 10-K: founded in 1850. From 1850 to 2008 is 158 years.

SOLID"Lehman pushed about $50 billion off its books at quarter-end"

$50.38 billion at the end of Q2 2008 (Valukas testimony, 6 Apr 2011). The assets were not sold for good: a few days after closing day, Lehman bought them back.

CAUTION"Repo 105 is what brought Lehman down"

Repo 105 hid leverage; it did not create losses. The losses came from real estate and mortgages (8-K of 10 Sep 2008) on a heavily borrowed balance sheet. Repo 105 made investors see it later.

CAUTION"The Fed had no legal power to save Lehman"

That is Bernanke's argument (2009): Lehman lacked collateral. Laurence Ball (NBER 2016, book 2018) disagrees: the Fed had the authority, Lehman had enough assets, and the decision was political. The question is still open.

CAUTION"Repo 105 was illegal in the US, so it was done in London"

No US law firm would opine it was a "true sale"; Lehman relied on a Linklaters opinion under English law, so the deals went through its London arm. Valukas did not conclude Linklaters was wrong. Secondary sources summarising vol. 3.

CAUTION"Fuld knew all about Repo 105"

Fuld told the House (20 Apr 2010) he had no recollection of hearing about Repo 105. Valukas said there was evidence to the contrary. This is the examiner's view, never tested in court.

CAUTION"Lehman was just unlucky with the housing crash"

The crash was real. But Valukas describes a choice to "double down" after the market had turned, breaching risk limits and then raising them (vol. 1).

FALSE"Lehman collapsed only because the government didn't save it"

The word "only" is wrong. Two losing quarters (~$2.8 and ~$3.9 billion), leverage of about 30x and hard-to-sell real estate came before the weekend of 13–14 Sep. The government closed the last door; it did not set the house on fire.

FALSE"People went to prison over Repo 105"

No one was charged. Valukas's "colorable claims" are not a verdict. The SEC closed its investigation in 2012 without bringing a case (secondary source).

FALSE"No one inside raised the alarm"

Matthew Lee's letter in May 2008; Bart McDade's "another drug we r on" email in April 2008 (Valukas report, vol. 3, via secondary sources).

FALSE"The auditors knew nothing"

Valukas: Ernst & Young knew Lehman used Repo 105 and did not challenge it. E&Y disputed this, then settled for $99 million with investors (2013) and $10 million with the New York Attorney General (2015), without admitting wrongdoing (secondary sources for both settlements).

FALSE"Creditors lost everything"

By the 30th distribution (April 2025), the Lehman estate had paid creditors $129.4 billion, of which $96.4 billion went to third-party creditors. Many still lost money, but not everything.

Source: Chapter 11 petition (15 Sep 2008); 2007 10-K; Lehman Form 8-K of 9 Jun and 10 Sep 2008; Valukas report, vols. 1 and 3; Valukas testimony of 20 Apr 2010 and 6 Apr 2011; Bernanke (2009); Ball (2016, 2018); Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc. Plan Trust Form 8-K (2025). E&Y settlements and SEC closing its probe: D&O Diary Dec 2013, CFO.com Apr 2015 (secondary sources).

Lehman did not fall because of one weekend. It fell because of years of over-borrowing, and because of numbers chosen to look fine on exactly the day people looked.

More: why "valid on paper" can still be window dressing

Valukas did not say every repo was wrong. What he pointed to was purpose: Repo 105 was used most heavily right around quarter-end and was not disclosed, so readers of the reports saw lower leverage than reality.

This is where the lesson enters an ordinary office. A technique can be within the rules and still mislead the reader. The question worth asking is not "is it allowed?" but "if it weren't reporting day, would we do this?".

Source for "More": Valukas report, vol. 3 and testimony of 20 Apr 2010; the rest is Mark's commentary.

Part 6

Work lesson: be careful when numbers are "dressed up" at period-end

Applies to any number with a closing date: month-end sales, tickets closed at sprint end, quarter-end inventory, the metric you report upward.

Compare the 15th with the 30thSelf-check: take your team's main metric mid-period and at period-end. A big gap deserves a "why".
Count what lands in the last 3 daysWhat share of orders, tickets or contracts falls in the last 3 days of the period? A rising share is a signal.
A log for every limit increaseBudget, deadline, risk threshold: each increase records who approved it and why. Two in a row means a meeting.
A channel to say "this number isn't real"Who on the team may say it, to whom, and how they are protected. Write it down before you need it.
