Part 2

The story

Who, where, when: a discovery pieced together by many people in many countries.

1987 · Japan: odd repeats in E. coliYoshizumi Ishino's team sequenced a gene of the gut bacterium and found, right next to it, a short sequence repeated again and again with different stretches in between. Nobody knew what it was for.
Early 1990s · Spain: the Santa Pola salt marshesFrancisco Mojica, a PhD student in Alicante, found similar repeats in a salt-loving archaeon from the salt ponds. He chased the question "what are they for" for more than ten years.
2002 · A nameRuud Jansen's team (Netherlands) coined CRISPR, for "clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats", and found the cas genes that always sit nearby.
2005 · The answer: bits of virus DNAMojica compared the in-between stretches with sequence databases and found they matched DNA from viruses and plasmids. His guess: this is the immune memory of bacteria.
2007 · Yogurt proves itAt the dairy company Danisco, Rodolphe Barrangou, Philippe Horvath and colleagues showed that yogurt bacteria add a new piece of virus to their CRISPR after an attack, and from then on resist that virus.
2011 · The missing pieceEmmanuelle Charpentier (then in Sweden) found tracrRNA, an RNA molecule the scissors need. The same year she began working with Jennifer Doudna (Berkeley, USA).
28 June 2012 · The Science paperJinek, Doudna, Charpentier and colleagues showed that the Cas9 protein, guided by two RNAs, cuts both strands of DNA at the right place. Join the two RNAs into one and you have "programmable" scissors: change the RNA, change the cut. The same year, Virginijus Šikšnys's team (Lithuania) independently reported Cas9 cutting DNA guided by RNA.
2013 · Into human cellsFeng Zhang's team (Broad Institute) and George Church's team (Harvard) showed Cas9 can edit genes in human and mouse cells. From then on, thousands of labs could use CRISPR.
7 Oct 2020 · Nobel Prize in ChemistryAwarded to Charpentier and Doudna "for the development of a method for genome editing".

Why do many people think CRISPR was "invented by two people"?

A Nobel Prize goes to at most three people a year, and in 2020 it went to the two scientists who turned a bacterial mechanism into a tool. But the story spans at least four countries: Japan (the first repeats), Spain and the Netherlands (understanding and naming), France and Denmark (Danisco's yogurt experiments), Lithuania and the US (the scissors in a test tube and in human cells).

Two US institutions (the University of California, where Doudna works, and the Broad Institute, where Zhang works) are still fighting over patents. In May 2025 a US federal appeals court sent the case back to the Patent Office. Who "thought of it first" is still legally unsettled.

Source: Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2020 press release; US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit decision of 12 May 2025 in Regents of the University of California v. Broad Institute, via summaries by MoFo and White & Case (secondary sources).

Part 3

How the gene works

1. A guide RNA carries about 20 letters that match the DNA stretch to be found. It walks the Cas9 protein along the genome until it hits the match.

2. At the match, Cas9 cuts both DNA strands. The cell rushes to repair the cut; in doing so it may break the gene (switch it off) or copy a repair template the scientists supplied.

3. Newer versions such as "base editing" do not cut through the DNA but change exactly one letter, for example A to G. This is the approach used for baby KJ.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is rumour

14 things often said about CRISPR, checked against the original papers and regulators' decisions. Tap a row for the reasoning.

9 SOLID
3 CAUTION
2 FALSE
SOLIDCRISPR started as a bacterial immune system against viruses

The in-between stretches match virus DNA (Mojica 2005); yogurt bacteria copy a new piece of virus and become resistant to it (Barrangou 2007).

SOLIDThe odd repeats were first seen in 1987, when nobody understood them

Ishino et al. (1987) saw repeats next to the iap gene in E. coli; Nakata et al. (1989) described a 29-base-pair sequence repeated 14 times.

SOLIDThe name CRISPR dates from the early 2000s

Jansen et al. (2002) used it in Molecular Microbiology and identified the accompanying cas genes.

SOLIDIn 2012, Cas9 was "programmed" with RNA to cut a chosen site

Jinek et al. (2012), Science 337: 816–821; the same year Šikšnys's team reported a similar result in PNAS.

SOLIDThe 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Charpentier and Doudna

Press release of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 7 October 2020.

SOLIDThe first CRISPR therapy was approved in 2023

The UK granted conditional authorisation on 16 Nov 2023; the US FDA approved it on 8 Dec 2023 for sickle cell disease from age 12 (MHRA, Vertex; ASGCT).

SOLIDIn the trial, 29 of 30 people had no pain crises for at least 12 months

30 people with enough follow-up out of 44 infused; 29/30 (97%) free of severe vaso-occlusive crises for at least 12 consecutive months; 30/30 free of hospital stays for them (Frangoul et al. 2024, NEJM).

SOLIDEditing body cells is not passed on to children

Only changes to eggs, sperm or embryos pass to the next generation (MedlinePlus Genetics). The story of embryo editing is piece #10.

SOLIDBaby KJ was the first person to receive a custom gene-editing drug infused straight into the body

Musunuru et al. (2025), NEJM, published 15 May 2025; Penn Medicine and CHOP releases the same day.

CAUTION"CRISPR has cured sickle cell disease for everyone"

Trial results are very good, but patients need high-dose chemotherapy (which can affect fertility), median follow-up was only about 19 months, and the US price is 2.2 million USD (Reuters, AAFP; secondary sources). Very few hospitals can do it.

CAUTION"KJ is completely cured"

His doctors say it is "still really early days" and do not call it a cure; he still needs care. No liver biopsy was done, so how many cells were edited is unknown (Penn, CHOP; press, secondary).

CAUTION"Anyone with a rare disease can now get a custom gene-editing drug"

Only one case has been published. KJ's team plans a trial for babies with similar disorders; the FDA has only a draft guidance (23 Feb 2026) for this kind of individualised drug.

FALSE"Doudna and Charpentier invented CRISPR on their own"

They turned the mechanism into a tool. Discovering the mechanism took many teams: Ishino, Mojica, Jansen, Barrangou and Horvath, Šikšnys; bringing it into human cells involved Zhang's and Church's teams.

FALSE"CRISPR is perfectly precise and never cuts the wrong place"

It can cut off-target, and right at the cut site large deletions or scrambled DNA can occur (Kosicki, Tomberg and Bradley 2018, Nature Biotechnology 36: 765–771). The Casgevy label warns of off-target editing.

Part 5

Where we are today

From the first medicine to baby KJ, and what is still pending.

16 Nov 2023 and 8 Dec 2023 · The first CRISPR medicineThe UK, then the US, approved exa-cel (brand name casgevy) for sickle cell disease; the UK also approved it for transfusion-dependent beta thalassaemia. It edits blood stem cells to switch fetal haemoglobin back on.
2024 · Results published in NEJM29/30 people had no crises for at least 12 months. US price 2.2 million USD per treatment (secondary source).
Aug 2024 · KJ is bornA baby in Philadelphia (USA) with severe CPS1 deficiency: his liver cannot clear ammonia, which builds up in the blood and harms the brain. Very rare, estimated at about 1 in 200,000 to 1 in 1,300,000 babies.
Feb–Apr 2025 · Three infusionsThe team of Kiran Musunuru and Rebecca Ahrens-Nicklas built his drug in about 6 months; the FDA allowed its use one week after the application. First dose at 6–7 months of age.
15 May 2025 · NEJM paperThe paper describes the treatment and early follow-up. According to the press, he eats more normally and needs less medicine; in June 2025 he went home after 307 days in hospital (secondary source).
Dec 2025 · First stepsHe took his first steps (MIT Technology Review, Jan 2026, secondary). His doctors still say: very early days.
23 Feb 2026 · FDA draft guidanceA "plausible mechanism framework" for custom genetic drugs for ultra-rare diseases. It is a draft, not a new legal approval pathway.

Solid as science, Caution as a cure: the scissors have proven they cut true; getting them to every patient is a long road.

More: why does a case like KJ matter if it is only one person?

One case is not enough to say a drug works. But KJ's case shows a new process is feasible: read the gene, design the drug, test safety in cells and animals, get permission, all in about half a year.

For thousands of rare diseases, each affecting only a few people, no company can run a large trial. If the "made to measure" process can be repeated for many babies, that would be the real change. Those data do not exist yet.

Part 7

Lessons: how to read a "cured by gene editing" headline

There will be many more CRISPR headlines. Four questions tell you how far a story really goes.

For reading and understanding, not a substitute for medical advice.

1 · How many people?One case, a few dozen in a trial, or thousands? KJ is one case; Casgevy had 30 people with enough follow-up.
2 · Followed for how long?A few months or many years? Gene-editing drugs need long follow-up to catch late side effects.
3 · Which cells were edited?Body cells (not passed to children) or embryos, eggs, sperm (passed to the next generation, banned in many places)?
4 · Who approved it, where?A peer-reviewed paper? Which regulator allowed it? "In trials" is not "approved".
Beware "CRISPR" advertsReal gene-editing drugs are given only in hospitals, in trials or under licence. There is no "gene-fixing" pill or cosmetic.
Ask your doctor the right questionIf someone in your family has a genetic disease: "Are there gene therapy trials for this? Where? Who is eligible?"
Self-check: read one headline in 5 minutes

1. Find a "cured by gene editing" story in the news or on social media.

2. Answer the four questions above. Which ones does the story fail to answer?

3. Does it name the paper or the approving regulator? If not, treat it as unverified.

4. Rewrite the headline at the right strength, e.g. "one baby treated experimentally, early results good".

The four questions and the self-check are Mark's suggestions, based on the facts in Parts 4 and 5; body vs germline cells per MedlinePlus Genetics.
