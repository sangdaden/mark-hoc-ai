Part 1

The question

When we focus hard on one task, do we see something odd, large and obvious right in front of us? The answer: very often, no. And we hardly ever know what we missed.

Key concepts

Inattentional blindness

inattentional blindnessMack and Rock, 1998

Harder task, more misses; different colour, harder to see

load · attentional setSimons and Chabris, 1999 · Most et al., 2001

Looking straight at it and still not seeing

looking without seeingMemmert 2006 · Drew 2013 · Simons and Levin 1998

About 1 second

Children who missed the gorilla still looked at it for about 1 second on average, as long as those who saw it (Memmert, 2006, eye tracking).

12 of 20

Of the 20 radiologists who missed the gorilla, 12 looked directly at its location (Drew, Võ and Wolfe, 2013).

Person swapped, 7 of 15 noticed

A close cousin is "change blindness": a stranger asking directions was swapped behind a passing door, and only 7 of 15 pedestrians noticed (Simons and Levin, 1998).

Looking isn't seeing. Seeing needs attention to come along.

Multitasking: attention split in two

dual-task · task switchingStrayer and Johnston 2001 · Hyman 2010

Knowing doesn't help, and everyone is affected

familiarity · individual differencesSimons 2010 · Kreitz 2015 · Memmert 2006

Knowing the trick doesn't help

People who had seen the gorilla video all spotted the gorilla in a new one, but only 17% noticed other surprises, versus 29% of newcomers (Simons, 2010; the difference was not statistically significant).

Can't predict who will notice

Working memory and other cognitive abilities barely predict who sees the unexpected object (Kreitz et al., 2015).

Expertise helps only in-field

Basketball players noticed the gorilla more than novices (Memmert, 2006). Yet radiologists still missed a gorilla in a lung scan, because it isn't what they look for.
Part 4

Evidence check: how far to trust it

10 things often said about the gorilla experiment, checked against current research. Tap each line for the reasoning.

5 SOLID
3 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDAbout half of viewers miss the gorilla while counting

Simons and Chabris (1999): 44% noticed the gorilla, 65% the umbrella woman, 54% overall noticed the surprise guest. The effect has been repeated many times, in classrooms and labs, with many video versions.

SOLIDThe harder the task, the more you miss

In the original study, 64% of viewers on the easy task noticed the surprise guest, versus 45% on the hard task. Chabris et al. (2011) found the same in real life: higher attentional load, fewer people noticed the fight.

SOLIDThings that resemble what you're tracking are easier to see

The black gorilla was seen 58% of the time by viewers tracking the black team and 27% by those tracking the white team (Simons and Chabris, 1999). Most et al. (2001) repeated the pattern with on-screen shapes.

SOLIDExperts also miss the unexpected

Drew, Võ and Wolfe (2013): a gorilla 48 times the size of an average nodule was inserted in lung scans; 20 of 24 radiologists (83%) did not report it. None of 24 non-experts on the same task saw it.

SOLIDTalking on a phone while driving means more misses, even hands-free

Strayer and Johnston (2001), 48 students in a simulator: the probability of missing a signal more than doubled during conversation, handheld and hands-free alike; radio or audiobooks made no reliable difference. Strayer's group has repeated this many times since.

CAUTIONPeople who miss the gorilla were looking right at it

Memmert (2006) tracked children's eyes: those who missed it looked at the gorilla for about 1 second, as long as those who saw it. Drew et al. (2013): 12 of 20 radiologists who missed it looked straight at the spot. Same direction, but few eye-tracking studies and small samples so far.

CAUTIONChecklists stop you missing what matters

Haynes et al. (2009), 8 hospitals: post-surgical deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8% and complications from 11.0% to 7.0% after the WHO checklist. But in Ontario, 101 hospitals required checklists and almost nothing changed: 0.71% versus 0.65% (Urbach et al., 2014). Checklists work only when actually used, not ticked for show.

CAUTIONHeavy media multitaskers are worse at focusing

Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009) found heavy multitaskers filtered distractions worse. A meta-analysis by Parry and le Roux (2021) across 118 assessments found only a small link, and for performance-based tasks it largely disappeared after correction. Cause and effect are unclear.

CRITICISEDOnce you know the gorilla effect, you won't be fooled again

Simons (2010): people who knew the original video expected and saw the gorilla, but were no better at spotting other surprises (17% versus 29%, not significant). Knowing you have a blind spot doesn't remove it.

CRITICISEDOnly absent-minded, unfocused people miss things

Kreitz et al. (2015) measured many cognitive abilities: almost none predicted who would notice. Top experts miss things too (Drew, 2013). It's a general feature of attention, not a flaw of weak people.

One of the sturdiest experiments in psychology. The weak spot is the retelling, not the data. Robustness 9/10.

More: why this one stands when so many others fell

Many famous experiments fell because of small effects, small samples and flexible analysis. The gorilla is different: the effect is large enough to see with the naked eye, viewers answer yes or no, and anyone can rerun it in a class. Many independent groups have.

The part that needs caution is the exact rate. "50% miss it" is the number for one video and one task. A different video, task or audience gives a different rate. What holds is the pattern: the busier and narrower attention gets, the more we miss the unexpected.

Simons and Chabris won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2004 for this study, a prize for research that "makes people laugh, then think". In 2010 they wrote The Invisible Gorilla, extending it to other "illusions" about memory, confidence and knowledge.

Part 6

Lessons to apply

Work skill: do one thing at a time, and know in advance what you'll miss.

One-task time blocksNotifications off, chat tabs closed. In meetings, close the laptop.
Checklists for repeated workQuotes, contracts, handovers: write down the steps people tend to skip.
A second reviewer, a different taskYou check the numbers; a colleague only looks for "anything odd".
We don't just miss a lot. We don't know that we're missing it.Paraphrasing the spirit of The Invisible Gorilla (Chabris and Simons, 2010), not a verbatim quote.
