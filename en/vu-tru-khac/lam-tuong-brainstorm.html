Common Myths #8: Group brainstorming produces more ideas

"When the whole team thinks together, you get more ideas, and better ones."

The mytha group meeting produces more and better ideas than its members thinking on their own
Spread byAlex Osborn, the book Applied Imagination, 1953
Key evidenceTaylor et al. (1958), Diehl and Stroebe (1987), Mullen et al. (1991)
Quick verdictwrong on the number of ideas; groups still help with pooling and choosing

One-minute recap

Essentials

A promise from 1953Osborn wrote that people think up twice as many ideas in a group. He never measured it.
The 1958 test: the reverse4 people working alone, then pooled, produced nearly twice the ideas of 4 people meeting together.
The culprit: waiting to speakOnly one person can talk at a time. Ideas of those waiting get forgotten or dropped.
Why we still believe itGroup sessions are fun and feel productive. People in groups rate themselves higher than they performed.
Don't cancel the meetingGroups still help to pool, build on and pick ideas. Better: write alone first, pool later.

Sources: Osborn, Applied Imagination (1953), as quoted by Taylor, Berry and Block (1958) and Diehl and Stroebe (1987); Diehl and Stroebe (1987); Stroebe, Diehl and Abakoumkin (1992); Paulus et al. (1993).

Part 1

The question behind the myth

If four people sit in a room calling out ideas, do they come up with more than the same four people writing alone and pooling the results? Almost 70 years of experiments say no. The full answer is more interesting: groups fail at generating ideas, and help at something else.

→
1958–1991The testDozens of experiments: groups that meet lose to "nominal" groups (alone, then pooled).

TodayThe methodWrite alone first, pool later, choose by private vote. The meeting stays, the order changes.

Part 2

Where the myth came from

An advertising man, a good process, and a promise no one measured.

1939 · BBDO hits a rough patchCo-founder Alex Osborn looks for ways to get staff to "think up" ideas faster.
1942 · The booklet How to "Think Up"The first sketch of an idea meeting.
1953 · Applied ImaginationNames "brainstorming" and its four rules: no criticism, the wilder the better, quantity wanted, combine and improve on others' ideas.
1958 · Taylor, Berry and Block (Yale)The first test: 4 people alone, then pooled, beat 4 people meeting together.
1987–1991 · Finding out whyDiehl and Stroebe identify "waiting to speak"; a meta-analysis by Mullen and colleagues confirms groups lose on both quantity and quality.
2012 · Reaching the public"Groupthink" in The New Yorker calls brainstorming a "myth"; critics say it overstates the case. Most companies still meet the old way.

Sources: 1939 and 1942 from Regent University, Journal of Transformative Innovation, "The history of brainstorming: Alex Osborn" (secondary); the four rules from Applied Imagination (1953) via review literature; Taylor, Berry and Block, Administrative Science Quarterly 3 (1958); Diehl and Stroebe (1987); Mullen, Johnson and Salas (1991); J. Lehrer, The New Yorker, January 2012, and Scott Berkun's rebuttal (2012).

The grain of truth

A group of 4 meeting together does produce more ideas than one person alone (Taylor 1958 found that too). The mistake is the comparison: it should be 4 people working alone.

Feeling productive

The room is loud, ideas fly. It is hard to remember which ideas were yours, so you think you contributed more than you did.

Fun and bonding

A group session is nicer than facing a blank page alone. Enjoyable is easily mistaken for effective.

Sources: Taylor, Berry and Block (1958); Stroebe, Diehl and Abakoumkin, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 18 (1992); Paulus, Dzindolet, Poletes and Camacho, PSPB 19 (1993).

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, False

12 things people say about brainstorming, checked against the research. Click each line for the reasoning.

4 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDGroups that meet produce fewer ideas than the same number of people working alone, then pooled

From Taylor (1958) to the 22-experiment table of Diehl and Stroebe (1987, meeting groups lost in 18 of 22) and the meta-analysis by Mullen et al. (1991). One of the most consistently replicated results in group psychology.

SOLIDThe bigger the group, the bigger the loss

Bouchard and Hare (1970) compared groups of 5, 7 and 9: the gap to nominal groups widened. Mullen et al. (1991) also found group size increased the loss.

SOLIDThe main reason is waiting your turn to speak

Diehl and Stroebe (1987, Experiment 4; 1991) manipulated turn-taking directly and found it explained most of the loss. Nijstad et al. (2003) found long waits disrupt the flow of thinking.

SOLIDPeople in groups think they performed better than they did

Stroebe, Diehl and Abakoumkin (1992); Paulus et al. (1993): group members are more satisfied with their performance, even though those working alone have more reason to be.

CAUTION"More people produce more ideas than one person"

True, and this is the grain of truth in the myth: a group of 4 meeting together still beats one person alone (Taylor 1958). But it is the wrong comparison. The right question: with the same 4 people, which method yields more?

CAUTION"So thinking alone is always best"

Don't fix one myth with another. Working alone yields more ideas, but Rietzschel et al. (2006) found nominal groups choose no better. The hybrid process (Girotra 2010) and writing-then-passing slips (Paulus and Yang 2000) sometimes beat pure solo work, though not every study finds this.

CAUTION"Brainstorming is useless, cancel the meetings"

Sutton and Hargadon (1996) followed the design firm IDEO for over a year: its brainstorms also kept a shared memory of solutions, built skills and impressed clients. Lab studies only count ideas in a short session (12 minutes per problem in Taylor 1958); companies need more than that.

CAUTION"Brainstorm online, type ideas in, problem solved"

Typing in parallel means no one waits their turn. A 2007 meta-analysis of electronic brainstorming (usually cited as DeRosa, Smith and Hantula) found large electronic groups beat nominal groups, but for small groups working alone still won. Dennis and Valacich (1999) called it a new "illusion of productivity".

CAUTION"Banning criticism is what produces more ideas"

Osborn's first rule. Nemeth et al. (2004), in the US and France, found that telling groups "debate, even criticise" worked better than "don't criticise". The evidence is thin; treat it as an open question, not a new rule.

FALSE"Osborn proved groups produce twice as many ideas"

It was a claim in a 1953 book, based on advertising experience, with no comparative experiment. The first test (1958) found the opposite. Osborn himself later wrote that the method spread "too fast" and was often misused.

FALSE"Fewer ideas, but the group's ideas are better"

Mullen et al. (1991): meeting groups lost on quality too. Taylor (1958) also found nominal groups ahead on quality scores; once total ideas were accounted for, that edge disappeared, so real groups do not make up for it in quality.

FALSE"The more people you invite, the more ideas"

For groups talking aloud, the opposite: every extra person is one more person waiting to speak (Bouchard and Hare 1970; Mullen et al. 1991). The one notable exception is large groups typing ideas on computers.

What the myth gets right
  • A group beats one person alone
  • Hearing others' ideas can spark new ones
  • Meetings help pool, build on ideas and share ownership
What it gets wrong
  • Loses to the same people working alone, on quantity and quality
  • Bigger groups lose more
  • Feeling productive is not being productive

Wrong exactly where it is proudest, generating ideas; right where few people look, pooling and choosing. The harm is modest: wasted time and lost ideas from the quieter people.

More: why "persistent" scores 5/5

The contrary evidence dates from 1958, has been replicated dozens of times, was meta-analysed in 1991 and reached major media in 2012. Yet "let's all brainstorm" is still how countless meetings begin.

One reason is the illusion of productivity: the person who called the meeting also feels it went well. Another: group sessions have real benefits (bonding, shared understanding, shared ownership), just not the one the myth promises.

Part 6

Work lesson: write alone first, pool later

A script for a 30-minute idea meeting for 4–8 people. You need: sticky notes (or one shared document), pens, a timer.

One question, sent a day ahead"How can we halve our response time to customers?" beats "ideas for customer service".
The boss writes last, speaks lastThe most senior person's ideas easily anchor the room. When reading ideas aloud, go round the table, boss last.
Write the criteria before votingFor example: testable within 2 weeks, no extra headcount. Vote on the criteria, not on who spoke best.
Online: use a shared documentEveryone types in their own column for 8 minutes, mics off. Typing in parallel means nobody waits their turn.
Invitation template (copy and use)

"Thursday 10:00, 30 minutes. Question: [one sentence]. For the first 8 minutes everyone writes alone, no discussion; then we pool and choose. If you have ideas already, jot them down and bring them."

Afterwards: send a photo of the sticky-note wall, the 1–3 chosen ideas, the owner and the deadline. Keep the ideas that weren't chosen in a shared list.

Groups are bad at thinking at the same time. Groups are good at listening, building on ideas and choosing together. Give each job its own slot.Mark, summarising the studies in Part 3.
