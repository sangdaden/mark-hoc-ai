Essence

About 6 levelsHear a tone and name its pitch: people start confusing them beyond about 6 levels.
About 7 items backHear a list and repeat it: young adults hold about 7 digits.
Counted in chunksThe limit is in chunks, not in amount of information. Group things and you hold more.
Really closer to 4Cowan (2001): with rehearsal and chunking blocked, the limit is about 3 to 5 chunks.
Not a "7-item rule"The paper never says menus or slides need 7 items at most. Miller even called the sevens a coincidence.

Part 1

The paper's question

How much can a head hold at once? Miller saw the number seven turning up everywhere, then pointed to something more important than the number: we count in chunks, and whoever knows how to chunk remembers more.

The question is Mark's summary of the paper's opening and closing.

Part 2Immediate memory spanRepeating a list: about 7 items, nearly regardless of how much each item carries.

Part 2

The story of the discovery

An American psychologist, the heyday of information theory, and a number that would not let go.

My problem is that I have been persecuted by an integer.Opening line of the paper, Miller (1956). He writes that the number had followed him for seven years, intruding into his own data and the pages of leading journals.
1948 onward · Information theoryThe idea of measuring information in bits and of "channel capacity" starts to enter psychology. Miller uses this frame to look at a person as a communication channel.
15/4/1955 · PhiladelphiaMiller gives an invited address to the Eastern Psychological Association.
3/1956 · Psychological ReviewThe talk is printed as a paper, volume 63, pages 81–97.
11/9/1956 · A symposium at MITMiller later wrote (2003) that he dated the birth of cognitive science to this day, the second day of a symposium on information theory.
1991 · A medalMiller receives the US National Medal of Science for his work on how the mind operates.
2012 · DeathMiller dies on 22/7/2012, aged 92, in New Jersey.

Source: the footnote printed in Miller (1956) on the 15/4/1955 address (checked via excerpts of the PDF); Miller (2003), Trends in Cognitive Sciences, via PhilPapers and Lateral magazine (secondary sources); Princeton University news, 26/7/2012 (1991 medal, date of death).

Two different limits

Absolute judgment is measured in bits. Memory span is measured in chunks. Both come out near 7, but they are not the same thing.

A witty talk

Miller writes as if telling the story of being "persecuted" by a number, and ends by doubting those very sevens.

What got forgotten

The main idea is chunking. What people remember is the number seven.

Source: Miller (1956); "two limits of different kinds" per the paper itself and Wikipedia (secondary source). The last card is Mark's view.

Part 3

What the paper says

In 3 sentences, then in pictures.

  1. When naming a stimulus along one dimension (pitch, loudness...), we can tell apart only about 6 levels.
  2. When hearing a list and repeating it, we hold about 7 items, and this limit is counted in chunks, not in amount of information.
  3. So grouping information into bigger chunks (recoding) is the strongest way past the limit.

The three sentences are Mark's summary of Miller (1956).

More: why Miller doubted the number seven himself

The paper's ending runs through the sevens: seven wonders, seven seas, seven deadly sins, seven notes, seven days. Then Miller writes that he suspects it is only "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence".

In other words, the author himself did not claim seven was the brain's capacity. Cowan (2001) reads it the same way: the seven was a rough estimate and a rhetorical device.

Source: Miller (1956), closing section, quoted via secondary pages (uxdesign.cc and others); Cowan (2001), abstract.

Part 4

How this paper has been judged

Where it appeared, citations, replications, criticism. Each line has a source.

Where, whenPsychological Review, volume 63, issue 2, pages 81–97, March 1956. Originally an address given on 15/4/1955.
CitationsNot retrieved. Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex were blocked from the writing environment (checked 15/10/2026). Wikipedia calls it one of the most highly cited papers in psychology (secondary source). No number is given.
Replicated?Partly. A digit span of about 7 is still measured. But with rehearsal and chunking blocked, the limit drops to 3–5 chunks (Cowan 2001); lists with no structure hold only about 3 items (Chekaf, Cowan and Mathy 2016).
Main criticismCowan (2001, 2015): seven is rhetorical, the real limit is near 4. Baddeley (1994) asked "still magic?" in Psychological Review itself. Ma, Husain and Bays (2014): there may be no fixed "slots" at all, but a flexibly shared resource.
StandingOne of the best-known papers in psychology (Cowan 2015). Miller received the US National Medal of Science in 1991 for his work on the mind.
VerdictCAUTION Chunking and the existence of a short-term memory limit: solid. Seven as "brain capacity": not solid, closer to 4.

Part 5

Fact-check: what holds up and what doesn't

14 things often said about "the number seven". Tap a row for the reason.

6 SOLID
4 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDPublished in Psychological Review in 1956, from a 1955 talk

The paper's footnote: an invited address to the Eastern Psychological Association, Philadelphia, 15/4/1955.

SOLIDWe can tell apart only about 6 levels of pitch

Miller summarises Pollack's experiment: about 2.5 bits, roughly 6 levels, before confusion begins.

SOLIDYoung adults can repeat about 7 digits

Miller (1956) on immediate memory span. This is a digit-span figure, measured when listeners are free to rehearse and chunk.

SOLIDChunking lets you hold more

Miller (1956); Chase and Simon (1973): strong chess players recall real positions far better, but their edge shrinks a lot for random positions; Chekaf et al. (2016): span grows when a list has structure.

SOLIDWithout chunking, we hold about 3 to 5 chunks

Cowan (2001) brings many kinds of data together and concludes the central limit averages about 4 chunks.

SOLIDVisual short-term memory holds about 4 objects

Luck and Vogel (Nature, 1997): about 4 colours or orientations; each object can carry several features.

CAUTIONSmith recalled about 40 binary digits by recoding

One self-trained person, known only through Miller's account; the figure 40 has not been checked against the original (via Harnad's discussion list, secondary source).

CAUTIONSpan differs by material: digits more than letters, letters more than words

Summaries give about 7 for digits, 6 for letters, 5 for words (secondary sources). The direction is plausible; the exact numbers are unchecked.

CAUTIONWorking memory has a fixed number of "slots"

Ma, Husain and Bays (2014) argue it may be a resource shared flexibly among all items, with quality dropping as items increase. The "slots or resources" debate continues.

CAUTIONDigit span has stayed the same for decades

Gignac (2015) found no trend across 10 normative samples, 1923–2008; Woodley of Menie and Fernandes (2015) re-analysed and found one; Wongupparaj et al. (2017): forward span rose slightly, backward span fell slightly.

FALSE"Miller proved that seven is the brain's capacity"

Miller himself called the sevens "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence" (quoted via secondary sources). Cowan (2001): the seven was a rough estimate and a rhetorical device.

FALSE"Short-term memory holds exactly 7 things, plus or minus 2"

With rehearsal and chunking blocked, the limit is about 4 (Cowan 2001). "7 plus or minus 2" describes digit span under easy conditions, not a number of slots in the head.

FALSE"Phone numbers have 7 digits because of Miller's paper"

New York used 2 letters plus 5 digits (7 in all) from 1930; the 1947 North American numbering plan standardised that format, before the paper (Wikipedia, secondary source). No record shows the phone company chose it because of Miller.

FALSE"Menus and slides should have at most 7 items, per Miller's law"

Tufte (2003): the paper neither states nor implies any rule for how much to show in a presentation. What is in front of your eyes need not be remembered; Miller's limit is about memory.

Source: as given in each row; full list in Part 10.

Part 6

What the latest research says

From "7" to "4", then to whether there are slots at all.

1994 · "Still magic?"Baddeley revisits the seven in Psychological Review itself.
1997 · Vision is about 4 tooLuck and Vogel (Nature): about 4 colours or orientations in visual short-term memory.
2001 · The number 4Cowan (Behavioral and Brain Sciences): a central limit of 3–5 chunks, averaging about 4.
2014 · Slots or resources?Ma, Husain and Bays (Nature Neuroscience): perhaps no fixed slots, but a flexibly shared resource.
2015 · Miller in retrospectCowan (Psychological Review): the 1956 paper was followed by about 40 years with little research on the numerical limit.
2016 · Structure raises spanChekaf, Cowan and Mathy (Cognition): lists with no structure hold about 3 items; the more structure to chunk, the bigger the span.
2017 · Across decadesWongupparaj et al. pooled data from 1973–2016: forward span rose slightly, backward span fell slightly.
2018 · A set of benchmarksOberauer et al. (Psychological Bulletin) list the robust short-term memory findings any theory must explain.

Miller was right about what matters: we count in chunks. The seven is just a pretty label, and he did not believe it himself.

More: why the "rule of 7" won't die

A number, a scientist's name, and advice you can use at once. Those three put "Miller's law" into design books, presentation courses and skills workshops.

The accurate version is less tidy: the limit is near 4 without tricks, and much larger once you chunk. What is worth teaching is how to chunk, not the number.

"More" is Mark's commentary, based on the sources in Parts 4 and 5.

Part 8

Apply it: break information into small chunks

Doable from tomorrow: vocabulary, numbers, note-taking, exam revision.

Groups of 3–4Twelve new words? Split them into 3 themed groups of 4 and learn one group at a time.
Hook it to what you knowTie numbers to years, birthdays, familiar prices. Existing meaning turns many items into one.
Name your chunksTake notes as 3–4 big ideas, each with a short name. Remember the names first, details second.
Write it, don't carry itOn a hard problem, put the steps on paper. Short-term memory is small; don't make it hold and think at once.
Self-check: your memory span, 5 steps

1. Have someone read you 8 random digits, one per second. Repeat them. How many did you get?

2. Try a new list, but this time group them in 3s and 4s in your head. How many now?

3. Try again while saying "la la la" as you listen, so you can't rehearse. How far does it drop?

4. Write today's lesson as exactly 4 named big ideas. Tomorrow, try to recall the 4 names.

5. Compare steps 1 and 2: the gap is the power of chunking.

Step 3 mimics how researchers block rehearsal when measuring the "about 4" limit (Cowan 2001). This is a self-test, not a standard measurement.
