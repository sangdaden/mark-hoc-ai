Part 1

Core thesis

Attention is our most valuable resource. Manage it in two modes, a narrow beam to work and a wide light to think, and you get more done and become more creative.

Step 1Limited attentionWe spend much of the day on autopilot, and the mind holds only a few things.
→
Step 2HyperfocusSet an intention, clear distractions, put all attention on one important task.
→
Step 3ScatterfocusDeliberately let the mind wander to rest, plan and connect ideas.

Part 4

How solid is the evidence

11 figures and studies the book leans on, checked against the sources. Tap a row to see why.

4 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDAt a computer we switch screens after about 40 seconds

Gloria Mark and colleagues measured this directly over many years; later rounds found about 40–47 seconds. It is time spent on one screen, not necessarily "losing focus".

SOLIDSpecific intentions ("when, where, what") help you follow through

A meta-analysis of 94 studies (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006) found medium-to-large effects. The book's 62% versus 22% comes from one small study.

SOLIDWorking memory holds about 4 items

A common estimate in cognitive psychology (Cowan, 2001), replacing the older "7 ± 2".

SOLIDSwitching between tasks has a cost

Switch costs have been measured repeatedly in the lab (Monsell, 2003). Each switch costs a little time and raises errors.

CAUTIONAttention residue hurts the next task

Sophie Leroy's (2009) idea is plausible, but there are few studies and samples are small.

CAUTIONOur minds wander 47% of the time, and it makes us less happy

Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010) sampled over 2,000 people via a phone app. The result is correlational, and app users aren't representative of everyone.

CAUTIONA few weeks of meditation raise working memory and GRE scores by 16%

Mrazek and colleagues (2013) studied only 48 students. Meta-analyses on meditation find real but small benefits for attention.

CAUTIONSimple tasks while wandering produce the most ideas

Incubation effects are real according to a meta-analysis (Sio and Ormerod, 2009). The claim that undemanding tasks work best rests mainly on one small study (Baird and colleagues, 2012).

CAUTIONThe brain takes in 11 million bits a second, consciousness handles 40

A rough 1970s estimate Timothy Wilson used as an illustration, not a measurement. Read it as "a lot versus very little".

CRITICISEDHappy people are 31% more productive

Popularised by Shawn Achor but not traceable to a clearly published study. The general direction (positive mood helps work) has support; the number doesn't.

CRITICISEDTime in nature makes creative problem-solving 50% better

Atchley and colleagues (2012) had only 56 people, no random assignment, and hikers also left their phones at home, so nature's effect can't be separated out.

Strengths
  • Concrete tips you can use the same day
  • The scatterfocus half is a rare angle
  • The author tests ideas on himself before recommending them
Weaknesses
  • Repetitive; many passages could be shorter
  • If you've read Deep Work, the first half feels familiar
  • Many neat numbers from small studies

Read it as a practical handbook. Keep the methods, not the numbers.

More: compared with Deep Work, and the book's limits

The first half is close to Cal Newport's Deep Work (2016): deep work, blocking distractions, scheduling focus. The difference is that Bailey works at the level of each hour and each notification, which makes it easier to apply for office workers.

The second half is the most worthwhile part. Most productivity books treat mind wandering as the enemy; Bailey argues it is a different mode of work, for resting, planning and connecting ideas, as long as we choose it on purpose.

The book leans heavily on the author's own experience and on single psychology studies. Many numbers are quoted to make a point and haven't been tested by replication. Readers should try the methods on themselves rather than trust the figures.
