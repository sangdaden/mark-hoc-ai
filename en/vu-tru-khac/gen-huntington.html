Part 2

The story

Who, where, when: from a young American doctor in 1872 to a laboratory in 1993.

1872 · A short paperGeorge Huntington, a young doctor raised on Long Island (USA), described a hereditary "chorea" in families he had seen as a boy on his father's rounds. The disease has carried his name ever since.
1950s · By Lake MaracaiboVenezuelan doctor Américo Negrette noticed many people with chorea in the fishing villages along Lake Maracaibo and recognised it as Huntington's disease.
1968 · A diagnosisLeonore Wexler was diagnosed. Her father and three brothers had had the disease. Her husband, psychoanalyst Milton Wexler, started a research fund (today the Hereditary Disease Foundation). Their daughter Nancy, 23, learned she had a 50% risk.
1979 · The first tripNancy Wexler, then a psychologist, led a team to Venezuela. They returned every year: examining people, drawing blood, recording every name, linking every branch of the family.
1983 · A marker on chromosome 4James Gusella's team (Boston) used blood from an American family and the large Venezuelan family to find a DNA marker, G8, that travels with the disease. It was the first genetic disease mapped this way.
26 March 1993 · The geneA collaboration of many labs reported in Cell: the IT15 gene (now HTT) carries an abnormally long CAG repeat in all 75 affected families studied. Ten years from marker to gene.
2004 · The largest family treeA PNAS paper: 18,149 people over 10 generations, 15,409 of them living. One kindred alone had 14,761 people.
2020 · The researcher is also a patientAt 74, Nancy Wexler said publicly that she has the disease she spent her life studying.

Source: Huntington (1872), "On Chorea", Medical and Surgical Reporter (via the biography in PMC6394661); Gusella et al. (1983), Nature 306: 234–238; Cell 72: 971–983 (1993); Wexler et al. (2004), PNAS 101: 3498–3503; Columbia CUIMC press release 2004 (1979). Secondary sources: Mail & Guardian 2007 and HDBuzz (1968, Nancy's age, the fund); Nancy Wexler interview on the Lasker Foundation site (relatives with the disease); New York Times, Denise Grady, 10 March 2020 (2020 disclosure); a review article on Negrette (1950s).

Context: why Venezuela?

In the villages along Lake Maracaibo, many generations lived close together and families were large, so one disease allele passed through a great many relatives. For geneticists it was a rare "natural laboratory". For the people there it was a grief stretching over generations.

The team collected more than 4,000 blood samples (secondary source). Those samples later helped find genes for other diseases too.

Source: Wexler et al. (2004), PNAS (83 kindreds, family size); Franklin Institute page on Nancy S. Wexler and New York Genome Center (more than 4,000 samples, secondary source).

Part 3

How the gene works, in 3 sentences

1. Everyone has two copies of HTT. Inside it, the three letters C-A-G repeat many times; when the run is too long (from 36, more surely from 40), the huntingtin protein comes out faulty and brain cells slowly fail.

2. One faulty copy is enough (dominant inheritance), so each child of a carrier has a 50% chance of receiving it, sons and daughters alike.

3. The repeat does not sit still: it can grow when passed to a child (especially through the father), and it grows inside brain cells over the years.

More: why can the disease come earlier in the next generation?

Long repeats, especially when passed through sperm, tend to grow. That is why most very early cases (before about age 20, often more than 60 repeats) inherit the gene from the father. Earlier onset in later generations is called "anticipation".

The disease is not only about movement. GeneReviews describes Huntington's as a progressive disorder of movement, thinking and mental health: trouble concentrating and planning, depression or irritability can come before abnormal movements.

Source: reviews of early-onset Huntington's (PMC1801258; PMC9291924); GeneReviews "Huntington Disease".

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

14 things often said about Huntington's, checked against the original sources. Tap a row to see why.

6 SOLID
3 CAUTION
5 FALSE
SOLIDHuntington's is caused by an overlong CAG repeat in the HTT gene on chromosome 4

A longer-than-normal repeat was present in all 75 affected families in the 1993 paper (Cell 72: 971–983); confirmed by GeneReviews.

SOLIDEach child of a carrier has a 50% chance of inheriting it

Autosomal dominant; each birth is independent (GeneReviews).

SOLIDThe Lake Maracaibo family tree covers more than 18,000 people

18,149 people, 10 generations, 15,409 living, 83 kindreds (Wexler et al. 2004, PNAS).

SOLIDVery early cases usually inherit the gene from the father

The repeat grows more easily through sperm (review PMC1801258). This does not make inheritance from the mother safe: the risk is still 50%.

SOLIDNancy Wexler, who led the gene hunt, also has the disease

She went public in 2020, at 74 (New York Times, 10 March 2020; secondary source).

SOLIDInternational guidelines: predictive testing from age 18, with counselling before and after

Consistent from the IHA/WFN guidelines of 1994 to MacLeod et al. (2013), Clinical Genetics.

CAUTION"Huntington's is very rare everywhere"

5.70 versus 0.40 per 100,000 depending on region (Pringsheim 2012). A 2016 study found about 1 in 400 people in the general population have 36 or more CAG repeats, but mostly 36–38, which rarely leads to disease (Kay et al. 2016, Neurology).

CAUTION"A gene therapy now slows Huntington's"

AMT-130: the company reported 75% slowing after 36 months in 12 high-dose patients, compared with an external control group, not a randomised placebo trial (press release, 24 September 2025). The application awaits the FDA; not approved (checked 16 October 2026).

CAUTION"The faulty protein poisons brain cells for the patient's whole life"

A 2025 study of more than 500,000 cells found the CAG repeat keeps growing inside neurons; cells only fail quickly once they pass about 150 repeats. Most of the time the gene is "a ticking clock" (Handsaker et al. 2025, Cell 188: 623–639).

FALSE"Knowing your CAG count tells you the year you will get sick"

The CAG count is the strongest factor, but people with the same count still vary widely in age of onset; other genes and environment play a part (Wexler et al. 2004).

FALSE"The disease skips a generation"

A dominant disease does not skip. It only looks that way when a parent died early of something else, carried 36–39 repeats without symptoms, or carried an intermediate repeat that grew in the child (GeneReviews; ARUP).

FALSE"Huntington's is just a movement disorder"

It also affects thinking and mental health, sometimes before abnormal movements appear (GeneReviews).

FALSE"27–35 CAG repeats means you will get the disease"

Intermediate range: the carrier is not at risk, but the repeat may grow when passed to a child (ARUP; GeneReviews).

FALSE"Most people at risk get tested"

In the UK, 1993–2014, only about 17.4% of people at 50% risk had a predictive test; more than 80% chose not to know yet (Baig et al. 2016).

Part 5

Where things stand today

The gene was found in 1993, but as of October 2026 no drug has been approved to slow the disease. Current drugs only ease symptoms.

March 2021 · A "huntingtin-lowering" drug stopsThe phase 3 trial of tominersen stopped dosing on the advice of an independent monitoring board: benefits did not outweigh risks.
January 2025 · A DNA clock inside cellsA Broad Institute team measured cell by cell: the CAG repeat keeps growing in neurons, and cells fail once they pass about 150 repeats. A new direction: slow the growth of the repeat.
September 2025 · Gene therapy AMT-130A one-time injection into the brain. The company reported: the high dose slowed progression by 75% after 36 months, compared with an external control group, in 12 people. No placebo group, not yet peer reviewed.
July 2026 · Roche ends tominersenThe phase 2 trial (GENERATION HD2) lowered the faulty protein but showed no clear clinical benefit after 16 months.
Q3 2026 · Waiting for the FDAThe FDA agreed that an accelerated-approval filing for AMT-130 is reasonable; the press reports it has been filed. Four-year data (September 2026) missed one statistical test. No decision yet.

One family turned fear into a map: finding the gene is done, curing the disease is still on the road.

More: why "Solid" overall but "Caution" on drugs?

The gene part has been confirmed in thousands of families across many countries over 30 years. The drug part has two failed attempts (tominersen) and one promising approach that so far rests on a few dozen people and an external control group (AMT-130). The two parts do not share the same certainty.

Part 7

Lessons: learn to ask before you want to know

For reading and understanding, not a substitute for medical advice. These are habits of understanding, not treatment advice.

Ask about genetic counsellingIf someone in the family is affected, the first question for a doctor is: where can I get genetic counselling before and after testing?
Ask which range your number falls in"What is my repeat count, which range is it in, and what does it NOT tell me?" (for example, it cannot tell the year of onset).
Respect those not ready to knowOne person's result can reveal the risk of siblings and parents. Talk as a family before testing.
Read new-drug news with three questionsCompared with whom (placebo or outside data)? How many people? Company press release or peer-reviewed paper?
Self-check: read a "breakthrough" story in 5 minutes

1. Find a news story about a new drug for any disease.

2. Underline: number of participants, comparison group, follow-up time.

3. Where does it come from: a company, a hospital, or a peer-reviewed journal?

4. Has a regulator (FDA, EMA, a health ministry) approved it, or has it only been "filed"?

5. Rewrite the story as one honest sentence. Example: "In 12 people, compared with outside data, the disease progressed more slowly; not approved."

The self-check is Mark's suggestion, not a protocol from a study.
