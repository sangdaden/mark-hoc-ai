Part 1

The question of the case

Same explosion, same international expert group: why did the 1986 conclusion blame the operators while the 1992 one pointed at the design? The answer teaches us how to write incident reports: the first version is usually the easiest story to tell, not necessarily the truest.

Part 2

What happened

Unit 4 was powered down for a safety test: if the grid power failed, could the turbine, still spinning on momentum, run the cooling pumps until the backup diesels started?

1983 · Ignalina, LithuaniaFirst discovery that control rods can raise the reaction as they start to go in. The chief design engineer announced changes; none were made.
25/4/1986, 14:00 · Emergency cooling disconnectedPart of the test programme. INSAG-7 later noted this was permitted if the chief engineer authorised it.
26/4, 00:28 · Power falls to about 30 MWDuring a switch of control mode. Operators pulled out more rods to bring power back up.
01:22:30 · Equivalent of 8 rods leftPost-accident calculations: the reactivity margin was only 8 equivalent rods, below the minimum of 15.
01:23:04 · Test beginsSteam to the turbine is shut off. Coolant flow falls and the water in the core boils harder.
01:23:40 · AZ-5 pressedEmergency shutdown. Three seconds later, power is above 530 MW and surging. Two explosions, 2–3 seconds apart, destroy the core and the roof.
27/4 · Pripyat evacuatedAbout 50,000 residents moved out within 40 hours of the accident.
28/4 · The world finds outSweden and Finland announce elevated radiation. On the evening of 28 April, the USSR confirms a severe accident.

Source: INSAG-7, sections 3(1), 3(5), 4.1; INSAG-7 Annex I via the World Nuclear Association table (01:22:30, 01:23:04, 01:23:40, 01:23:43); OECD/NEA (2002) chapter I (two explosions); Chernobyl's Legacy (2006), p. 17 (Pripyat); A. Salo, IAEA Bulletin 28(3), 1986 (28 April).

Part 3

Why: five layers of cause

Physics layer: a reactor that speeds itself up

positive void coefficientINSAG-7, sections 2.1, 4.2

Design layer: a stop button that raised the reaction

positive scramINSAG-7, sections 2.2, 2.3, 6(4)

Information layer: the number no one could see

operating reactivity margin (ORM)INSAG-7, sections 4.2, 5; Annex I, section I-3.4

Operations layer: a test rewritten on the spot

ad hoc test changesINSAG-7, sections 3, 5.2.2, 6(6)

Below the programme

The test began at 200 MW, "well below that prescribed in the test procedures" of 700 MW.

Procedure changed that night

"Unapproved changes in the test procedure were deliberately made on the spot."

Margin through the floor

The minimum ORM was violated by 01:00. Running 11 hours without emergency cooling is "indicative of an absence of safety culture."

Source: INSAG-7 (1992), sections 3(1), 3(5), 5.2.2 (p. 19) and 6(6) (p. 24).

Source: INSAG-7 (1992), foreword, sections 1, 5.1, 6(2), 6(4), 6(5), 6(6); Annex I, section I-1 (quoting the official 1986 account). The INSAG-1 PDF could not be opened during research, so INSAG-1 is quoted through INSAG-7.

More: INSAG-7 did not erase human error

INSAG-7 says plainly: "As pointed out in INSAG-1, the human factor has still to be considered as a major element in causing the accident." The report did not swap one culprit for another. It showed operators doing risky things inside a system that did not show them the risk, overseen by a regulator that "did not function as an independent component in ensuring safety."

The 1987 trial came before that picture existed. Plant director Viktor Bryukhanov and deputy chief engineer Anatoly Dyatlov each received 10 years in a labour camp.

Source: INSAG-7, sections 5.6.1 and 6(6); ANS Nuclear Newswire (2 Nov 2021) on Bryukhanov; New York Times (30 Jul 1987) on Dyatlov (via a secondary source).

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

First, the most argued question: how many people did Chernobyl kill? Each number is right for its own question.

SOLIDThe RBMK was unstable at low power

INSAG-7: at the time of the accident the void and power coefficients were both positive; "the physical characteristics of the reactor made possible its unstable behaviour" (sections 2.1, 6(2)).

SOLIDPressing the emergency stop first increased the reaction

The graphite section at the rod's tip pushed water out of the bottom of the channel. INSAG-7 calls this "probably a decisive contributory factor" (sections 2.2, 6(4)).

SOLIDThe control-rod flaw was known before 1986

Found at Ignalina in 1983; the design was not changed and no compensating measures were taken (INSAG-7, sections 4.1, 6(4)).

SOLIDThe world learned from radiation measured abroad before the USSR confirmed

Sweden and Finland announced on 28 April 1986; the USSR confirmed that evening (A. Salo, IAEA Bulletin 28(3), 1986).

SOLIDChildhood thyroid cancer rose sharply

UNSCEAR: more than 6,000 cases among those exposed as children or adolescents, by 2005. These are cases, mostly treatable, not deaths.

SOLIDThe psychological impact is the largest health burden

Chernobyl Forum: the mental health impact is "the largest public health problem unleashed by the accident to date," along with a "paralysing fatalism" (Chernobyl's Legacy, pp. 37, 41).

CAUTIONThe accident was caused by operators breaking the rules

That was the 1986 conclusion. INSAG-7 revised it: "certain actions by operators that were identified in INSAG-1 as violations of rules were in fact not violations," but the human factor "has still to be considered as a major element," because the test was altered on the spot and run below the prescribed level (section 6(6)).

CAUTION"Chernobyl killed only 31 people"

31 is the early official count of immediate deaths (OECD/NEA 2002). It leaves out later fatal thyroid cancers (15 by 2002 per the Chernobyl Forum; WHO in 2005 said "at least nine children") and all long-term projections.

CAUTION"Chernobyl killed 4,000 people"

This is a projection of lifetime cancer deaths that may occur among about 600,000 most exposed people, not a count. WHO in 2006 added about 5,000 for 6.8 million people with low doses, for up to 9,000 in total (Nature, 2006). Note: the 2005 WHO release says "some 50 emergency workers who died of acute radiation syndrome," while the Chernobyl Forum and UNSCEAR give 28.

CAUTION"Chernobyl will kill more than 93,000 people"

Greenpeace's 2006 projection for Belarus, Russia and Ukraine to 2056. It uses assumptions and data very different from the UN agencies; cite it clearly as an advocacy group's estimate, next to the others.

FALSEThe reactor exploded like an atomic bomb

The first explosion was a steam explosion as overheated fuel met water; the second may have involved hydrogen, cause uncertain (OECD/NEA 2002, chapter I). Not a bomb-type nuclear explosion.

FALSEOperators illegally switched off emergency cooling

INSAG-7: disconnecting the emergency core cooling "was in fact permissible at Chernobyl if authorized by the Chief Engineer" (section 3(1)). The problem was running 11 hours like that, not that it broke a rule.

FALSEChernobyl has killed nearly a million people

The 985,000 figure (Yablokov et al., 2009) comes from statistical comparisons between regions that cannot separate radiation from poverty, illness and the collapse of the USSR. Scientist M. Balonov, in a review published by the New York Academy of Sciences itself in 2010, called it closer to "science fiction" than science (secondary source).

FALSERadiation caused a wave of birth defects

WHO (2005): no evidence of increases in congenital malformations attributable to radiation, and no decrease in fertility.

Chernobyl shows that the first investigation report can be wrong, and that a trustworthy organisation is one willing to write the second.

More: why the first version tends to blame people

Mark's reading: the first version was written fast, relying on insiders with interests of their own: the reactor's designers belonged to the same system as the people giving the account. Blaming a few individuals who were in the control room was the neatest story, and it did not require fixing dozens of reactors still running.

INSAG-7 came after a 1991 report by a Soviet state commission, with data that had not been made available in 1986.

Source: INSAG-7, foreword and Annex I. The first paragraph is Mark's inference, not an IAEA conclusion.

Part 6

Work lessons: blame people or fix the system

Use it whenever your team writes an incident report: an outage, a wrong shipment, a late project.

Incident reports get a "v2"Book a re-read a month later. Write down what is still unknown.
Critical limits must be visibleIf a number is a safety limit, put it on the screen and let the system block, don't just write it in a manual.
Don't pick a sideINSAG-7 fixed the system but kept the human factor. Ask both: who did what, and how did the system make it easy.
Every number says what it measures31 and 4,000 are both "right". Say counted or projected, which group, up to when.
It is reprehensible that such a deficiency had been known of for so long without its having been eliminated.IAEA, INSAG-7 (1992), section 5.1, on the control-rod flaw found in 1983.
