Part 1

Core thesis

Emotional intelligence can be trained like a skill, and the training starts with mindfulness: train attention, know yourself, then build good habits of mind. Doing so makes you better at work and happier in life.

Step 1Attention trainingBreath meditation so the mind becomes both calm and clear.
→
Step 2Self-knowledge and self-masteryNotice emotions as they show up in the body, then choose how to respond.
→
Step 3Useful mental habitsPractise kindness and empathy until they become reflexes.

Mindful walking

Notice your feet touching the ground. The walk from your desk to a meeting room is a practice session.

Mindful listening

One person talks for 3 minutes; the other only listens, without interrupting or planning a reply.

Looping back

Say back what you understood and ask "did I get that right?" before you answer.

The goal is to carry that calm, clear state into ordinary tasks, not only onto the meditation cushion.

Part 4

How solid is the evidence

12 scientific claims the book leans on, checked against current research. Tap a row to see why.

4 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 WRONG
SOLIDAn 8-week mindfulness course reduces anxiety and depression

A meta-analysis of 47 trials (Goyal et al., JAMA 2014) found a real but small-to-moderate effect, comparable to other interventions, not a "miracle cure".

SOLIDNaming an emotion softens it

Lieberman et al. (2007) found that labelling emotions reduced amygdala activity; later "affect labeling" studies point the same way.

SOLIDLoving-kindness meditation increases positive emotions

A meta-analysis (Zeng et al., 2015) found a moderate effect on positive emotions. Individual studies are small but results are fairly consistent.

SOLIDBeing kind to others makes you happier

The "spending money on others" experiments (Dunn et al., 2008) have replicated across many countries, though the effect is small.

CAUTION8 weeks of meditation at a biotech firm changed brain and immunity

Davidson, Kabat-Zinn et al. (2003) had only 41 participants; the "left frontal asymmetry" they measured was later doubted as a reliable marker of wellbeing.

CAUTIONWriting about emotions improves health and mood

Pennebaker's line of research is real, but a meta-analysis (Frattaroli, 2006) found a very small effect.

CAUTIONA few weeks of compassion training make people more generous

Weng et al. (2013) had striking results but a small sample (about 40 people), measured with a money game in the lab.

CAUTIONOptimists sell more insurance

Seligman's MetLife study (1980s) is widely cited, but its design is old and its published data limited. The idea of "changing your self-talk" does have support from cognitive therapy.

CAUTIONGoogle's SIY participants improved markedly

The results are mostly self-reports, with no control group, and people who sign up voluntarily are already motivated.

CRITICISEDEQ matters more for success than IQ

A claim popularised by Goleman (one of the foreword writers). Meta-analyses show general intelligence remains the best predictor of job performance; EQ adds only a little (Joseph and Newman, 2010).

CRITICISEDA few weeks of meditation change brain structure

Early studies (such as Lazar 2005, Hölzel 2011) were small. A larger trial (Kral et al., 2022, over 200 people) found no structural change after an 8-week course.

CRITICISED"Level 5 leaders" turn good companies into great ones

Jim Collins picked successful companies and looked backwards (survivorship bias); many of the companies in his book later declined. Humility is still a good trait, but there is no such causal evidence.

  • Connects mindfulness to concrete work skills
Weaknesses
  • Cherry-picks flattering studies
  • Lots of jokes, sometimes diluted
  • Says little about meditation's downsides

A good introductory course in book form. Do the exercises, don't believe every promise.

More: the critique of "corporate mindfulness"

The book is a flagship of the wave that brought mindfulness into business. Researcher Ronald Purser (McMindfulness, 2019) criticises this wave for stripping meditation of its ethical roots and pushing the burden of stress onto individuals, when the cause often lies in how work is organised.

Meditation is not harmless for everyone. Some people have unpleasant experiences such as heightened anxiety or resurfacing memories (Britton et al.). People with serious mental health problems should practise with a qualified guide.

On the other hand, the book's exercises are modest and safe: a few minutes of breathing, listening more carefully, pausing before reacting. That is where its real value lies, not in its promises about the brain or world peace.
