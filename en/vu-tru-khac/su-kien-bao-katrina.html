Part 1

The question

A year earlier, officials spent eight days practising for exactly this kind of storm. So why did Katrina still become a catastrophe? Not because nobody saw it coming. Because after the rehearsal, the gaps it exposed were not closed.

Source: Senate hearing record of 24 Jan 2006 and GovExec Jan 2006 (secondary sources); House report "A Failure of Initiative" (15 Feb 2006). The answer is Mark's view.

Part 2

Timeline

From the exercise to the new law: seven years, but the deciding part fits in three days.

Assumed scenario: storm surge over the levees, damage across 13 parishes, more than 1 million people evacuating, 500,000 to 600,000 buildings destroyed, more than 60,000 deaths, pumps out of action for up to 6 months.
Dec 2004 · First draftPam's draft report is finished. Follow-up sessions are postponed.
Jul 2005 · Follow-up, a year lateFunding was short. A month before Katrina, Pam planners put New Orleans' evacuation plan at about 10% complete.

Part 3

Key decisions

Four decisions, seen through the eyes of the time: who decided, what they knew, what they chose, and what happened next.

1 · Follow up the exercise now, or later?

2004–2005 · FEMA and the State of Louisiana

What they knew

Pam had just exposed many gaps: evacuation, shelter, rescue in a flooded city. Money was tight; the exercise itself had been scaled back after FEMA cut funding.

What they chose

The follow-up session was postponed and not rescheduled until July 2005. "Money was not available to do the follow-up," the FEMA director said.

What followed

"No additional planning documents were generated before they were so urgently needed" (the Senate committee chair). The evacuation plan was put at about 10%.

Source: GovExec Jan 2006 on the Senate hearing of 24 Jan 2006 (Susan Collins); CBS News 2005 (Michael Brown); Fox News/AP 2005 (10%) (secondary sources).

To be fair to the decision makers: one draft and a few workshops a year is normal for a public agency. The cost only showed when the real storm arrived before the second session could finish its work.

2 · When to order a mandatory evacuation, and where do people without cars go?

27–28 Aug 2005 · Mayor of New Orleans

What they knew

Saturday 27 August: forecasts aimed at southeast Louisiana. Sunday morning 28 August: the storm was Category 5 in the Gulf. The city had never issued a mandatory order.

What they chose

Voluntary evacuation calls first; on the morning of 28 August, after a statewide conference call, the first mandatory order in the city's history. The Superdome opened as the refuge of last resort, with buses taking people there.

What followed

About 1.3 million people in the region left before the storm. About 100,000 New Orleanians stayed (estimate). The House report found the evacuation was not announced promptly.

Source: Mayor C. Ray Nagin's letter to the Senate Homeland Security Committee, 1 Feb 2006 (read via excerpts); Esri conference paper 2007 citing officials (1.3 million) and Rich Campanella (100,000) (secondary sources, estimates); House report 15 Feb 2006 via NBC News (secondary source).

3 · Reports of water in the streets: wait for confirmation, or act on the worst case?

29 Aug 2005 · federal, state and city operations centers

What they knew

The storm had come ashore at 6:10 a.m. as a Category 3, weaker than the day before. Reports of rising water in neighborhoods arrived piecemeal; no one had the full picture.

What they chose

The House report: the federal government's biggest failure was not recognizing the likely consequences as the storm approached; information gaps ran through the whole response.

What followed

"Passivity did the most damage," the report concluded. Failures existed at every level of government, not one.

Source: House report "A Failure of Initiative" (15 Feb 2006) via NBC News, NY Sun, NPR (secondary sources); NHC TCR (landfall time).

4 · Rescue now, or wait for full coordination?

29 Aug onward · US Coast Guard

What they knew

Tens of thousands stranded on roofs and in flooded homes. Communications and unified command were in disarray.

What they chose

The Coast Guard went straight in with helicopters and boats, without waiting for a complete coordination picture (Mark's summary).

What followed

Of about 60,000 people stranded, more than 33,500 were saved by the Coast Guard: 24,135 rescued from peril, 9,409 patients evacuated.

Source: GAO-06-903 (Jul 2006), read via excerpts on gao.gov; Coast Guard Katrina history index (24,135 and 9,409). "What they chose" is Mark's summary.

Part 4

What the world learned

What actually changed after Katrina: law, organization, infrastructure, and even how deaths are counted.

A law about organizationFEMA became a distinct entity within the Department of Homeland Security; the Secretary may not substantially reduce FEMA's functions without new legislation; the 10 regional offices were written into law.
People easily left outThe 2006 law created a Surge Capacity Force, a Disability Coordinator and a National Emergency Family Registry to reunite separated families.
Infrastructure rebuiltAbout 350 miles of levees and floodwalls, 78 pump stations, a 1.8-mile surge barrier at Lake Borgne (construction began May 2009).
Counting properlyNearly 18 years later, the NHC separated direct deaths (520), indirect deaths (565) and deaths of undetermined cause (307).

Source: GAO-09-59R, CRS RL33729, FEMA course IS-822 (2006 law); US Army Corps of Engineers Senate testimony and Facts & Figures Aug 2012 (350 miles, 78 pump stations, IHNC barrier); CNN citing NOAA (520, 565, 307; secondary source).

Part 5

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

14 things often said about Katrina, checked against investigation reports and official figures. Tap each row for the reasoning.

4 SOLID
5 CAUTION
5 FALSE
SOLIDThe US Coast Guard rescued more than 33,500 people

GAO-06-903 (Jul 2006): of about 60,000 people stranded, more than 33,500 were saved by the Coast Guard. Coast Guard index: 24,135 rescued from peril, 9,409 patients evacuated; the Senate record gives 33,544.

SOLIDAbout 80% of New Orleans flooded

A widely used figure (for example Waterways Journal, secondary source). Mark could not open the original document in this session, but found no source contradicting it.

SOLIDKatrina was the costliest hurricane in US history

The NHC calls Katrina the costliest and one of the five deadliest hurricanes ever to strike the US. Damage $125 billion per NOAA; about $190 billion adjusted for inflation (NCEI via The Advocate, secondary source).

SOLIDAfter Katrina, the flood defenses were rebuilt

About $14.5 billion (AP 2022, secondary source; Corps Aug 2012: $14.45 billion). Since 2011 it can withstand a storm with a 1% chance in any year. Note: a 1%-a-year standard does not mean protection from every storm.

CAUTION"More than 1,800 people died"

That is the old figure (about 1,833). The NHC update of 4 Jan 2023: 1,392, including 307 of undetermined cause. Sources still disagree on Louisiana's own figure, so this piece does not use one.

CAUTION"The Pam exercise was shelved and none of its lessons were used"

The follow-up was delayed to July 2005 for lack of money and the evacuation plan was put at about 10% (GovExec, Fox News/AP; secondary sources). But state and local officials testified to the Senate that some ideas from Pam were used and reduced the damage. "Half done" is closer than "shelved".

CAUTION"Officials never ordered an evacuation"

They did: on the morning of 28 August, the first citywide mandatory evacuation in the city's history (Mayor Nagin's letter, 1 Feb 2006). But the House report found the order was not announced promptly, and people without cars had no way out.

CAUTION"Almost everyone left"

About 1.3 million people in the region evacuated before the storm, but an estimated 100,000 New Orleanians stayed (Esri conference paper 2007, Rich Campanella; secondary sources, both estimates).

CAUTION"The Pam exercise predicted Katrina"

Right direction: water over the levees, mass evacuation, rescue in a flooded city. Wrong details: Pam assumed more than 60,000 deaths (actual: 1,392) and had no scenario of floodwall foundations failing below design height.

FALSE"Nobody could have anticipated it"

This was said publicly after the storm. But the 2004 Pam exercise had simulated levee overtopping, mass casualties and mass evacuation. The Senate committee chair called Pam "a wake-up call that could not be ignored". What was genuinely less anticipated was the type of failure (foundations giving way below design height), not that the city could flood.

FALSE"Katrina hit New Orleans as a Category 5"

Category 5 in the Gulf on 28 August. Landfall near Buras at 6:10 a.m. on 29 August was Category 3, 125 mph winds (NHC TCR).

FALSE"The city flooded only because water went over the levees"

IPET: at the 17th Street Canal, two London Avenue Canal sites and one Industrial Canal site, floodwall foundations failed before water reached design levels. The National Research Council found the 17th Street mechanism plausible but not fully convincing. For the engineering details, see the original reports.

FALSE"The levees were deliberately blown up to save rich neighborhoods"

The engineering investigations explain the breaches by foundation failure and overtopping (IPET, reviewed by the NRC). No official investigation reported any deliberate breaching.

FALSE"It was the fault of one level of government, or one person"

The House report by a bipartisan committee: failures at every level, city, state and federal. The White House report also set out 17 lessons for the federal government itself.

Source: as given in each row; full list under Sources.

Katrina was a real storm, but a catastrophe this big was man-made: known in advance, rehearsed in advance, and not yet fixed.

More: why "nobody could have anticipated it" is easy to believe

After a disaster, "nobody could have anticipated it" is a comforting line: it turns an organizational failure into bad luck. The Katrina record shows the opposite: people anticipated it in roughly the right direction, wrote it down, and left the report waiting for funding.

That version is more useful for anyone at work. Most teams do not lack retrospectives. What they lack is the step that turns "lessons" into "tasks with a name and a date".

The "More" section is Mark's commentary, based on the sourced facts in Parts 2 to 5.

Part 7

Work lesson: after an exercise, fix what the exercise found

For any team that runs retrospectives, incident drills, pre-launch tests or "lessons learned" meetings.

Findings become tasksAfter every exercise, each finding gets an owner and a due date. "Noted" does not count as done.
Fund the follow-upWhen you ask for money to run an exercise, ask for the money and time to fix things too. If there is only half the money, shrink the exercise, not the fixing.
Rehearse wrong assumptionsList what "will definitely hold" (systems, partners, people), then run one round where exactly that fails.
Bad news first, confirm laterWrite the thresholds down: which signals get escalated immediately even if unconfirmed. Waiting for "confirmation" is a decision, not standing still.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. How many findings came out of your team's last retrospective or drill? How many have an owner and a due date?

2. Which findings are "waiting for budget" or "waiting for next quarter"? What if the real incident comes next week?

3. What does your plan quietly assume will not break? Has anyone tried the case where it does?

4. Who on the team may sound the alarm while the news is still unconfirmed? Do they know they have that right?

5. Which people or customers are most easily left out in an incident (your team's "people without cars")? Are they named in the plan?
