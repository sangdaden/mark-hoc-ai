Essence

A rubber ringThe O-ring in the right booster's joint stiffened in the cold, and hot gas burned through.
Known since 1977The joint flaw had been on file for 9 years. 7 of the 23 earlier flights had damaged O-rings.
The burden of proof flippedThe night before, engineers had to prove it was "not safe" instead of being asked "is it safe yet?"
Drift into normalEvery flight that came home safely made the damage look a little more "acceptable".
No villainPeople followed the process. It was the process itself that had drifted, without anyone noticing.

Sources: Rogers Commission report (1986), chapters 4–6; flight counts from Dalal, Fowlkes and Hoadley (1989), table 1.

Part 1

The question of the case

An organisation full of good engineers, aware of the weak point for 9 years, with engineers objecting the very night before: why did it still press the launch button? The answer lies not in one person, but in how a whole organisation slowly got used to risk.

What happened

Every time stamp below comes from chapter 3 of the Rogers Commission report.

1977 · Pressure testThe two halves of the joint moved apart instead of pressing together. Thiokol considered it insignificant.
1978–1979 · NASA engineers object in writingEngineers at Marshall called the design "unacceptable". The memos never reached Thiokol.
1982 · No backup seal any moreThe joint was put in the highest criticality class (failure means loss of the vehicle), then granted a waiver in 1983.
Jan 1985 · Flight 51-C, 53°F (12°C)The coldest launch up to then. Hot gas got past the primary O-ring.
31 Jul 1985 · Roger Boisjoly's memo"The result would be a catastrophe of the highest order — loss of human life."
27 Jan 1986, evening · The teleconferenceEngineers advised against launch. After a 30-minute private caucus, Thiokol's leadership changed its recommendation.
28 Jan 1986, 11:38 · LaunchIce covered the launch pad. There was no rule on surface temperature.

Normalization

Every safe return was taken as evidence of safety. Feynman: when playing Russian roulette, the fact that the first shot got off safely is little comfort for the next.

Culture of production

NASA planned 24 flights a year. 1986 had 15 flights on the schedule; Challenger had to borrow 45 parts from other orbiters (Rogers, chapter 8).

Structural secrecy

No one was hiding anything. Information was filtered at every level, so the top could not see how serious it was.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

13 things often said about Challenger, checked against the primary record. Click each row to see why.

4 SOLID
6 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDA failed seal in the lower joint of the right booster was the direct cause

The consensus finding of the Rogers Commission (chapter 4), backed by images of smoke at 0.678 s and flame at 58.8 s at exactly that spot.

SOLIDThe joint flaw was known years in advance

The 1977 test, the 1978–1979 memos calling the design "unacceptable", and damaged O-rings on 7 of the 23 flights whose boosters were recovered (Rogers, chapter 6).

SOLIDThiokol engineers advised against launching below 53°F

Recorded in chapter 5, along with Boisjoly's testimony: "we should not fly outside of our data base, which was 53 degrees".

SOLIDThere was schedule pressure from inside NASA

A plan of 24 flights a year, parts taken from other orbiters, squeezed training time (Rogers, chapter 8). Vaughan calls it the "culture of production".

CAUTIONThe engineers were ignored, nobody listened

They were heard for hours, and NASA's side said it would not launch over Thiokol's objection. What happened is that Thiokol's own leadership reversed after the private caucus, and the burden of proof was flipped. Engineer Bob Ebeling later said, "I could have done more".

CAUTIONA good chart would certainly have stopped the launch

Edward Tufte (1997) says yes. A 2002 rebuttal co-authored by Boisjoly himself says the engineers did not have the temperature data despite asking for it, that Tufte's temperature axis mixes O-ring temperature with air temperature, and that the core issue was the burden of proof, not the graphics. The dispute is unresolved.

CAUTIONMillions of schoolchildren watched it live

The major broadcast networks had cut away before launch; CNN carried it live, and NASA sent a satellite feed to schools for the "Teacher in Space" program. Many pupils really did watch live, but most Americans saw replays. No original survey was found for the percentages often quoted.

CAUTIONChallenger "exploded"

There was no explosion in the sense of a detonation or shock wave. The external tank failed, the vehicle was torn apart by aerodynamic forces, and the propellants burned in a fireball. "Broke apart" is more accurate.

CAUTIONThe crew were conscious until they hit the ocean

Dr Kerwin's report (July 1986): 3 of the 4 recovered emergency air packs had been activated by hand, so at least some of the crew were alive and acting after the breakup. But they "possibly, but not certainly" lost consciousness in the seconds that followed. Whether the cabin lost pressure could not be established.

CAUTIONFeynman found the cause on his own

The televised demonstration of dropping the O-ring into ice water was his. But the initial hint came from General Donald Kutyna, and after Sally Ride died (2012), Kutyna said it was she who had given him the NASA documents on O-rings stiffening in the cold. (Secondary source: Kutyna's account.)

FALSEThe crew died instantly

The crew cabin separated nearly intact, with accelerations at breakup of about 12–20 G, which are "survivable". The cabin fell for about 2 minutes 45 seconds before hitting the sea. The cause of death "cannot be positively determined" (Kerwin).

FALSEThe White House pushed for a launch in time for the State of the Union

The Commission investigated this rumor. Those involved stated there was no plan for a live hookup, and the launch decision was made by NASA officials "without any outside intervention or pressure" (Rogers, chapter 8).

FALSEAn asbestos ban ruined the joint putty

A long-running online myth. Space journalist James Oberg, who compiled the myths about Challenger, calls the putty change "unrelated to the disaster".

Done right after the disaster
  • A public report that named the decision failures plainly
  • The joint was redesigned, with a third O-ring and heaters
  • A safety office reporting directly to NASA leadership
Not done right
  • Boisjoly was isolated and taken off space work
  • The design was fixed faster than the culture
  • 17 years later, Columbia repeated the same kind of mistake

The most avoidable disaster of the US space program, because every piece of information needed was already in the meeting room the night before.

More: why the lessons were forgotten

In 2003, Columbia broke apart on re-entry because a piece of foam insulation struck its wing at launch. Foam shedding, too, had happened many times and had slowly come to be seen as normal. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board wrote that "the history of engineering decisions on foam and O-ring incidents had identical trajectories", and engineers were again asked to prove the flight was "unsafe".

Vaughan worked with the Columbia investigation board. She concluded that "from Challenger NASA learned incomplete lessons": it fixed the O-ring, but not the pressures and structure that dulled the danger signals.

Part 6

Work lessons: don't let small deviations become normal

Applies to any "go or no-go" meeting: a product launch, pushing code to production, signing a contract.

Track small incidents as a trendThe Commission found "little or no trend analysis" of O-ring erosion. Build a table; don't handle each case on its own.
Separate the engineering hat from the management hatRecord the technical recommendation and the business decision separately. Whoever changes the recommendation signs their name.
Ask for risk numbers independentlyAsk the people doing the work before you ask the boss. A big gap is a signal.
Compare with the original design, not with last timeThe erosion limit went from "no erosion allowed" to "0.090 inch of erosion is fine". Go back to the original baseline regularly.
For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled.Richard Feynman, closing line of appendix F, Rogers Commission report (1986).
