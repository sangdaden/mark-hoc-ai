Part 1

The question

The company that sold more phones than anyone, with great engineers and plenty of cash: why did it have to sell its phone business only six years after the iPhone? The familiar answer is "arrogance". The records and the research point to something more uncomfortable: leaders saw the threat, but could not hear the truth about themselves.

Source: Microsoft press release, 3 Sep 2013; Vuori & Huy (2016).

Why: four layers of causes

Emotional layer: two different fears

distributed attention · shared fearVuori & Huy, 2016

Organisational layer: bad news filtered at every level

filtered bad newsVuori & Huy, 2016

Strategic layer: an aging platform and a bet

aging platform · Windows Phone betNokia 6-K 11 Feb 2011

Incentive layer: the urgent eats the important

short-term pressureVuori & Huy, 2016

Short-term targets

Pressure to ship products and hit quarterly sales made every level prioritise the immediate (Vuori & Huy, 2016).

Fear of losing status, resources

Middle managers worried about losing status and resources if they said what bosses did not want to hear (Huy & Vuori, INSEAD Knowledge).

Overconfidence

Leaders rated Nokia's technological capabilities higher than they were, so investment in a new platform came late (Vuori & Huy, 2016).
The first iPhone shipped in 2007, and we still don't have a product that is close to their experience.Stephen Elop, memo to Nokia staff, 8 Feb 2011, full text published by Engadget (secondary source).
Part 4

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

14 things often said about Nokia, checked against Nokia's reports, Microsoft's SEC filings and peer-reviewed research. Click each line for the reasoning.

SOLIDIn 2007 Nokia led the phone market

Nokia estimated its Q4 2007 device market share at 40%, with 133.5 million units sold in the quarter (release of 24 Jan 2008, Form 6-K). Market research firms give slightly lower full-year figures (IDC, Gartner, secondary sources).

SOLIDMicrosoft paid EUR 5.44 billion, of which 3.79 billion for the device business

Microsoft press release of 3 Sep 2013 and Form 8-K: EUR 3.79 billion for Devices & Services, EUR 1.65 billion to license patents. Nokia's 2014 20-F gives a total of EUR 5,440 million.

SOLIDIn 2015 Microsoft wrote down about USD 7.6 billion and cut up to 7,800 positions

Microsoft, Form 8-K exhibit 99.1 (8 Jul 2015). Plus restructuring charges of USD 750–850 million.

SOLIDIn February 2011 Nokia chose Windows Phone as its primary platform

Nokia 6-K of 11 Feb 2011 and Form 20-F 2010. Symbian became a "franchise" platform, MeeGo a longer-term project.

SOLIDInternal fear filtered bad news on its way up

The main finding of Vuori & Huy (2016), in a leading peer-reviewed management journal. Note: it is a qualitative study based on recollections after the fact, not a measurement.

CAUTIONThe "burning platform" memo is real

The memo leaked on 8 Feb 2011. Engadget published the full text and later wrote "it's real" based on multiple sources. This piece has not seen a version published by Nokia itself, so it is treated as a secondary source.

CAUTION"Nokia's CEO cried and said: we didn't do anything wrong, but somehow, we lost"

The line is very widely shared online (for example Korea Times, Apr 2016, citing a LinkedIn post). No press release, press-conference transcript or video containing it was found. Some versions even date it wrongly to 2014.

CAUTION"Nokia had a touchscreen phone in 2004, three years before the iPhone"

The source is one former manager, Ari Hakkarainen, quoted by the New York Times (Sep 2010, secondary source). A prototype may well have existed, but one account is not enough to say "Nokia already had the iPhone".

CAUTION"Nokia lost only because of the iPhone"

Elop's own memo names Android too (overtaking Nokia in smartphone volumes in early Feb 2011) and low-cost phones. Vuori & Huy point to internal causes.

CAUTION"Elop was a Trojan horse planted by Microsoft"

This is speculation; no regulator or court has found it. What is true: Elop was a former Microsoft executive; Nokia's documents for shareholders (Sep 2013) disclosed a change-of-control payment of about EUR 18.8 million to Elop, 70% borne by Microsoft (TechCrunch, Computing, secondary sources). The payment drew criticism in Finland.

CAUTION"Microsoft killed Nokia"

Nokia Group net sales had already fallen from EUR 51.1 billion (2007) to EUR 30.2 billion (2012) before the sale. But after buying, Microsoft itself wrote off most of the deal's value (2015). Both share the story.

FALSE"Nokia was arrogant and dismissed the iPhone"

Vuori & Huy (2016): top managers feared outside competitors, Apple included. The 2011 memo admitted the gap bluntly. The problem was not failing to see, but not knowing precisely how weak they were, because bad news was filtered. There may have been public remarks downplaying the iPhone, but that is not the main explanation.

FALSE"Nokia didn't make smartphones"

Nokia sold more smartphones than anyone until early 2011, according to Elop's own memo ("this week they took our leadership position in smartphone volumes"). In Q4 2012 Nokia still sold 2.2 million Symbian phones.

FALSE"Nokia went bankrupt"

Nokia sold only its device business (closed 25 Apr 2014). The company carries on, mainly in network infrastructure (Nokia 20-F 2014). Nokia never filed for bankruptcy.

Source: Nokia 6-K of 24 Jan 2008, 6-K of 11 Feb 2011, 20-F 2010 and 2014, release of 25 Apr 2014, 2012 results; Microsoft 8-K of 3 Sep 2013 and 8 Jul 2015; Vuori & Huy (2016); Engadget 8 Feb 2011, New York Times Sep 2010, Korea Times Apr 2016, TechCrunch and Computing Sep 2013 (secondary sources).

Nokia did not lose because it could not see the future. It lost because it could not hear its own present.

More: why "transparent record" gets only 3/5

Unlike cases with an official investigator, such as Knight Capital, no one required Nokia to publish what went wrong inside. The financial reports tell us what happened, not why. The "why" in this piece rests mainly on one academic study that interviewed insiders after the fact.

Recollections after the fact can be bent by memory and self-interest. So this piece uses the study for the overall picture, not to blame any individual.

Part 6

Work lesson: let bad news travel up fast

If bad news can't reach the top, leaders are blind. For team leads, project managers, and the people who write the reports.

Time the bad newsLast incident: when did the doer know, when did the boss know? That gap is how blind your team is.
First words: "thank you"When someone brings bad news, thank them first, ask why later. Punish the messenger once and the team goes quiet for years.
Listen to the hands-on peopleOnce a month, sit with someone doing the work, skipping a layer of management. Ask: "What isn't working?"
Bosses share real worriesPushing deadlines without explaining the urgency only spreads fear. Name the threat, and the team knows what to report.
Check your own team (5 questions, 5 minutes)

1. Last month, did anyone say "this isn't OK yet" in a meeting with the boss present?

2. Does your weekly report have a real "risks" section, or does it always say "none"?

3. The last time someone brought bad news, what happened to them afterwards?

4. What does your boss believe about the product that the hands-on people disagree with?

5. If a competitor launched something far better tomorrow, who on the team would dare to say so first?

Two or more answers of "I don't know" or "no" is a sign that bad news is getting stuck.
