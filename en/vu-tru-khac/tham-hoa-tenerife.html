Essence

One runway, two 747sThick fog. The tower could not see the runway.
One ambiguous sentence"We are now at take-off": the first officer meant "taking off"; the tower heard "in position, waiting".
One "OK" and 3 seconds of squealTwo warnings went out at the same moment and blocked each other on the radio.
One question brushed asideThe flight engineer asked, "Is he not clear, that Pan American?" The captain: "Oh, yes." Fifteen seconds later, impact.
Not just one manThe report names the KLM captain, but lists six contributing factors. The Dutch did not fully agree.

Source: Spanish report (Subsecretaría de Aviación Civil, 1978), sections 1.2, 1.7, 2.1 and conclusions; FAA, "Lessons Learned", PH-BUF/N736PA.

Part 1

The question

The best-known captain, a full crew, a flight engineer who spoke up: why did the 747 still roll into the fog? Because everyone heard what they expected to hear, and in that cockpit a junior's question was not heavy enough to stop a senior.

Part 4

Fact check: truths and myths

13 things often said about Tenerife, checked against the original record. Tap a line to see why.

4 SOLID
6 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDKLM began its take-off run without a take-off clearance

Fundamental cause no. 1 in the Spanish report. At 17:05:53 the tower gave only the route clearance; "OK… stand by for take-off" came after KLM had released its brakes.

SOLIDThe KLM flight engineer asked whether Pan Am was clear of the runway

CVR, 17:06:32: "Is he not clear, that Pan American?" The captain answered emphatically that it was (Spanish report, section 2.1).

SOLIDThe KLM captain was under duty-time pressure

Dutch rules changed in December 1976; captains could no longer extend the duty limit. The report calls the uncertainty a significant psychological factor and lists it as a contributing factor.

SOLID583 dead, the deadliest accident in aviation history

248 on KLM and 335 on Pan Am (Spanish report, section 1.2). The FAA calls it the deadliest accident in aviation history.

CAUTION"It was only the KLM captain's fault"

The Spanish report names him as the fundamental cause, but lists six contributing factors: duty time, weather, blocked radio, non-standard language (including the tower's "OK"), Pan Am not leaving at the third exit, and an overloaded airport. The Dutch did not accept blame on him alone. He made the final decision, but he was not the only hole.

CAUTION"Pan Am got lost and ignored its exit"

Pan Am read back "the third", but C-3 required a turn of about 148° and then another, very hard for a 747 (ALPA, via Wikipedia). The crew was still debating which exit to count. The Spanish report still lists it as a contributing factor: they should have checked.

CAUTION"The controllers were listening to football"

A Dutch contention, based on background sound on the tower tape, worded as "may have been distracted". The official report makes no such finding.

CAUTION"CRM was born from Tenerife"

The name "Cockpit Resource Management" was coined at a NASA workshop in 1979, and the first comprehensive CRM programme was United Airlines' in 1981. Helmreich and colleagues' history of CRM (1999) cites a 1978 United crash, not Tenerife. Tenerife is the classic teaching case for CRM, but not its only origin.

CAUTION"After Tenerife, English became mandatory in aviation"

Both sides in this accident spoke English on the radio. ICAO's English proficiency requirements were adopted on 5 March 2003 and applied from 5 March 2008, 26 years later. The ICAO/IFALPA document does not mention Tenerife.

CAUTION"The extra fuel made KLM too heavy to clear Pan Am"

KLM loaded 55,500 litres and did get airborne, scraping its tail. The Spanish report classes the refuelling as an indirect factor. No official calculation was found showing a lighter KLM would have cleared Pan Am. That the fuel made the fire worse is widely reported.

FALSE"The KLM captain was an inexperienced pilot"

He had 11,700 flight hours and more than 10 years as an instructor (Spanish report, sections 1.5.1 and 1.16). That reputation was part of the problem: the report says his great prestige may have made others reluctant to object.

FALSE"The bomb caused the disaster"

The bomb started the chain by pushing flights to a small airport. But the Spanish report states clearly that it was not a direct factor.

FALSE"Nobody in the KLM cockpit spoke up"

The first officer said "wait a minute, we don't have an ATC clearance" at 17:05:41, and the flight engineer asked about Pan Am at 17:06:32. They did speak, but only with a mild reminder and a question, never a demand to stop.

Source: Spanish report (1978), sections 1.2, 1.5, 1.16, 2.1 and conclusions; FAA "Lessons Learned"; Helmreich, Merritt and Wilhelm (1999); IFALPA briefing leaflet 08ATSBL03 on ICAO language requirements; the Dutch position and the C-3 angle via Wikipedia (secondary).

Not done right
  • Two countries published two explanations, with no shared picture

A disaster of communication: everything needed to stop was said out loud, but not heard in time.

More: why "Lesson retained" is 4, not 5

The lessons on wording and teamwork were adopted worldwide. But in 2007, 30 years later, the FAA still had to launch a "Call to Action" on runway incursions, and the FAA lists Tenerife as part of the reason.

Source: FAA, "Lessons Learned", PH-BUF/N736PA. Some sources say CRM became mandatory in 2006, but Mark found no primary source for that date and does not use it.

Part 6

Work lesson: say it clearly, read it back, and dare to interrupt the boss

For any decision that travels by voice or chat: shift handovers, code-review approvals, an "OK" in the team chat.

One word, one meaningAviation now says "take-off" only to clear or cancel a take-off, and "departure" otherwise. Which words in your team, like "done", "agreed" or "ship", carry two meanings?
Close the loop: say, read back, confirmFor anything important, the receiver repeats it in their own words. The sender listens and corrects at once.
Agree on a "stop phrase" in advanceA phrase anyone may use, including the newest person. When it is said, everyone stops and checks before arguing.
Treat a question as a signalWhen a junior asks "are we sure?", the person in charge should pause and check, not answer straight away with confidence.

Source: the "take-off"/"departure" rule per SKYbrary, "Immediate Takeoff Clearances", citing ICAO Doc 9432 and Doc 4444, chapter 12; recommendations 3.2–3.3 of the Spanish report.
