Part 1

The question behind the experiment

When someone asks us for something, do we actually listen to the reason, or does hearing "because" make us nod? Langer's answer: for small favors, many people nod out of habit; for big ones, people start to listen. But the evidence for this answer is thinner than its fame.

Learning the format from the bin

The authors collected 83 interoffice memos from secretaries; 68% shared one format: a request, unsigned.

Sending a pointless memo

40 secretaries received a memo asking them to return the memo itself to "Room 238". There was no reason to do so.

Familiar format: 90% returned it

Other formats: 60% on average. Only 10 people per group, so these numbers swing a lot.

The point: what looks familiar gets handled by habit. At work, an email in the usual template is more likely to be acted on, and also more likely to be skimmed without reading.

Part 4

Evidence: how far can we trust it

10 common claims about the experiment, checked against the original paper and later checks. Tap each row to see why.

3 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDPeople agree to small favors far more readily than large ones

In the original paper this is the strongest difference (5 pages vs 20 pages, p < .001), and it fits a great deal of later research on the cost of helping.

SOLIDWhat the reason says matters, especially when the favor takes effort

Even Folkes (1985), who disputed Langer, found that the type of reason changed compliance: reasons within the asker's control got less help than reasons outside it. So listeners do weigh reasons.

SOLIDWe underestimate how often a direct request gets a yes

Not part of Langer's experiment, but the lesson that goes with it. Flynn and Lake (2008): to get strangers to fill in a questionnaire, participants guessed they would need to ask about 20 people; in fact about 10 was enough. Bohns's (2016) review, across more than 14,000 strangers: we underestimate how many will say yes by about 48%.

CAUTIONFor small favors, an empty reason works as well as a real one (93% vs 94%)

Each group had only 15–16 people: 93% is 14 of 15, 94% is 15 of 16. The direction is clear, but one or two people changing their mind would change the numbers a lot.

CAUTIONFor large favors, people wake up and only real reasons work

42% vs 24%, about 25 people per group. The original paper itself reports this comparison as only marginal (p between .05 and .10).

CAUTIONThe word "because" by itself triggers a yes-script

This is Langer's interpretation, not something measured directly. Folkes (1985) argued her results fit people thinking rather than running a script. Langer and colleagues wrote a reply standing by their view.

CAUTIONDocuments in a familiar format get followed without close reading

The office memo experiment: 90% vs 60%, only 10 secretaries per group, 40 in total. Plausible and interesting, but one small experiment.

CAUTIONMost everyday human behavior is "mindless"

The big idea Langer developed later (the book Mindfulness, 1989). Three small experiments in 1978 can suggest it, not prove a claim that broad.

CRITICISEDAdd "because" and people will agree

The popular version in sales books and "magic word" articles. It skips the second half of the experiment: with 20 pages, the empty reason got exactly 24%, the same as no reason. Gelman (2024) shows that figures like "more than 50% more likely" misread the table.

CRITICISEDThe result has been replicated many times

Mark has not found a direct replication that reproduced the original result. The most cited re-run, Folkes (1985), with 4 studies, did not reproduce it. So treat this as an idea worth trying, not a law.

Strengths
  • Run in a real setting, people did not know they were observed
  • Includes both small and large favors, so the result has nuance
  • Easy to understand, easy to try in daily life
Weaknesses
  • 15–25 people per group, one library, one moment in time
  • The key comparison for large favors is only marginal
  • The 1985 re-run did not reproduce it; few direct replications

Robustness 5/10. The experiment raises a good idea: we nod to small favors by habit and only listen for big ones. But the 93% figure is asked to carry more than 15 people can bear.

More: why such a small experiment became so famous

In 1984, Robert Cialdini retold the experiment in Influence, one of the best-selling books on persuasion. From there, the "magic because" spread into sales courses, copywriting and business articles, usually with only the first half of the result.

The story has everything it needs to spread: one simple word, a very high number (93%), and a trick anyone can try right away. The second half (20 pages, empty reason does nothing) is less catchy, so it is told less, even though it is what makes the experiment valuable.

Gelman (2024) uses this very experiment as an example of "mindlessness in the interpretation of a study on mindlessness": retellers look at the nice number, not at the sample size and the error.

Lessons to apply: ask with a reason

A work skill: asking colleagues, bosses and partners in a way that gets a yes, and staying alert when you are the one being asked.
