Part 1

The question behind the myth

If you ask someone "do you prefer to learn from pictures or words?" and then teach them that way, do they learn more? The experiments' answer: almost never. What should decide how you teach is the content, not the learner's label.

Part 2

Where the myth came from

There is no single "first person who said it". There is a chain of models, questionnaires and training courses, each step a little more confident than the last.

1978 · Rita and Kenneth DunnThe book Teaching Students Through Their Individual Learning Styles: teach each child through their own learning style.
1979 · Barbe and Swassing: three "modalities", visual, auditory, kinestheticThe book Teaching Through Modality Strengths and a test to find each person's "most efficient learning mode". The VAK model is usually credited to this group, though some sources trace it earlier.
1984 · David KolbThe book Experiential Learning and the Kolb learning style inventory, still one of the two most used.
1992 · Neil Fleming and Colleen Mills: VARKThey added "read/write" to make four types. The paper's title states its purpose: "Not Another Inventory, Rather a Catalyst for Reflection".
2004 · Coffield and colleagues count 71 models13 major, 58 minor. Many of the instruments have serious weaknesses in reliability.
2006 · Fleming himself is worriedHe wrote that "students and teachers invest more belief in VARK than it warrants" (secondary source).
2008 · Pashler and colleaguesFour cognitive psychologists review all the evidence: not enough basis to build teaching around learning styles.
2012 · Dekker and colleagues93% of UK and 96% of Dutch teachers in the survey still believe it. It is the most-believed brain myth in the study.
2015 · Newton89% of recent research papers on learning styles in higher education still endorse using them.
3/2017 · A letter from about 30 scientists in The GuardianIt calls learning styles a "neuromyth", wasteful and potentially harmful (secondary source).

Source: library catalogues (Dunn and Dunn 1978, Reston; Barbe, Swassing and Milone 1979, Zaner-Bloser); Kolb (1984), Prentice-Hall; Fleming and Mills (1992), To Improve the Academy 11; Coffield and colleagues (2004); Pashler and colleagues (2008); Dekker and colleagues (2012); Newton (2015), Frontiers in Psychology 6: 1908. Fleming's 2006 line and the 2017 letter: secondary sources (press; Centre for Educational Neuroscience, 20 March 2017).

It sounds like respecting difference

Who would object to teaching each person their own way? The claim carries a good value, so few people check it.

The feeling of ease is real

Knoll and colleagues (2017): studying "in your style" makes you believe you'll remember more, though you don't.

Quick test, catchy label

A few minutes of questions, one label. Easy to sell as a workshop, easy to put in a file.

Source: Knoll, Otani, Skeel and Van Horn (2017), British Journal of Psychology 108(3): 544–563.

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, False

10 common versions of the myth, checked against the original sources. Click each row to see why.

3 SOLID
3 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDPeople have their own preferences for taking in information

You can measure them by asking, and they shape how learning feels (Knoll and colleagues, 2017). Pashler and colleagues (2008) don't deny preferences; they show preferences don't prove a benefit from matching.

SOLIDWords plus pictures usually beat words alone

Mayer's multimedia principle (2009), for learners in general, not split by "type". This is the true part the myth borrows to sound reasonable.

SOLIDBeginners and experts need different teaching

Detailed step-by-step guidance helps novices but can slow down people who already know the material (the expertise reversal effect, Kalyuga and colleagues, Educational Psychologist, 2003). The difference here is prior knowledge, not "visual or auditory".

CAUTION"Some studies found matched teaching works"

True: the 2024 meta-analysis found a small benefit (g = 0.31) across 21 studies. But only 26% of measures showed crossover, study quality was low, and the authors themselves don't recommend wide adoption.

CAUTION"Taking the VARK quiz helps you understand yourself"

Fleming and Mills (1992) made VARK as "a catalyst for reflection", and thinking about how you learn can be useful. But using the result as a diagnosis to choose how to learn has no supporting evidence, and Fleming himself worried people believe VARK too much (secondary source).

CAUTION"Learning with more senses makes it stick"

Often true when the format fits the content (words with pictures, practice for skills). Piling on channels that don't fit can just add clutter. And if it's true, it's true for everyone, which is a reason to drop learning styles, not to support them.

FALSETeaching in someone's style makes them learn better

The core of the myth. Pashler and colleagues (2008) found virtually no evidence; Rogowsky and colleagues (2015, 2020) tested it properly with adults and fifth graders and found no difference.

FALSEEveryone belongs to one of three (or four) fixed types

Coffield and colleagues (2004) counted 71 models that sort people in different ways, many with unreliable measures. Of Husmann and O'Loughlin's 426 students (2019), most didn't study in their own style.

FALSEYou should avoid ways of learning that "aren't your style"

This is the harmful part: a self-described "auditory learner" who skips the diagram misses what the diagram holds. The 2017 letter from about 30 scientists called this approach wasteful and potentially damaging (secondary source).

FALSE"It's a finding of neuroscience"

Learning styles are on the list of "neuromyths" in Dekker and colleagues (2012), and the most believed one in that survey. Knowing more about the brain in general didn't protect teachers from it.

The true kernel
  • People do have preferences
  • Format matters, by content
  • Learners differ in prior knowledge
The false part
  • Matched teaching doesn't improve learning
  • People don't sort into 3–4 fixed types
  • Labels can make people avoid useful methods

A kind idea with one broken link: respecting learners' preferences is good, but using preferences to decide how to teach has no evidence behind it.

More: why Mark didn't give 5/5 for wrongness

The 2024 meta-analysis does find a small effect, and in 2025 papers were still debating why. Saying "learning styles are complete nonsense" goes beyond the evidence. More precisely: after more than 40 years, the matching hypothesis still hasn't passed the test it set for itself.

Harm is 3/5 because the cost is mostly money, time and opportunity: three versions of every material, split classes, and people who limit themselves with a label.

Part 6

Work lesson: design training around the content

For onboarding new hires, internal training, or teaching yourself a new skill.

Ask about the content, not the person"Does this have a shape? Is it a skill or knowledge?" instead of "What's your learning style?".
Drop the style quiz from onboardingDon't write "visual learner" in files, don't split classes by label, don't make three versions of materials.
Use preferences for motivation, not designLetting people listen to a recording on the commute is fine, as long as they still look at the diagram.
Measure with tests, not feelings"This way sinks in" is not evidence (Knoll, 2017). Test a week later to find out.
There is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning style assessments into general educational practice.Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 9(3), p. 105.
