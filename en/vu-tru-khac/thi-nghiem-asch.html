Part 1

The question

When the whole group says something plainly wrong, do we dare say what we see? Asch's answer: most of the time, yes, but group pressure is strong enough to pull about a third of answers toward the wrong one.

Part 3

Key concepts

A story told lopsided

conformity vs independenceAsch, 1955 · Griggs, 2015

Two kinds of pressure

normative · informational influenceDeutsch and Gerard, 1955

Fear of standing out

Normative pressure: you know the group is wrong but go along so as not to look odd. Drops sharply when answers are private.

Thinking the group knows better

Informational pressure: you start doubting your own eyes. Stronger when the question is hard or vague.

At work, the two blend

The boss has spoken, the room nods: you are both shy of disagreeing and thinking "they must know something I don't".

How many is enough

majority sizeAsch, 1955

The power of one ally

a single dissenter breaks unanimityAsch, 1955; 1956

Culture and era

culture · cohortBond and Smith, 1996 · Perrin and Spencer, 1980 · Mori and Arai, 2010

Collectivist countries: more conformity

133 studies in 17 countries: where group harmony is valued, conformity is higher (Bond and Smith, 1996).

In the US, falling since the 1950s

Later US studies show less conformity than in Asch's day (Bond and Smith, 1996).

Sometimes it nearly vanished

UK engineering students: 1 conforming answer in 396 trials (Perrin and Spencer, 1980). In Japan, without actors: women conformed near Asch's level, men did not (Mori and Arai, 2010).
Part 4

Evidence: how far to trust it

10 things often said about the experiment, checked against current research. Click each row for the reasoning.

5 SOLID
3 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDWhen the whole group is unanimously wrong, about 1 in 3 answers goes along

Asch (1955) reported 36.8% with 123 people. The controlled replication by Franzen and Mader (2023) with about 200 students in Bern found 33%, very close to the original.

SOLIDOne dissenting voice cuts conformity sharply

In the variant with an "ally" giving the right answer, errors fell to about a quarter (Asch, 1955). Even when the dissenter was also wrong, just in a different way, conformity fell to about 9% (Asch, 1956). Later studies found the same direction.

SOLIDFrom 1 to 3 wrong voices, pressure rises fast, then levels off

Asch (1955): almost no effect with 1 person, 13.6% with 2, 31.8% with 3, little increase beyond. Bond and Smith's (1996) meta-analysis also found larger majorities exert more pressure.

SOLIDPrivate, anonymous answers reduce conformity

When participants wrote their answer instead of saying it aloud, conformity fell clearly (Asch, 1956). Deutsch and Gerard (1955) also found anonymity reduces the "fear of standing out".

SOLIDConformity levels differ across cultures

Bond and Smith (1996) pooled 133 studies from 17 countries: collectivist countries showed more conformity than individualist ones. This is a correlation between countries, not an experiment, but it held across three different culture measures.

CAUTIONIn the US, people conform less today than in the 1950s

Bond and Smith (1996) found conformity in US studies declining since the 1950s. But the studies differ in participants and methods, so causes are hard to separate. The 2023 Swiss replication still found a rate close to Asch's.

CAUTION"The Asch effect was just a product of 1950s America"

Perrin and Spencer (1980) found only 1 conforming answer in 396 trials among UK engineering students. But that is a special group (trained in precise measurement), and other replications still find the effect. Better: conformity depends on people and context.

CAUTIONWomen conform more than men

Bond and Smith (1996) found a small difference; Mori and Arai (2010) in Japan found women went along on 3.44 of 12 trials on average, close to Asch's 4.41, while men were not swayed. Sex differences depend heavily on culture, measures and experimenter; many researchers still dispute them.

CRITICISED"Most people follow the crowd"

In Asch's data about 2 in 3 answers stayed correct, about a quarter of people never went along, and only about 5% went along every time. Griggs (2015) reviewed 20 textbooks: 14 gave the conformity rate, only 1 said most answers were independent.

CRITICISEDAsch proved people are "sheep"

Asch himself was interested in independence and struck by how many held their ground. Friend, Rafferty and Bramel (1990) called the "conformity study" retelling a misinterpretation. Hodges and Geyer (2006) argue that those who went along were sometimes weighing truthfulness against respect for others, not just afraid.

Strengths
  • Clean design, a question with a clear answer
  • Many variants show what raises and lowers conformity
  • Replicated many times, in many countries
Weaknesses
  • Original sample: male US students in the 1950s
  • Lines are not like real decisions at work
  • The popular retelling forgets the independence

Group pressure is real and replicable. But people are not sheep: most answers stay independent, and a single ally changes the picture.

More: why the "sheep" story spread

Asch's results appeared in early-1950s America, when worries about conformity and political suspicion ran high. The story "people follow the crowd even when they can see clearly" fit that worry, so it was retold again and again.

In textbooks, "3 in 4 people conformed at least once" is easier to remember than "2 in 3 answers stayed independent". Griggs (2015) shows that over time the independence almost disappeared from the retelling.

The 2023 replication still found about 33%. So the effect has not vanished. The right lesson lies in between: group pressure is strong enough to spoil a meeting, but weak enough to be broken by one person who speaks up.

Part 6

Applying the lesson

Work skill: daring to disagree in meetings, and if you run the meeting, knowing how to invite dissent.

Write your view before you listenNote your conclusion before the meeting. It is harder to be pulled away once you have committed on paper.
Find one allyTalk to one colleague beforehand. One person beside you is enough to break the unanimity.
Disagree with facts"I see it differently, because last week's numbers show…" Open with something anyone can check.
The boss speaks lastWhoever runs the meeting holds their view until the end and invites the least senior to go first.
Collect views privatelyBallots, anonymous forms or notes before discussion. Anonymity lowers the "fear of standing out".
Thank whoever disagreesYour first reaction decides whether the next person dares to speak.
What is striking is not that many people went along, but that many, under obvious pressure, still said what they saw.Paraphrasing the spirit of Asch's writing (1955, 1956), not a verbatim quote.
