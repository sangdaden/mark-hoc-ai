Part 1

The question

When we estimate a number, does the number we just saw drag us along, even when we know it means nothing? The answer after 50 years: yes, quite reliably. And at the negotiating table, the first number often wins.

Key concepts

Anchoring and insufficient adjustment

Searching for reasons that fit

selective accessibilityMussweiler and Strack, 1999

The comparison question

"Is it higher than 65?" To answer, the mind tries out "maybe it is 65".

Selected evidence

We first recall things that fit that number. They are ready at hand when we estimate.

The antidote

Actively think of reasons the number might be wrong. This reduces anchoring (Mussweiler, Strack and Pfeiffer, 2000).

The two explanations do not exclude each other. Both pull the answer toward the anchor.

The shocking original

Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec (2003): the last two digits of a social security number pulled what MBA students were willing to pay.

Weaker on replication

Fudenberg, Levine and Maniadis (2012) redid it in the lab: very weak anchoring for goods, none for lotteries. Maniadis, Tufano and List (2014) redid Ariely's unpleasant-sounds experiment: weaker and not statistically significant.

53 studies pooled

Li, Maniadis and Sedikides (2021): price anchoring is moderate, smaller than in early studies; non-random anchors pull harder.

At the negotiating table, numbers always look relevant. That is why anchoring there stays strong.

SOLIDA number shown first pulls estimates toward it

The original experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974) and hundreds of studies since. Many Labs (Klein et al., 2014) redid four anchoring questions in 36 samples: all four replicated, among the clearest effects of the project.

SOLIDThe effect holds across countries, in the lab and online

Many Labs had 6,344 people. The authors found that setting (lab or online, US or elsewhere) mostly did not change effect size. Note: Many Labs used fixed anchors in comparison questions, not a wheel.

SOLIDPaying for accuracy or warning people does not remove the anchor

Tversky and Kahneman (1974) report that paying for accuracy did not reduce the effect. Wilson et al. (1996) found that neither an incentive nor detailed warnings about anchoring made the effect smaller.

SOLIDExperts are pulled too

Estate agents' valuations followed the listing price they were given (Northcraft and Neale, 1987). Experienced judges were pulled by a number from dice when proposing a sentence (Englich, Mussweiler and Strack, 2006). Individual samples are not large, but many studies point the same way.

SOLIDWhoever makes the first offer usually gains

Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001), 3 experiments. A meta-analysis of 90 studies, 16,334 people (Petrowsky et al., 2025): there is a first-mover advantage, and first-offer level correlates 0.62 with the final price. Two preregistered experiments with 2,121 people replicated this.

CAUTIOUSPurely random numbers pull as hard as plausible ones

Ariely et al. (2003) results were much weaker on replication: very weak anchoring for goods, none for lotteries (Fudenberg, Levine and Maniadis, 2012); the unpleasant-sounds experiment gave a weak, non-significant effect (Maniadis, Tufano and List, 2014). A meta-analysis of 53 price studies (Li, Maniadis and Sedikides, 2021): non-random anchors pull harder.

CAUTIOUSThe higher the first offer, the better

Petrowsky et al. (2025): ambitious first offers give better final prices, but mean fewer deals and less satisfied recipients. The advantage depends on the deal happening at all.

CAUTIOUSThe first-offer advantage holds in every negotiation

In the same meta-analysis, first-offer effects shrink when many issues are negotiated at once. If you lack market information, going first can anchor yourself low; this is a rule of thumb from the Harvard Program on Negotiation, not a measured number.

CAUTIOUSPrecise numbers (1,865) pull harder than round ones (2,000)

Mason et al. (2013): recipients of precise offers counter closer to them. Leib et al. (2022) still found the effect but confirmed only part of the mechanism. A small effect, use it as a minor tip.

CRITICISEDKnowing about anchoring makes you immune

Wilson et al. (1996): warnings, whatever their form or timing, did not remove the anchor. What works is a concrete step: think of reasons for the opposite (Mussweiler, Strack and Pfeiffer, 2000), or focus on your own target, the other side's reservation price and their alternatives (Galinsky and Mussweiler, 2001).

Robustness 8/10. One of the most robust effects in psychology. Be careful only with "random numbers pull the value of everything" stories.

More: why anchoring stood firm when other effects fell

In psychology's "replication crisis", many famous effects shrank or vanished when redone with large samples. Anchoring is different: in Many Labs, the anchoring questions gave clear results at almost every site.

One likely reason is that the effect is large and easy to measure: all you need is a number and a question. The more surprising versions, like a social security number pulling the price of a bottle of wine, depend a lot on method and are smaller on replication.

The lesson for readers: trust the basic effect, be wary of too-good stories built on it.

Part 6

Applying the lesson

Work skill: salary talks, quotes, budget sign-off. Who names the number first, and how to handle someone else's anchor.

Write your numbers down firstTarget, floor, alternatives. Your own anchor must exist before you hear theirs.
Quotes: lead with the number and the reasonSend a quote with a clear figure and its basis; don't let the client invent one.
Absurd number? Don't counter right awaySay clearly it is not a starting point, then give your own number.
Estimation meetings: write privately firstEveryone notes their number before anyone speaks, so the first speaker doesn't anchor the room.
If the other side opens with an outrageous number, don't answer with an equally outrageous one. Make it clear you will not negotiate around that number.Paraphrase of Daniel Kahneman's advice in Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), chapter on anchors, not a verbatim quote.
