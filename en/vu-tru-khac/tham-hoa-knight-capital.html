Part 1

The question

A firm handling about 10% of trading in U.S. listed stocks, living on software, lost almost half a billion dollars from one update. Why? Not because of one careless technician, but because a deployment process had no safety net at all.

Source: SEC Order 34-70694, paragraph 11 (about 10% of trading) and paragraph 17 (the loss).

What happened

Timeline from the SEC order; the loss from Knight's own releases.

2003 · Power Peg retired, not deletedThe old feature was no longer used. The code stayed in the SMARS order router and could still be called if the right flag was set.
2005 · Changed without retestingThe part that counted filled shares, which told orders when to stop, was moved. Power Peg was not retested.
Oct 2011 · A different warningTest data was mistakenly used for live quotes, a loss of nearly $7.5 million. Knight fixed that spot and did not look wider.
From 27 Jul 2012 · New code rolled outFor NYSE's new Retail Liquidity Program (RLP). The new code reused Power Peg's flag. A technician did not copy it to 1 of the 8 servers.
1 Aug 2012 · About 45 minutesServer 8 runs the old code and sends child orders nonstop for each parent order, never learning it is filled.
2 Aug 2012 · Knight speaksA "technology issue" "related to Knight's installation of trading software", a pre-tax loss of about $440 million.
6 Aug 2012 · Rescued$400 million of preferred stock from six investors, convertible at $1.50 a share.
16 Oct 2013 · The SEC concludesA $12 million penalty. The SEC's first enforcement action under the market access rule (Rule 15c3-5).

Source: SEC Order 34-70694, paragraphs 13–15 (2003, 2005, rollout), 33 (Oct 2011 incident), 16 (1 Aug); Knight releases of 2 Aug 2012 (8-K) and 6 Aug 2012; SEC press release 2013-222.

Why: five layers of cause

Technical layer: one flag, two meanings

repurposed flag · dead codeSEC, paras 13–16

Deployment layer: one server missed, no second pair of eyes

no peer review · no written procedureSEC, paras 15 and 26

Amplification layer: from 212 orders to billions

amplificationSEC, paras 17–18

Monitoring layer: signals, but no alarms

signals that are not alarmsSEC, paras 19, 21–25

Response layer: no stop button, and the rollback made it worse

no kill switch · no incident playbookSEC, paras 21 and 27

Paper said "compliant"

The initial compliance assessment concluded Knight's controls satisfied Rule 15c3-5 (SEC, paragraph 28).

Old incident, narrow fix

The nearly $7.5 million loss in October 2011 was patched on the spot, with no broad review (SEC, paragraph 33).

Soft signature

The CEO certification (March 2012) said Knight had "processes" to comply, not that its controls complied (SEC, paragraph 34).
Part 4

Fact check: facts and myths

12 things often said about Knight Capital, checked against the SEC order and Knight's own SEC filings. Click a row to see why.

5 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 FALSE
SOLIDThe faulty orders ran for about 45 minutes

The SEC says "approximately 45 minutes" in paragraphs 1, 10 and 17. It gives no exact end time, so this piece gives none.

SOLIDThe bug came from code unused since 2003 and changed in 2005 without retesting

SEC, paragraph 14: Knight stopped using Power Peg in 2003; in 2005 it moved the filled-share counter and "did not retest" Power Peg.

SOLIDRolling back the new code made it worse

SEC, paragraph 27: Knight removed the RLP code from the 7 good servers; incoming orders then triggered Power Peg on all 7.

SOLIDKnight handled about 10% of U.S. listed stock trading

SEC, paragraph 11, for 2011–2012. SMARS alone was about 1% or more.

SOLIDA $12 million penalty, the first case under the market access rule

SEC press release 2013-222: "the SEC's first enforcement action under the market access rule". Knight settled "without admitting or denying the findings".

CAUTION"It was just one technician who forgot to copy a file"

That was the trigger (paragraph 15). But most of the SEC order is about something else: no written deployment procedure, no second review, no output monitoring, no halt, no incident response (paragraphs 15, 21–27).

CAUTION"They lost $440 million in 45 minutes"

$440 million was Knight's first estimate. The booked trading loss was $457.6 million; the SEC says $460 million. Orders ran for 45 minutes, but the loss was locked in as positions were unwound afterwards.

CAUTION"There were 97 warning e-mails and nobody bothered to read them"

The 97 e-mails are real (paragraph 19). But the SEC says Knight "did not design these types of messages to be system alerts". The lesson is about alert design, not lazy staff.

CAUTION"The new code was buggy"

The new RLP code worked on 7 servers (paragraph 16). The danger was old code not deleted, a reused flag, and one missed server.

CAUTION"Knight had no kill switch at all"

SMARS had no procedure to halt on aberrant activity (paragraph 21) and there was no incident response procedure (paragraph 27). But Knight did have automatic shutdown for certain strategies of one trading group (footnote 7).

FALSE"A rogue AI algorithm"

There is no AI or machine learning anywhere in the record. It was old order-routing code doing exactly what it was written to do, woken by a reused flag on a server that missed an update (SEC, paragraphs 13–16).

FALSE"Knight went bankrupt that day"

On 6 Aug 2012 Knight received $400 million from Jefferies, Blackstone, GETCO, Stephens, Stifel and TD Ameritrade, convertible into about 267 million shares at $1.50 a share. On 1 Jul 2013 Knight and GETCO combined to form KCG Holdings.

Source: SEC Order 34-70694 (paragraphs as noted in each row); SEC press release 2013-222; Knight releases of 2 Aug, 6 Aug and 17 Oct 2012.

No villain, no AI. Just a deployment process missing every safety net, meeting an order machine faster than any human.

More: why this is a lesson for every software team

Take away the word "stocks" and the rest is everyday life on any software team: an old feature nobody deleted, a config flag reused to save effort, an unchecked rollout to several machines, an error-mail channel everyone filters into a folder.

What made Knight different was speed. An automated order machine can do in minutes what people would need a week to do. That is why the SEC stresses that controls must be as automatic as the system itself, and that stopping the system must be prepared in advance, not invented mid-incident.

Source for "More": SEC Order 34-70694, paragraphs 3–4 (the role of controls in automated trading); the rest is Mark's commentary.

Part 6

Work lesson: deploy with a checklist and a stop button

For every "push to production": software updates, config changes, mass e-mails, ad campaigns.

A checklist with a name on itList every target. After the deploy, someone else confirms each one runs the right version.
Delete what you don't useCode, flags, accounts, access left idle for nine years are nine years of risk waiting.
Fewer alerts, real alertsA channel that gets 97 e-mails nobody reads is no channel. Cut the noise; keep only what should wake someone up.
Practise stopping before you need toWrite down who may stop it, how, and when. Don't "roll back" until you know the old version is safe.
Without appropriate controls, automation can turn an otherwise manageable error into an extreme event.SEC, Order 34-70694, paragraph 3 (2013), paraphrased.
