One-minute recap

Essence

Solved is not learnedPeople reached the answer, but very few noticed the rule behind it.
A crowded deskAiming at the answer forces the mind to hold the goal, the current state, the gap and subgoals. No room left to learn.
Worked examplesFollowing ready-made steps leaves room to see the structure. A 2023 meta-analysis in maths: a medium effect.
Not for everyoneExperienced learners do worse with examples than with solving: the expertise reversal effect.
Hard to measure"Load" is usually self-rated. The types of load overlap, and researchers still argue.

Part 1

The paper's question

Why doesn't solving lots of problems always make us better at them? Sweller's answer: while we push toward the answer, the mind is so busy that there is no room left to learn how to solve.

Source: abstract of Sweller (1988). The question is Mark's restatement of the paper's core idea.

The discovery

Who, where, how, and what people thought about practice problems at the time.

1969–1972 · AdelaideJohn Sweller takes his BA and then his PhD in psychology at the University of Adelaide, and later works at the University of New South Wales (UNSW), Sydney.
1972 · Means-ends analysisNewell and Simon describe how people solve problems: look at the gap between where you are and the goal, find a move that shrinks it.
1980 · Experts differ from novicesStudies of physics problem solving: novices work backward from the goal and set subgoals; experts work forward using "schemas" they already hold.
Early 1980s · The number puzzleOnly multiply by 3 and subtract 69. Goal reached, rule rarely seen.
1985 · Worked examplesSweller and Cooper give algebra learners examples to study instead of problems to solve: faster learning, and later problems of the same type solved faster with fewer errors.
1988 · The paperPulls it all into one explanation: goal-directed solving fills working memory.

Source: Sweller's UNSW profile; degree years from his ATD profile (secondary source); Newell and Simon (1972), Human Problem Solving; Larkin, McDermott, Simon and Simon (1980), Science 208; Sweller (2023); Sweller and Cooper (1985), Cognition and Instruction 2(1), 59–89, per the paper's abstract.

Part 3

What the paper says, in 3 sentences

  1. When we solve a problem by aiming at the goal, we must hold at once the goal, where we are, the gap between them, and any subgoals.
  2. That uses up most of working memory, leaving little room to learn "schemas" (recognising the problem type and how to solve it).
  3. So teaching that reduces goal-chasing, such as problems without a specific goal or worked examples, can help novices learn faster.
"Conventional problem solving in the form of means-ends analysis requires a relatively large amount of cognitive processing capacity which is consequently unavailable for schema acquisition."Sweller (1988), abstract.

Part 5

Fact check: what holds and what is myth

12 things often said about the paper and cognitive load theory. Click a line to see why.

4 SOLID
6 CAUTION
2 FALSE
SOLIDNovices learn better from worked examples than from solving from scratch

Sweller and Cooper (1985) in algebra; Barbieri et al. (2023) meta-analysis: 55 maths studies, g = 0.48, medium. Correct examples alone worked better than incorrect ones or a mix.

SOLIDSolving a problem does not mean you learned how to solve it

The multiply-by-3, subtract-69 puzzle: goals reached, but very few saw the alternating rule (Sweller recounting in 2016 and 2023 the work of Sweller, Mawer and Howe 1982). The 1988 paper: solvers with a specific goal learned less of the problem structure.

SOLIDThe 1988 paper says goal-directed solving crowds out learning

That is what the abstract says: capacity used for means-ends analysis is "unavailable for schema acquisition".

SOLIDThe theory has entered official guidance for teachers

NSW Department of Education, Centre for Education Statistics and Evaluation (CESE), 2017: "Cognitive load theory: Research that teachers really need to understand".

CAUTION"The 1988 paper proved it with large classroom experiments"

The paper's evidence is a computer simulation and controlled problem-solving experiments. Evidence from many real classrooms came later, through meta-analyses.

CAUTION"Cognitive load can be measured precisely"

It is usually measured with self-rated effort scales. de Jong (2010) criticised how hard it is to measure and to separate load types. A 2022 meta-analysis: the questionnaires are reliable, but correlations between load types partly conflict with the theory.

CAUTION"Letting students struggle first is a waste of time"

Sinha and Kapur (2021), 53 studies: trying first and being taught afterwards can help conceptual understanding when well designed; for procedural skills the two orders were equal. Ashman, Kalyuga and Sweller (2020): the more interacting elements in a task, the better explicit instruction first. It depends on the task and the learner.

CAUTION"Working memory holds 7 items"

Miller's 7 (1956) has been revisited: Cowan (2001) argued for about 4 chunks. See Papers That Changed How We Learn #2. Load theory only needs "working memory is limited".

CAUTION"The 1988 paper is the whole of cognitive load theory"

The 1988 paper was the seed. Three load types and "germane load" came in 1998 (Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas), the expertise reversal effect in 2003, a major update in 2019. Sweller (2023) writes that failed replications forced the theory to become more detailed.

CAUTION"It is the single most important thing for teachers to know"

Dylan Wiliam wrote this on Twitter on 26 Jan 2017 (secondary source: blogs embedding the post). He did say it, but it is an opinion. An article on the Chartered College of Teaching site calls it a bold claim.

FALSE"Worked examples are always better, for everyone"

Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003): techniques that help novices a lot can lose their effect, or even backfire, with experienced learners. Experts should move on to solving.

FALSE"The easier learning feels, the better"

The theory says cut extraneous load (messy presentation, irrelevant tasks), not all effort. Since 1998 it has included "germane load". Self-testing (paper #6) is effortful and still helps memory last.

Source: as noted in each line; full list in the Sources section.

Part 6

What the latest research says

2019 · "20 years later"Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas review two decades of the theory and set new directions (Educational Psychology Review 31, 261–292).
2021 · Try first, learn afterSinha and Kapur pool 53 studies, 166 comparisons: trying a problem before instruction helps conceptual understanding when well designed (Review of Educational Research).
2022 · Measuring load by questionnaireA meta-analysis of 4 common questionnaires: reliable, but the relations between load types partly do not fit the theory (Educational Psychology Review).
2023 · Worked examples in mathsBarbieri et al. pool 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes: a medium effect, g = 0.48 (Educational Psychology Review 35).
2023 · Sweller looks backFailed replications did not topple the theory; they forced it to say more clearly when it applies and when it does not (Educational Psychology Review 35:95).

Part 8

Apply it: study a worked example before solving alone

Usable from tomorrow in any subject with exercises: maths, physics, programming, accounting, grammar.

Each new type: one exampleDon't dive into solving a problem type you have never seen. Find a correct solution and read it slowly.
Ask "why this step?"Write one line of explanation per step. If you can't, you don't understand it yet.
A twin problem right afterClose the example, solve the same type with new numbers. Stuck? Look at just that step.
Keep it in one placeMute notifications while studying, keep diagrams next to their explanations. Cut extra load, not thinking.

The first three rules rest on the worked-example effect (sources in the figure above). "Keep it in one place" is Mark's suggestion based on the theory's idea of cutting extraneous load.

Self-check: am I ready to drop the examples?

1. Close the example. Can I rewrite the solution and explain each step?

2. Same type, new numbers: can I solve it in about the time it took to read the example?

3. A slightly different type: can I spot which part resembles the example?

4. If all three are "yes", drop the example and solve alone. If question 1 is "no", go back to step 1.

Goal-directed problem solving serves to reach the goal, not to learn.Sweller's conclusion from the number puzzle, as he recounts it in Sweller (2023); paraphrased by Mark.
