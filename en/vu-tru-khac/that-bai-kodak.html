Part 1

The question

The company that built the first digital camera, and later sold more digital cameras than anyone in America, still went bankrupt. Why? Not because it could not see the future. Because for too long the future was graded with the yardstick of the past.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

14 things often said about Kodak, checked against Kodak's SEC filings, the bankruptcy record and clearly labelled sources. Click a line to see why.

6 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDA Kodak engineer built the first hand-held digital camera in 1975

Steven Sasson, 1975 prototype; Kodak was granted US patent 4,131,919 in 1978. Note: it was the first self-contained hand-held digital camera, not the first device ever to make a digital image (IEEE Spectrum, secondary source).

SOLIDIn 2003 Kodak cut its dividend to fund digital, and shareholders objected

2004 POS AM filing: semi-annual dividend from $0.90 to $0.25 from 24 Sep 2003. Wall Street Journal (21 Oct 2003, secondary source): stock down about 14%, a group of shareholders campaigned to reverse it.

SOLIDIn 2005 Kodak was No. 1 in US digital cameras

IDC via AP (secondary source): 24.9% unit share, 7.05 million cameras; Canon 17.7%, Sony 16.9%. Kodak itself claimed the No. 1 spot in its Q1 and Q2 2005 8-Ks.

SOLIDIn 2005 digital revenue overtook traditional revenue

54% of total 2005 revenue, per Kodak's January 2006 results release (via dpreview, secondary source). This is the company's own figure.

SOLIDOnce film started falling, it fell fast

2008 10-K: in 2007 film capture sales fell 30% and traditional photofinishing 19% from 2006.

SOLIDKodak filed for bankruptcy on 19 Jan 2012, with debts above assets

Kodak release, 19 Jan 2012: Chapter 11 in the Southern District of New York, $950 million loan from Citigroup. The petition listed $5.1 billion in assets and $6.75 billion in debts (CNN Money, Rochester Business Journal; secondary source). Case no. 12-10202, Judge Allan Gropper.

CAUTION"Management buried the invention to protect film"

Sasson recalls scepticism. But the 1975 device was not usable: 23 seconds to record one image, viewed on a TV. Snopes (secondary source) concluded the main reason was that it was not ready. Kodak still patented it and kept researching (1986 sensor, 1991 DCS 100).

CAUTIONThe line "That's cute, but don't tell anyone about it"

Widely shared, but no dated original source was found. The sourced remark is Sasson's to the New York Times in 2008: managers were convinced "no one would ever want to look at their pictures on a television set" (secondary source).

CAUTION"In 1981 Kodak knew it had 10 years"

Only in the memoir of Vince Barabba, who ran the study (The Decision Loom, 2011). Another summary says 20 years. The original study has not been seen.

CAUTION"Digital cameras killed Kodak"

Kodak won in digital cameras (2005), then abandoned that business too (8-K, 9 Feb 2012). Film fell fast, a camera sold once has no loop like film, and retiree costs were heavy (above $1.2 billion, 8-K November 2012). Several causes stacked up.

CAUTION"Fujifilm faced the same and survived, so Kodak was just badly led"

Fujifilm diversified early; film was about 60% of its operating profit in 2000 (Harvard d3, PetaPixel; secondary sources). But its structure differed (Fuji Xerox), and secondary sources disagree on film's share of revenue (54% or 33.5%). The comparison is suggestive, not proof of a single cause.

FALSE"Kodak did not see digital cameras coming"

Kodak built a prototype in 1975, patented it in 1978, made a megapixel sensor in 1986, a professional digital camera in 1991, turned to digital in 2003 and was No. 1 in the US in 2005. Kodak saw. What it lacked was speed, and a way of making money to replace the film loop.

FALSE"In 1998 Kodak had 170,000 employees and sold 85% of the world's photo paper"

A post circulating since 2016. Peak headcount was 145,300 in 1988 (Kodak chronology via the Rochester Business Journal); about 86,000 in 1998 (The Week, 2011, via TruthOrFiction). Both are secondary sources, but both are far below 170,000. The 85% has no source.

FALSE"Kodak went bankrupt and disappeared"

The court confirmed the reorganisation plan on 20 Aug 2013 (Kodak 8-K). On 3 Sep 2013 Kodak emerged, smaller, focused on commercial printing. The personal imaging business went to the UK Kodak Pension Plan and became Kodak Alaris (8-K April 2013; Institutional Investor, secondary source).

Kodak was not blind. It saw very early, but let the film business sit as judge for too long, and when it turned, the new money was no longer in what Kodak sold.

More: why the "didn't see it" story is so easy to believe

"Kodak didn't see digital coming" is a tidy story: an arrogant giant, an ignored future. It is easier to tell than the messy truth: a company that saw, studied, invested, even won, and still lost.

The messy version is more useful at work. Most of us do not lack information about the future. We lack a way to stop new ideas being graded by the yardstick of whatever pays our bills today.

Part 6

Work lesson: don't let the cash cow judge the new idea

For any team with one thing that "feeds the family" and one new idea that is still small: a product, a sales channel, a way of working.

A separate judgeGrade a new idea by what you learned and whether real users come back. Not by this month's revenue of the old business.
Eat yourself firstWrite one page: "If we were the rival, how would we kill this business?" Then ask: why aren't we doing it first?
Switch points in advanceWrite it down now: which metric, at which threshold, moves people and money. Decide while calm, act when due.
Where is the recurring money?Is the new product sold once or sold forever? Winning the "camera" but losing the "film" can still be losing.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. What question stopped your team's last new idea? Was it a yardstick of the old work?

2. Who on the team can say "yes" to a new idea without the old business agreeing?

3. If a rival launched a cheaper, faster version next month, what would you lose first?

4. Is your switch point written down? If not, write one number today.

5. Where does the new product earn recurring money: service, supplies, a subscription, or nowhere?
