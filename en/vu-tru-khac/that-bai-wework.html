Part 1

The question

A company with hundreds of thousands of members and revenue nearly doubling each year: why did it still fall? Because each desk it sold did not pay for its own rent, and a story about "community" covered that up for years.

Source: 527,000 members at June 30, 2019 (CNBC citing the S-1, secondary source); revenue and losses: S-1, selected financial data. The answer is Mark's analysis.

Up to the peak and down again in about five years. Dates and numbers come from SEC filings and the companies' own announcements.

People layer: too much power in one place

governanceS-1 and amended S-1

20 votes per share

The founder's high-vote shares held control. The September 2019 amendment cut this to 10 votes; he still kept a majority.

The "We" name for $5.9 million

The company issued $5.9 million of partnership interests to the founder's private entity to acquire the "We" trademark. Unwound in the amended filing.

Related real estate deals

The founder pledged to give the company any profits from real estate transactions he had entered into with the company.

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

14 things often said about WeWork, checked against SEC filings and the companies' announcements. Click each row for the reasoning.

SOLIDIn 2018, WeWork lost more than its revenue

S-1, selected financial data: revenue $1.82 billion, total net loss $1.93 billion.

SOLIDThe IPO filing's mission was "to elevate the world's consciousness"

The line sits in the S-1 prospectus summary (cited by CNBC and Fortune; WeWork's own 2019 newsroom post says the same).

SOLIDThe company paid $5.9 million for the "We" name to the founder's private entity

As stated in the S-1 (paid in partnership interests, not cash). The September 2019 amendment says the issuance was unwound "At Adam's direction". Secondary sources disagree on the date it was returned.

SOLIDIn 2023, WeWork itself wrote "substantial doubt" about its survival

Q2 2023 10-Q (Aug 8, 2023), citing losses, cash needs and higher member churn. The interim CEO called it a technical accounting determination.

CAUTION"A $1.9 billion loss" or "a $1.6 billion loss" in 2018?

Both are in the S-1. $1.93 billion is the total net loss; $1.61 billion is the part attributable to the company after noncontrolling interests. Press reports picked one or the other, so they look contradictory.

CAUTION"WeWork was once worth $47 billion"

That was the valuation of a private funding round in January 2019 (press reports, secondary source). After the IPO filing, talk of the valuation fell to about $10–15 billion, then the IPO was pulled. An open market never paid $47 billion.

CAUTION"WeWork owed $47 billion in rent"

Close on the number, wrong on "owed". S-1: $34.0 billion of undiscounted fixed minimum lease payments, plus $13.1 billion of leases signed but not yet commenced. It is paid over many years ($23.8 billion falls in 2024 and later), not debt due now.

CAUTION"The IPO failed only because of Adam Neumann"

The governance problems were real and sat right in the S-1. But losses larger than revenue and $47 billion of rent were in the S-1 too. In 2021–2023, after he had left the CEO job, the company still lost money and then went bankrupt.

CAUTION"SoftBank pumped in $9.5 billion to save WeWork"

The package was $5 billion of new financing (mostly debt), $1.5 billion of an existing commitment accelerated, and a tender offer for up to $3 billion of existing shareholders' stock. SoftBank ended the $3 billion tender offer on Apr 2, 2020.

CAUTION"WeWork went bankrupt because of COVID"

The pandemic did hurt: 2021 revenue ($2.57 billion) was below 2020 ($3.42 billion). But the big losses date from 2018, before the pandemic. In 2023 the company cited member churn, office oversupply and rising competition.

CAUTION"After bankruptcy, WeWork disappeared"

Chapter 11 is a restructuring, not a shutdown. WeWork kept operating and emerged from bankruptcy in 2024. The 2024 date was not checked against court records for this piece, hence the caution label.

FALSE"Shared office space was a bad idea from the start"

The demand was real: about 527,000 members in mid-2019 (CNBC citing the S-1). In 2023 WeWork itself cited rising competition in flexible space, meaning many others do the same thing. What failed was not the idea but the structure: long lease commitments, short member commitments, and growth faster than profitability.

FALSE"Listing in 2021 saved WeWork"

After the SPAC merger (Oct 20, 2021), the company still lost $4.44 billion in 2021 and about $2.03 billion in 2022, then wrote "substantial doubt" (Aug 2023) and filed for bankruptcy (Nov 2023).

FALSE"WeWork was a tech company"

The biggest obligation in the S-1 is rent, not software or servers. Its economics are those of a landlord re-letting space, with the same vacancy risk.

Source: S-1 (Aug 14, 2019) and amended S-1 (Sep 2019); SoftBank releases Oct 23, 2019 and Apr 2, 2020; WeWork Inc. 8-K Oct 20, 2021; 10-K 2021, 2022; Q2 2023 10-Q; Chapter 11 announcement Nov 6, 2023; secondary sources named in each row.

The idea was not wrong. The math was: selling short what you lease long, then using a story and fresh capital to avoid looking at that math.

More: why "warnings in advance" gets 5/5

Every number that sank the IPO was in the S-1 itself: losses larger than revenue, about $47 billion of rent owed, voting power concentrated in one person. No investigation or leak was needed. You only had to read it.

The lesson is that for years before, private funding rounds still paid high prices for the same model. The good story ran ahead of the numbers, until the numbers had to be printed for the public to read.

Part 6

Work lesson: separate a good story from unit economics

For any "go big" proposal: opening a branch, launching a product, signing a long contract, hiring a new team.

One page per unitOne client, one store, one project: money in per month, money out per month, what is left. Without this page, don't talk about "scaling".
Match the termsHow long is the contract we sign, how long do clients commit? If 20% of clients leave next month, who pays the gap?
Ask "what was left out"Whenever you see an "adjusted", "before costs", "excluding..." metric, ask: what is the real number after all costs?
The storyteller is not the approverThe person most in love with the idea should not be the only one signing off. Someone must be allowed to say "not yet".
Check your own team: 5 questions for the next proposal

1. What is our unit (a client, an order, a branch)? How much does each unit make or lose after all costs?

2. How long are we committing to spend, and how long are clients committing to pay?

3. If you strip the adjectives (community, revolution, mission), which numbers are left on the slide?

4. Which costs were left out of the proposal's best-looking number?

5. Who on the team may say "not yet", and when did they last say it?
