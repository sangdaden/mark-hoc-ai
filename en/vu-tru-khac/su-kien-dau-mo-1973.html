Part 1

The question

The world did not run out of oil in 1973. So why did a shortfall of about a tenth nearly quadruple the price, and send people into lines for gasoline? Because in the short run almost nobody can use less oil right away, so the price has to jump very far to balance. Where the price was held down, the shortage showed up as lines.

Part 3

Key decisions

Four decisions, seen through the eyes of the time: who decided, what they knew, what they chose, and what happened next. No judging with hindsight.

1

Using oil output during the war

17 Oct 1973
Who decided
Oil ministers of the 11 OAPEC members (Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries), meeting in Kuwait.
What they knew
War had been under way since 6 October. Industrial countries depended heavily on Middle East oil, and oil demand is slow to fall.
What they chose
Cut output 5%, then a further 5% each month, plus an embargo on some countries, with the stated condition that Israel withdraw from the occupied territories.
What happened
Supply fell about 9% by the estimate at the time. The US embargo was lifted on 18 March 1974, once talks between Israel and Syria looked likely.

Source: FRUS vol. XXV, doc. 200; 24 Oct 1973 memo (CIA Reading Room); Office of the Historian, "Oil Embargo, 1973–1974"; the 18 March date per UPI (secondary source).

2

Setting prices alone, not negotiating with the oil companies

16 Oct and Dec 1973
Who decided
Six Persian Gulf OPEC members (including a non-Arab state).
What they knew
The market was already tight before the war: the posted price had risen from $1.80 (1971) to about $2.90 (mid-1973). With supply cut, buyers would pay far more.
What they chose
Raise the posted price 70% ($3.01 → $5.12) on 16 October, then to $11.65 from 1 January 1974.
What happened
Prices stayed high after the embargo ended: imported oil cost the US nearly $14 a barrel in 1975, against about $3.20 in 1972.

Source: FRUS vol. XXV, doc. 200; CIA Reading Room, posted price table; Federal Reserve History; Bernanke 2008 ($3.20 and nearly $14).

3

Holding prices down by decree

Nov 1973 – 1974
Who decided
The Nixon administration and the US Congress.
What they knew
Inflation was already a big worry; the economy had been under wage and price controls since 1971. Letting gasoline prices jump would hit working people hard.
What they chose
Add price controls specific to oil (Nov 1973), allocate fuel supplies, call for conservation, set a 55 mph speed limit.
What happened
With prices not doing the rationing, lines did it instead; in 1974 some places allowed gasoline purchases only on odd or even days by license plate. Regular gasoline still rose from 38.8 to 53.2 cents a gallon (1973 → 1974).

Source: Bernanke 2008; signing statement of 2 Jan 1974; EIA Monthly Energy Review table 9.4 (1973) and US Department of Energy AFDC (1974), leaded regular, including taxes.

4

Coordinate, or every country for itself

Feb 1974 – Nov 1974
Who decided
Governments of oil-importing countries: the US, Japan, Canada, Norway and the European Community states.
What they knew
Each could sign its own deals with exporters, but that meant bidding against each other and pushing prices up. Views were split: the French foreign minister doubted the conference would work.
What they chose
Meet in Washington (11–13 Feb 1974), keep negotiating, and sign the Agreement on an International Energy Program (Nov 1974).
What happened
The IEA was born: stocks of at least 90 days of net imports, sharing in a shortage. France did not sign at first.

Source: FRUS vol. XXXVI, doc. 318; CVCE, Michel Jobert press conference, 13 Feb 1974; the IEP Agreement and France's initial absence via secondary sources (CORES, Wikipedia).

Part 4

What the world learned

Nobody can prevent the next shock. Oil importers learned to build buffers so that a shock does not become a crisis.

New institution

The IEA (Nov 1974), 16 founding members (secondary source), autonomous within the OECD. In March 2026, the sixth release: 400 million barrels, the largest ever; in 2022 it was 182 million barrels in two stages (IEA release).

New laws

US: the 1975 EPCA created the reserve with a policy of storing up to 1 billion barrels; first oil arrived on 21 Jul 1977 (US Department of Justice). Japan: the 1975 Petroleum Stockpiling Law helped companies keep 70 days of oil products (IAEA).

New habits

More fuel-efficient cars (CAFE). In Japan, oil fell from 77.4% of primary energy in 1973 to 49.7% in the early 2000s, thanks to industrial conservation and other energy sources (IAEA).
More: the lesson on price controls

In 2008, Fed Chairman Ben Bernanke recalled the image of gas lines in the 1970s and tied it to price controls: when prices are not allowed to rise, gasoline gets rationed by waiting. He also compared: after the 1973 shock, US inflation rose by about 6 percentage points.

Many countries later chose to let prices reflect the shortage and to help vulnerable people directly, instead of holding prices down for everyone. That is a policy trend, not a formula that is right everywhere.

Source: Bernanke, "Remarks on Class Day 2008", Federal Reserve, 4 Jun 2008. The last sentence is Mark's comment.

Part 5

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

13 things people often say about the 1973 oil crisis, checked against named sources. Tap each line for the reasoning.

6 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 FALSE
SOLIDOil prices nearly quadrupled in a few months

From about $2.90 to $11.65 a barrel by January 1974 (Federal Reserve History). The State Department's Office of the Historian: the price "first doubled, then quadrupled".

SOLIDThe embargo targeted the US, then extended to the Netherlands, Portugal and South Africa

Office of the Historian, "Oil Embargo, 1973–1974": it banned oil exports to these countries and included output cuts.

SOLIDThe embargo ended in March 1974 but prices did not go back

Office of the Historian and Federal Reserve History. Bernanke (2008): imported oil was about $3.20 in 1972 and nearly $14 in 1975.

SOLIDThe 55 mph limit was set to save fuel

Nixon's signing statement of 2 Jan 1974: the main purpose of the law was to reduce gasoline and diesel use; states wanting highway funds had to set a 55 mph maximum.

SOLIDThe IEA was born in 1974 with a 90-day stock obligation

Agreement on an International Energy Program (Nov 1974): at least 90 days of net imports; in a severe shortage, release stocks, restrain demand, share supplies (via secondary sources, as the IEA site was blocked in the working environment).

SOLIDThe US Strategic Petroleum Reserve was a child of the crisis

EPCA signed 22 Dec 1975; first oil entered the reserve on 21 Jul 1977, about 412,000 barrels (US Department of Justice, ENRD).

CAUTION"OPEC imposed the oil embargo"

The output cuts and embargo were decisions of OAPEC, the group of Arab oil exporters. The 16 October 1973 price rise was by six Persian Gulf OPEC members (FRUS vol. XXV, doc. 200). Two organisations, two kinds of decision.

CAUTION"Oil went from $3 to $12"

Right order of magnitude, but these are posted prices, and the starting point depends on the source: about $2.90 (Fed, mid-1973) or $3.01 (FRUS, just before 16 Oct). The end point is $11.65 (1 Jan 1974). Spot prices at the tightest moments sometimes differed from posted prices.

CAUTION"The gas lines were caused only by the embargo"

The embargo cut supply. But Bernanke (2008) points out that price controls meant gasoline was rationed by waiting instead of by price. Economists still debate which part mattered more.

CAUTION"The oil crisis caused the inflation of the 1970s"

Federal Reserve History: oil complicated an inflation picture that already had many causes (Fed Chairman Burns listed Vietnam War spending, dollar devaluations, the 1972–73 boom, rising food prices).

CAUTION"Japan learned the lesson best"

IAEA: oil's share of Japan's primary energy fell from 77.4% (1973) to 49.7% (early 2000s). A big, real change. But "best" is a comparison no source here proves.

FALSE"Gasoline prices rose because the world was running out of oil"

The oil underground did not shrink in October 1973. What was cut was the amount offered for sale, by decisions to cut output and embargo (FRUS vol. XXV, doc. 200). Demand was slow to fall, so prices jumped; where prices were held down, the shortage became lines (Bernanke 2008).

FALSE"The whole world couldn't buy oil"

The embargo targeted certain countries. By the estimate at the time, supply fell by a net 4 million barrels a day, roughly 9% (US Defense Department memo, 24 Oct 1973). Most oil kept flowing, at much higher prices.

Source: as noted in each line; full list in Part 9.

More: why "early detection" gets only 2/5

Dependence on imported oil was no secret in 1973: in Japan, oil was nearly four fifths of energy. But almost no importing country had a large enough reserve or a sharing mechanism in place. The buffers were built only after the shock.

To be fair: oil had been cheap for years before 1973, so spending money on stockpiles looked like waste. This is exactly the kind of risk that is easiest to ignore.

Part 7

Work lesson: don't let a single source of supply decide your survival

For any team that relies on one "can't do without" thing: a supplier, a big customer, the one person who knows how, a tool.

Find single points of failureList the 5 things your team can't do without. Mark the ones with only one source.
Count your days of survivalIf that source broke tomorrow morning, how many days could the team hold out? Write the number down.
Use less, add a second sourceReduce the dependence (document, automate) and try out an alternative before you need it.
Back each other up in advanceAgree with another team: when either side is short of people or tools, the other lends.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. What would freeze your team if it disappeared for a week? How many sources are there for it?

2. What are the team's "days of survival" if that source breaks? Who knows this number?

3. When did the team last try the backup? If never, assume you don't have one.

4. When short, is the team "holding the price": acting as if all is fine, so the shortage shows up as missed deadlines?

5. Is there a team you have already agreed to back up, and that will back you up?
