Essence

Real technologyThe internet changed how we buy, read the news and work. That part was not a bubble.
Inflated pricesThe Nasdaq went from 1,000 points (Jul 1995) to 5,048 (Mar 2000): five times higher in under 5 years.

Part 1

The question

If the internet was real, why did the bubble still burst? Because a real technology does not make every price reasonable. People were right about the future, but paid too much, too early, and measured it in page views instead of money.

The answer is Mark's view, based on the sourced facts in Parts 2 and 4.

Part 3

Key decisions

Four decisions, seen through the eyes of the time: what the decision makers knew and did not know.

1 · Raising the offer price for an unprofitable company 9 Aug 1995

Who decided
Netscape and its underwriting banks.
What they knew
The web browser was spreading fast, revenue was reportedly doubling every quarter, demand for shares was huge. The company had no profits.
What they chose
Raise the offer price from $14 to $28 and still list early.
What followed
Opened at $71, closed at $58.25, a value of about $2.9 billion. The door opened for unprofitable internet companies to go public.

Source: AP via the Tampa Bay Times, 10 Aug 1995; Virginian-Pilot, 10 Aug 1995; Bloomberg (Ritholtz, 9 Aug 2017) (secondary source).

2 · A warning as a question, with no tools used 5 Dec 1996

Who decided
Federal Reserve chairman Alan Greenspan.
What he knew
Low inflation, a growing economy, sharply rising stock prices. The Nasdaq was at about 1,300. No measure could say for sure what was "too high".
What he chose
Ask publicly about "irrational exuberance", but not use interest rates or regulation to restrain stock prices.
What followed
Markets fell for a day and then rose for more than three more years. In 2002 Greenspan explained that it is very difficult to identify a bubble definitively until after it bursts.

Source: speech "The Challenge of Central Banking in a Democratic Society", 5 Dec 1996 (checked via excerpts); DeLong and Magin (2006), NBER WP 12011; Jackson Hole remarks of 30 Aug 2002 per CNN Money (secondary source).

3 · Funding "get big fast", measured by traffic 1998–1999

Who decided
Venture capital funds, investment banks, stock market investors.
What they knew
New listings jumped on day one. Internet users were growing very fast. Whoever grabbed market share first seemed likely to win.
What they chose
Fund and list loss-making companies, scored on user growth and traffic.
What followed
1999 had 446 IPOs, up 70.9% on day one on average. By March 2000 at least 51 of 207 internet companies were forecast to run out of cash within 12 months.

Source: Jay Ritter's data via PBS Frontline; Barron's, 20 Mar 2000, "Burning Up", citing Pegasus Research International (secondary source).

4 · Raising rates against inflation, then cutting fast Jun 1999–Dec 2001

Who decided
The Fed's Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).
What they knew
A hot economy, low unemployment, worry that inflation would return after three cuts in late 1998.
What they chose
Six rate rises, from 4.75% (Jun 1999) to 6.5% (May 2000). When the economy weakened, 11 cuts in 2001, down to 1.75%.
What followed
Money got dearer just as many companies needed to raise more. The 2001 recession lasted 8 months, shorter than the post-World War II average according to the NBER.

Source: Fed rate history via fedprimerate.com, CNN Money, 24 Aug 1999, and BCA Research (one source dates the last rise to Jul 2000, the others to 16 May 2000); AP via CNN Money, 11 Dec 2001; NBER, 17 Jul 2003 (via excerpts) (secondary source).

Mark does not grade these decisions with hindsight from after 2000. The right question is: with what was known then, was there a more reasonable option? The judgement is Mark's.

Part 4

What the world learned

What changed after the bubble burst: accounting rules, disclosure rules, how research is done, and the habits of the people who fund companies.

Revenue must be real money

SAB 101 gave specific guidance for e-commerce companies; EITF 99-19 separated gross from net revenue reporting. Many companies switched to net, and revenue "shrank" to the part they actually kept.

Everyone hears it at once

Regulation FD bars companies from telling analysts material news privately before telling the public.

Research does not work for sales

The 2003 settlement: $875 million in penalties and disgorgement, $432.5 million for independent research, $80 million for investor education.

Source: SEC staff speech (spch495) and Journal of Accountancy, Jun 2001, on SAB 101 and EITF 99-19; FindLaw on Regulation FD; SEC press release 2003-54. Checked via excerpts (secondary source).

Survivors

Amazon posted its first net profit in Q4 2001: $5 million, though it still lost $567.3 million for the full year 2001.

The internet kept growing

By the end of 2018, 51.2% of the world's population (3.9 billion people) used the internet, per the ITU.

Central banks

The Fed kept the view that it should not fight bubbles with interest rates because they are hard to spot in advance; the question is still debated.

Source: Amazon release of 22 Jan 2002 (via excerpts); ITU press release 2018-PR40 (via excerpts); Greenspan at Jackson Hole, 30 Aug 2002, per CNN Money (secondary source). "Still debated" is Mark's view.

Part 5

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

13 things often said about the dot-com bubble. Click each line to see why.

4 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDThe Nasdaq peaked on 10 Mar 2000

It closed at 5,048.62, a day after first passing 5,000 (AP, secondary source). The intraday high was higher, about 5,132.

SOLIDThe Nasdaq lost nearly 80% of its value

On closing prices: 5,048.62 down to 1,114.11 (9 Oct 2002), a fall of 77.9% (Mark's calculation). Reuters gives 78.4%, using the intraday peak and low (a low of 1,108.49 on 10 Oct 2002). Different measures, neither is wrong.

SOLIDIt took 15 years for the Nasdaq to regain its peak

On 23 Apr 2015 it closed at 5,056.06 (Fortune, Reuters; secondary source). That is not adjusted for inflation; adjusted, it took longer.

SOLIDPets.com listed and closed within a year

IPO in Feb 2000 at $11; announced its shutdown in Nov 2000, about 268 days later. Sources give different announcement dates, 6 or 7 Nov 2000 (secondary source).

SOLIDRight at the peak, many internet companies were close to running out of money

Barron's, 20 Mar 2000, "Burning Up": per Pegasus Research International, at least 51 of 207 internet companies would run out of cash within 12 months (archived on Prof. Damodaran's NYU site; secondary source).

SOLIDAnalysts praised stocks in public and trashed them in private

New York Attorney General's affidavit (Apr 2002): a Merrill Lynch analyst privately called a stock "a piece of junk" while it carried the firm's top public rating (CNN Money, secondary source). The 28 Apr 2003 settlement with 10 firms came to about $1.4 billion; Henry Blodget paid $4 million and was barred for life (SEC 2003-54, CNN Money; via excerpts). The firms settled without admitting or denying wrongdoing.

CAUTION"The Fed blew the bubble with cheap money"

The Fed cut rates three times in late 1998, but raised them six times from Jun 1999 to May 2000, to 6.5%, exactly when the bubble was inflating fastest (secondary source). Rates were one factor, along with expectations about technology, IPO fever and wrong metrics. There is no agreed single cause.

CAUTION"The burst caused the 2001 recession"

The NBER dates the recession from Mar 2001 to Nov 2001, 8 months. The NBER only dates turning points, it does not assign causes; it also excluded September 2001 (the 9/11 attacks) from its trough analysis (via excerpts). The burst contributed, but was not the only cause.

CAUTION"$5 trillion vanished"

The figure depends on the index and the dates: summary articles give more than $5 trillion for tech/Nasdaq stocks; CNN Money (Dec 2002) estimated about $8.5 trillion for the whole market by the Wilshire 5000. All secondary sources, with no single official number.

CAUTION"1999 IPOs doubled on day one"

The average was 70.9% across 446 deals (Jay Ritter via PBS Frontline, secondary source): very high, but not double. An average also hides the spread: a few deals that multiplied pulled the number up while many others rose little.

FALSE"The internet was just a bubble"

Stock prices were a bubble; the technology was not. Online shopping went from 0.64% of US retail (Q4 1999) to 16.1% (Q2 2020) per the Census Bureau; by the end of 2018, 51.2% of the world's population used the internet per the ITU (via excerpts).

FALSE"Greenspan warned right before the bubble burst"

The "irrational exuberance" line was on 5 Dec 1996, with the Nasdaq at about 1,300, more than three years before the peak. And it was a question, not a statement that "this is a bubble". Anyone who sold on it missed a rise of almost four times.

FALSE"Every dot-com company died"

Many died, but not all. Amazon reported a net profit of $5 million in Q4 2001, its first ever (release of 22 Jan 2002, via excerpts), though it still lost $567.3 million for 2001.

Source: as noted in each line; full list in Part 9. "Almost four times" is Mark's calculation from about 1,300 to 5,048.62.

Got right
  • Public warnings in 1996 and in March 2000
  • The Fed cut rates quickly as the economy weakened (11 times in 2001)
  • Rules fixed: revenue, disclosure, research
Got wrong
  • Warnings with no tools or metrics anyone could act on
  • Research steered by investment banking interests
  • Traffic scored as if it were money

The world was not wrong to believe in the internet. It was wrong to pay for that belief with a yardstick that was not money.

More: why a bubble is hard to see from the inside

Everyone involved had a sensible reason: banks earned fees, funds had to keep up with the index, employees were paid in stock options, and anyone who sold early looked foolish for three years.

So the useful question at work is not "is this a bubble?" but "what am I measuring with, and who benefits when I believe that number?".

Part 7

Work lesson: separate real technology from inflated prices, and measure in real money

Use it when your team assesses a new project, a supplier, a tool everyone is excited about, or your own product.

Two separate questions"Is this technology real?" and "Is this price reasonable?". A yes to the first does not answer the second.
Measure in real moneyNet revenue (the part you keep), gross margin, customers who come back and pay a second time. Views and downloads are only signals.
How many months of cash?Cash divided by monthly burn. In 2000 this simple sum showed a quarter of the surveyed companies were about to run dry.
Who benefits if you believe it?Does the person praising a project earn a fee, commission or position from it? If so, find an independent opinion too.

"A quarter": 51 of 207 companies per Barron's, 20 Mar 2000 (secondary source).

Check your own team: 5 questions

1. What metric is your team's hottest project reported in? Is that metric money?

2. Strip out views, downloads and free sign-ups: how many customers have paid and come back?

3. At the current spending rate, how many months of budget are left? Who knows that number?

4. Who is the strongest supporter, and what do they gain if the project is approved?

5. Has the team written down its stopping conditions: which metric, which threshold, which review date?

How do we know when irrational exuberance has unduly escalated asset values?Alan Greenspan, American Enterprise Institute, 5 Dec 1996 (shortened; original sentence checked via excerpts, secondary source).
