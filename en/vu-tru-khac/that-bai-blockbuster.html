Part 1

The question of the case

A company with $6.1 billion in revenue, whose online service was slowing its rival down, why did it file for bankruptcy less than four years later? Not because it failed to see Netflix coming. Because a long-term project was judged with a short-term yardstick, while the company carried debt and changed who was at the wheel.

Source: Blockbuster 10-K 2004 ($6.1 billion revenue); Netflix 10-K 2007 (growth slowed because of Blockbuster Online); Blockbuster 8-K of 23/9/2010.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

13 things often said about Blockbuster, checked against SEC filings, the companies' own releases and those of government agencies. Tap each line to see why.

5 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDDropping late fees was a large sacrifice

10-K 2005: extended viewing fees were about 14% of 2004 rental revenue. Q2 2005 release: rental revenue lost almost $140 million in the quarter, largely offset by more base rentals. Full-year 2005 rental revenue fell only 5.0%.

SOLID"No late fees" came with conditions

Per the California and New York Attorneys General (3/2005): keep a disc more than 7 days and on day 8 it became a purchase; returning it within 30 days still cost a $1.25 fee. 47 states and DC settled; Blockbuster refunded customers and paid the states $630,000.

SOLIDTotal Access slowed Netflix down

Netflix itself wrote in its 2007 10-K that subscriber growth in 2007 slowed mainly because of the rapid expansion of Blockbuster Online.

SOLIDAfter the CEO change, Total Access was reined in

Netflix 10-K 2007: in the second half of 2007 Blockbuster moved to "profitable growth", cut marketing and raised Total Access prices. Blockbuster's 2008 annual report says it positioned Total Access as a "profitable and stable business".

SOLIDDebt delivered the final push

8-K of 23/9/2010: a plan to cut debt from nearly $1 billion to about $100 million or less. The petition listed about $1.46 billion of debts against about $1.02 billion of assets (via Bloomberg and University of Tennessee College of Law). The auditor had warned in the 2008 report.

CAUTION"Blockbuster turned down buying Netflix for $50 million"

This is the account of Netflix's two founders in their books. Antioco says there were no serious talks. Some sources say the offer was for 49%, without enough evidence. There is no original document.

CAUTION"Blockbuster laughed Netflix out of the room"

The image comes from Marc Randolph's memoir (2019), repeated by the press. There is no third witness or record.

CAUTION"Netflix killed Blockbuster"

Netflix competition was real (20.01 million subscribers at the end of 2010, per its 10-K). But there were also cheap rental kiosks, streaming, debt, and internal decisions. Keyes blames the studios' payment terms; that is his view.

CAUTION"Blockbuster's CEO looked down on Netflix"

The "radar screen" line is real (Motley Fool, 10/12/2008). But he named Wal-Mart and Apple as the worries, and in 2007 the company had bought Movielink for downloads. One quote is not a whole strategy.

CAUTION"Cutting Total Access was an obvious mistake"

Easy to say in hindsight. Total Access really lost money: Q1 2007 net loss of $46.4 million. The lesson is not "spend without limit", but which yardstick to judge a long-term project by and which milestone to stop at.

FALSE"Blockbuster laughed at Netflix and did nothing"

The filings show the opposite: online rental 8/2004, no late fees 1/1/2005, Total Access 1/11/2006, Movielink bought in 2007 for $7.7 million (10-K 2005, 2006, 2007; 8-K Exhibit 99.2).

FALSE"Blockbuster had nothing online"

It had online rental with over 3 million subscribers (Q1 2007) and Movielink for downloads, being integrated into Blockbuster.com (10-K 2007). The problem was scale and persistence, not absence.

FALSE"Bankruptcy made it vanish overnight"

DISH bought the assets in 4/2011 (about $320 million), and the company stores all closed in early 2014. One franchise store in Bend, Oregon still carries the Blockbuster name (AP, 2019, secondary).

Done right
  • Saw the rival early, online rental from 2004
  • Dared to drop a fee customers hated
  • Total Access used the store advantage well
Done wrong
  • "No late fees" ads hid conditions, settled with 47 states
  • A long-term project judged on quarterly profit
  • Entered a new race while carrying heavy debt

Blockbuster did not lose because it could not see the future. It saw it, ran for it, and was ahead for one leg. It lost because it changed the yardstick halfway, before the project could turn a profit.

More: why "avoidable" is only 3/5

The in-store rental market shrinking was an industry-wide shift, not Blockbuster's alone. No decision would have kept $6.1 billion of store revenue.

What was avoidable is how it fell: a company with a large online subscriber base, which its rival admitted was a threat, could have transformed instead of entering bankruptcy with debts larger than its assets.

Source for "More": Blockbuster 10-K 2004 ($6.1 billion); Netflix 10-K 2007; bankruptcy petition via Bloomberg (secondary); the rest is Mark's commentary.

Part 6

Work lesson: don't let short-term metrics kill a long-term project

Applies to every "pay first, earn later" project: a new product, a new sales channel, training a team, a digital shift, even your own learning plan.

Two separate scorecardsJudge the old business on profit. Judge the new one on customers, retention, learning speed. Don't add them up and conclude.
Stop rules written first"Spend at most X, over Y months; if metric Z is below this level, stop." Sign it before starting, not in the middle of a bad quarter.
Leading indicatorsFind the numbers that move before profit: returning customers, churn, a slowing rival. In 2007 Netflix had written that number down.
One page of whyWhat the project is for, how it is measured, when it stops. When the boss changes, the new one reads this page before cutting.
