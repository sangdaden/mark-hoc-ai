Part 1

The question behind the myth

A number printed in a Microsoft report, put in a Time headline, then repeated on thousands of slides. Why did nobody ask how it was measured? Because it travelled under credible names, and because it matched what we already felt.

Part 2

Where the myth came from

Sources: BBC More or Less (2017); Phys.org (15 May 2015) on the Microsoft report's method; Time (14 May 2015); law.temple.edu (law reviews, secondary); Medium, "The attention span stat everyone keeps getting wrong" (archived Statistic Brain page, secondary).

Why is this number so easy to believe?

It matches a feeling. Everyone has scrolled a phone and forgotten what they just read. A number that "confirms" that feeling is rarely questioned.

It carries big names. "Microsoft" and "Time" stand in front of it. Few people read the small source line under the chart.

It makes a funny comparison. Losing to a goldfish is memorable and shareable. The older "goldfish have a 3-second memory" saying, of unknown origin, makes the comparison feel plausible.

It is precise to the second. "8 seconds" sounds measured, though nobody measured it.

What the evidence says

There is no single number for attention

attention is task-dependentBBC 2017; Fortenbaugh 2015

Goldfish remember more than we think

goldfish cognitionGee 1994; Sibeaux 2022
More: how smart are fish in general?

Culum Brown's review (Macquarie University, Animal Cognition, 2015) concludes that fish cognition often matches that of other vertebrates. In an interview he said fish can learn to escape a trap and still remember a year later. That is fish in general, not goldfish specifically.

In 2008, Adelaide schoolboy Rory Stokes trained goldfish to swim to a beacon for food and found they still remembered days later. It was a school project reported only in the news, so treat it as a fun anecdote.

Sources: C. Brown, "Fish intelligence, sentience and ethics", Animal Cognition 18(1), 2015, and an interview on Macquarie University's site (secondary); news reports on Rory Stokes, Feb 2008 (secondary).

The grain of truth: we switch screens faster

screen switchingGloria Mark, 2023

Collective attention to a topic is getting shorter

collective attentionLorenz-Spreen 2019

Even "10 minutes in a lecture" is weak

lecture attentionBradbury 2016

The claim

Students only focus for 10–15 minutes, so some institutions cut lectures to 15 minutes.

Rereading the sources

Many papers cite each other, very few primary studies. The most cited source barely discusses attention.

What varies most

The teacher. Differences between teachers are bigger than differences between formats.

Source: N. A. Bradbury, "Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more?", Advances in Physiology Education 40(4), 2016, pp. 509–513.

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, False

11 common claims about "goldfish attention", checked against the original sources. Click each line for the reasoning.

3 SOLID
3 CAUTION
5 FALSE
SOLIDMicrosoft Canada did run a real study in 2015

A survey of about 2,000 Canadians plus EEG on 112 people (Phys.org May 2015; BBC 2017). But it is a marketing report, not peer reviewed, and the 8-second figure did not come from it.

SOLIDOffice workers switch screens faster than before

In Gloria Mark's studies: about 2.5 minutes (2004) down to 47 seconds recently. That measures behaviour, not capacity.

SOLIDThe public stays on any one topic for less time

Lorenz-Spreen et al. (Nature Communications, 2019), on decades of data. This is collective attention and says nothing about individuals.

CAUTIONSocial media ruins our focus

The Microsoft report itself found heavy social media users did worse at prolonged attention but had more short "bursts of high attention". That is a correlation in one sample, not proof that social media is the cause. (Report described via Phys.org and CSO Online, secondary.)

CAUTIONStudents only focus for 10–15 minutes in a lecture

Bradbury (2016) reread the literature and found the primary data do not support this limit. Listeners do drift, but there is no "10-minute clock".

CAUTIONYoung people focus worse than older people

Fortenbaugh et al. (2015, 10,430 people) found sustained attention ability rises to about age 43, then declines. But that compares age groups at one moment; it does not show "this generation is worse than the last".

FALSEThe human attention span is 8 seconds

No study can be traced. The BBC (2017) asked the two sources Statistic Brain named (NCBI and AP); neither found anything. Psychologists do not measure "average attention span" as one number.

FALSEAttention fell from 12 seconds (2000) to 8 seconds (2013)

Same empty source. No published 2000 measurement exists to compare with.

FALSE"Microsoft proved we are worse than goldfish"

The number was printed in Microsoft's report, but credited to Statistic Brain. Microsoft's own survey results are a different matter.

FALSEGoldfish have a 9-second attention span

No study has measured this. Statistic Brain gave no source for it.

FALSEGoldfish only remember for 3 seconds

Goldfish learn feeding times (Gee 1994) and estimate distance swum (Sibeaux 2022). Nobody knows who first said "3 seconds".

  • Writing tightly for busy people is good
What is wrong and harmful
  • An unmeasured number used as fact
  • Audiences treated as "goldfish brains", content chopped up
  • Goldfish wrongly maligned

A number nobody measured passed through three credible names and became "fact" on thousands of slides. It hurts nobody's health, but it teaches us to look down on our audience.

More: why it survives being debunked

The BBC debunked the number in 2017, and many researchers and educators have written about it since. But "shorter than a goldfish" is shorter and funnier than any correction. A correction must explain "measured what, measured whom"; the myth needs one sentence.

Beware the opposite overstatement: "attention has not changed at all". Screen-switching habits and the rhythm of crowd attention really have changed. The accurate sentence is: we have no number for "the human attention span", and the way we spend attention is changing.

Part 6

Work lesson: check a viral number before it goes on a slide

For sales decks, reports to your manager, company posts and training material.

Read the small source line"Source: Statistic Brain" sits right under Microsoft's chart. One second of reading is enough to doubt.
A big name is not a source"According to Microsoft" or "Harvard" only says where it was printed. Ask: did they measure it or copy it?
Beware numbers that are too neat, too funPrecise to the second, compared with an animal, matching a common feeling: three signs to check harder.
Replace it with a true sentenceSwap "people only focus for 8 seconds" for "clients are busy; put the main point first". You do not need a fake number to write tightly.

When you get a great numberSave the link to the origin right away, not a screenshot1 minute
