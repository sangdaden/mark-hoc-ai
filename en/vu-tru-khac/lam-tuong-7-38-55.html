One-minute recap

Essence

Single wordsListeners heard one word such as "dear" or "maybe". No sentences, no conversation.
Feelings onlyThe question was "does this person like me", not "what does the message mean".
Deliberate mismatchA warm word in a cold voice. When words and tone clash, listeners trust the tone.
"55%" is a photo of a faceA still photograph, no posture, no gestures. "Body language" was added later in the retelling.
The author objectsMehrabian wrote that unless you are talking about feelings or attitudes, the equations "are not applicable".

Sources: Mehrabian and Wiener (1967), JPSP 6(1); Mehrabian and Ferris (1967), Journal of Consulting Psychology 31(3); Mehrabian, Silent Messages (1971) and his website (kaaj.com).

Part 1

The question

If words were only 7%, why can't we follow most of a foreign film without subtitles? Because 7-38-55 answers a very narrow question: when words contradict voice and face, which one do listeners believe about your feelings? It never measured how much of a message words carry.

1967Two small experimentsA single word, with voice and face deliberately at odds with it. Measured liking.
→
1971A formula with limits7% words, 38% voice, 55% face, with a warning: feelings and attitudes only.
→
TodayAn unconditional "law""93% of communication is nonverbal", on training slides everywhere.

Sources: Mehrabian and Wiener (1967); Mehrabian and Ferris (1967); Mehrabian, Silent Messages (1971); spread: Lapakko (1997, 2007).

Part 2

Where the myth came from

The claim got trimmed with each retelling. Every step lost one condition.

1/1967 · The nine-word experimentMehrabian and Wiener played nine single words, read in a liking, neutral or disliking tone. Tone overrode the meaning of the word.
6/1967 · "Maybe" and a photo of a faceMehrabian and Ferris paired a voice saying one neutral word with photos of faces. The face weighed about 3/2 as much as the voice.
1970 · BirdwhistellAnthropologist Ray Birdwhistell suggested words carry "no more than 30 to 35 percent" of social meaning. Later described as a guess.
1971 · The book Silent MessagesMehrabian combined the two results into the 7-38-55 formula for liking, with a note on its scope.
1997 · "Three Cheers for Language"David Lapakko showed the formula in textbooks on public speaking, small groups, organisational and intercultural communication.
2007 · 75 out of 79 websitesLapakko looked again: almost every site gave the formula with no qualification at all.
2025 · A humanlike robotAn experiment with the android Nikola found the same order, face then voice then words, when viewers judged emotion. The true kernel is still there.

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Careful, Wrong

11 common claims about 7-38-55, checked against the original research. Tap each line to see why.

3 SOLID
4 CAREFUL
4 WRONG
SOLIDMehrabian ran the experiments and did publish the 7-38-55 formula

Two 1967 papers (JPSP 6(1); J. Consulting Psychology 31(3)) and the formula in Silent Messages (1971). The numbers are not invented.

SOLIDWhen words contradict voice and face about feelings, listeners tend to trust voice and face

That is exactly what the two experiments measured. The 2025 android experiment (Sato et al.) found the same order, face, voice, words, when viewers judged emotion.

SOLIDWords and facial expression that don't match lower how people rate the speaker

Newcombe and Ashkanasy (2002): 537 people watched videos of 4 leaders giving feedback. Leaders were rated lowest when positive feedback came with a negative face, and highest when their affect was positive and matched the words.

CAREFUL"Body language is very important"

True for feelings and attitudes. But Krauss et al. (1981) found that when people described things naturally, verbal content was the best predictor. Importance depends on the situation; there is no fixed ratio.

CAREFUL"Tone of voice is 38%"

The number only means something inside the experiment: one neutral word, read by a stranger, recorded, paired with a photo. Nobody has measured 38% in a 20-minute presentation.

CAREFUL"About two thirds of meaning is nonverbal" (Birdwhistell)

Birdwhistell (1970) wrote that words carry "no more than 30 to 35 percent" of social meaning. Philpott (1983) noted it was a guess, not a measurement. (Secondary source: quoted on the ROLSI blog, 2024.)

CAREFUL"The 1967 experiments are outdated and worthless"

Small samples, only female students, artificial stimuli: genuinely weak. But the narrow conclusion (feelings, under mismatch) is still supported by later work. The error lies mostly in the broad reading, not in the experiments.

WRONG"Words are only 7% of the message in all communication"

The experiments measured liking, not information. Mehrabian is explicit: unless you are talking about feelings or attitudes, the formula does not apply.

WRONG"93% of communication is nonverbal"

It is just 38 + 55, carrying the same limits. Lapakko (2007) calls it an "urban legend" and found 75 of 79 websites stating it without any qualification.

WRONG"55% is body language: posture, gestures, eye contact"

Experiment 2 used photographs of faces. No posture, no hand movement, no live person. "Body language" was added in the retelling.

WRONG"According to Mehrabian, great presentations are about posture and voice, not content"

The research had nothing to do with presentations. According to Lapakko (1997, quoting a 1995 letter), Mehrabian himself said his findings are "often misquoted". (Quote via a secondary source.)

Sources: original papers in Part 8; Newcombe and Ashkanasy, The Leadership Quarterly 13(5), 2002 (abstract); Lapakko (1997, 2007).

True kernel
  • Feelings travel strongly through face and voice
  • When words and face clash, listeners trust the face
  • Saying one thing while your face says another costs trust
What's wrong
  • Turns liking into "the message"
  • Turns a face photo into "body language"
  • Makes people practise posture more than ideas

A narrow, real result retold as a broad, false law. How wrong: 4/5 rather than 5/5, because its kernel is still true.

More: why this myth lives so long

It has three things that help a claim spread: a number precise to the percent, the name of a university professor, and a surprising conclusion. It is also useful for people selling courses: if 93% is posture and voice, everyone needs more posture and voice training.

And it matches real experience. Everyone has heard an "it's fine" that clearly wasn't. That true part makes people believe the false part too.

Part 6

Work lesson: content first, then make words, voice and face say the same thing

For giving feedback, presenting, online meetings and messaging colleagues.

1-on-1 feedbackLead with the main point. If you are worried, say "I'm a bit worried about the deadline" instead of smiling it away.
PresentingSpend most of your time on the message and examples. Rehearse your voice so it doesn't contradict what you say.
Online meetings, chatLess voice and face means words must carry the feeling too. Write "I'm happy with this" or "I'm not convinced yet".
Spot a "7-38-55" slideAsk: measured what, on whom, in what situation? Share Mehrabian's own caveat.
Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable.Albert Mehrabian, on the 7-38-55 formula in Silent Messages (his website, kaaj.com).
