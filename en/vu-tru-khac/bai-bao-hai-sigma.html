Part 1

The paper's question

If a private tutor can lift an ordinary student to the level of the top of the class, is there a way to teach a whole class that gets close, at a cost society can bear?

Source: the paper's title and question, Bloom (1984), Educational Researcher 13(6). Mark's paraphrase, not a direct quote.

Part 2

The discovery story

Not one big experiment. A senior professor wrote up results from two of his students' dissertations and turned them into a question for the whole field.

1956 · A taxonomy of objectivesBenjamin S. Bloom (1913–1999), University of Chicago, is the name behind "Bloom's taxonomy" of learning objectives, from remembering to evaluating.
1968 · "Learning for Mastery"Split a course into units, quiz after each one, and let students who have not yet mastered it correct and relearn before moving on.
Early 1980s · Two dissertationsTwo of Bloom's doctoral students, Joanne Anania and Joseph Arthur Burke, compared three ways of teaching the same content.
1984 · The paper appearsBloom summarizes the two dissertations in Educational Researcher and calls it "the 2 sigma problem".
2011 to 2026 · Measured againVanLehn, then meta-analyses of randomized trials, measure tutoring again. The numbers are much smaller.

Source: education.stateuniversity.com, "B. S. Bloom (1913–1999)" (secondary source, biography, 1956 and 1968); Bloom (1984) (the two students, three conditions); VanLehn (2011); Nickow et al. (2020); Kraft, Schueler and Falken (2026).

Part 3

The paper in 3 sentences

The average tutored student scored above 98% of the regular class, about 2 standard deviations higher ("2 sigma"); about 90% of tutored students reached a level only the top 20% of the regular class reached.

The "mastery learning" class, which only added quizzes and corrections, beat about 84% of the regular class, about 1 sigma.

Tutoring every student costs too much, so the problem is to find group methods that come close, for example by combining two or three changes schools can make.

What do "sigma" and "above 98%" mean?

Sigma (σ) is the standard deviation: how widely scores in the regular class spread. If scores follow a bell curve, a student at the average plus 1 sigma beats about 84% of the class, plus 2 sigma beats about 98%.

So "2 sigma" does not mean double the score. It means an average student, once tutored, jumps to the top of the class.

Source: standard normal-distribution convention; 84% and 98% are how Bloom (1984) himself expressed it.

Part 5

Fact check: what holds and what is myth

13 things often said about the paper, checked against the paper and later research. Click each row to see why.

5 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDBloom reported tutored students beat 98% of the regular class

The paper does say so, about 2 sigma; about 90% of tutored students reached the level of the top 20% of the regular class (Bloom 1984). "Solid" means the paper says it, not that the number has been confirmed.

SOLIDMastery learning reached about 1 sigma in the same studies

Bloom reported the class with quizzes and corrections beat about 84% of the regular class (Bloom 1984).

SOLIDThe data came from two doctoral dissertations

Joanne Anania and Joseph Arthur Burke, University of Chicago, students of Bloom (Bloom 1984).

SOLIDThe paper poses a "problem", not a solution

The title: find group methods as effective as one-to-one tutoring. Bloom suggested combining two or three changes that can be made to the learner, the materials, the home and peer group, and the teacher (Bloom 1984, via summaries).

SOLIDTutoring really does improve learning

Meta-analysis of randomized trials: 0.37 standard deviations (Nickow et al. 2020), 0.288 in the 2024 version. Earlier, Cohen, Kulik and Kulik (1982), 65 evaluations of tutoring programs: about 0.40.

CAUTION"Most of the effect is the tutor"

von Hippel (2024) reread Anania's dissertation: about half the gain came from quizzes and extra feedback, which the mastery class also had (secondary source).

CAUTION"It holds for every subject and age"

Original studies: grades 4, 5 and 8, subjects such as probability and cartography, about 3 weeks, tests matched to what was taught (von Hippel 2024, secondary source). Too narrow to generalize.

CAUTION"The three-curve graph is real data"

According to von Hippel (2024), it is a hand-drawn illustration, not plotted from real scores (secondary source).

CAUTION"Tutoring software is nearly as good as a human tutor"

VanLehn (2011): human tutors about 0.79, step-based intelligent tutoring systems about 0.76 (via summaries). VanLehn himself summarized both as about 0.75. But direct comparisons are still few.

CAUTION"AI will solve the 2 sigma problem"

No evidence yet at scale. Papers That Changed How We Learn #10 looks closely at new trials of AI and learning.

FALSE"The 2 sigma effect has been replicated many times"

Later syntheses give roughly 0.3 to 0.8 (VanLehn 2011; Nickow et al. 2020). von Hippel (2024): no trial has replicated 2 sigma.

FALSE"Tutoring at scale works just as well"

Kraft, Schueler and Falken (2026): for large programs measured with independent standardized tests, pooled effects are only about a third to a half as large, falling sharply as programs grow.

FALSE"Bloom said every student needs a tutor"

The opposite: because tutoring every child costs too much, Bloom looked for whole-class methods that come close (Bloom 1984).

Source: as noted in each row; full list in Part 10.

Part 6

What the latest research says

Tutoring works. The question now: how much, and how much is left when thousands of students are served?

2020 and 2024 · Randomized trials

Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan pooled tutoring trials from preschool to grade 12: 0.37, and 0.288 in the 2024 version.

2024 · Rereading the dissertations

von Hippel: small, short studies, narrow tests; about half the gain came from quizzes and feedback. "2 sigma" is best read as an ideal ceiling, not an expectation.

2026 · At scale

Kraft, Schueler and Falken, over 250 randomized trials: the bigger the program, the smaller the effect, down to a third to a half. Still meaningful, but far from 2 sigma.

Source: Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan (2020), NBER w27476, and the 2024 version (EdWorkingPapers); von Hippel (2024), Education Next (secondary source); Kraft, Schueler and Falken (2026), Review of Educational Research, DOI 10.3102/00346543261446660 (trial counts differ between versions, 263 to 282, hence "over 250").

Part 8

Lessons: be your own "tutor" with a quiz-and-fix loop

The most solid part of the paper needs no hired help: a fast feedback loop.

Split it up, quiz after each partAfter each section, answer 5 questions without the book. Under 4 out of 5? Do not move on yet.
Study in pairs, quiz each otherEach writes 5 questions for the other. The asker points out mistakes; the answerer fixes them. The cheapest form of tutoring.Source: Cohen, Kulik and Kulik (1982) include many peer-tutoring programs, average effect about 0.40.
If you hire a tutorAsk them to question and correct more than re-explain. A good session is one where you talk and work more than the tutor.
Realistic expectationsGood tutoring typically helps by about 0.3 to 0.8 standard deviations. That is already a lot. Do not wait for a 2 sigma miracle.Source: Nickow et al. (2020); VanLehn (2011).
Check yourself: do you have a feedback loop? 5 questions

1. The last time I got something wrong, how soon did I find out: minutes, days, or at the exam?

2. For each chapter, do I have a set of questions to answer without the book?

3. When I get something wrong, do I relearn just that part, or reread the whole chapter?

4. Is there someone (a friend, teacher, family member) who could quiz me 10 minutes a week?

5. After 2 weeks, redo the questions I missed: how many are still wrong? Write it down to compare.
