Part 2

The story

Who, where, when: from a clinic in Chicago to villages around Lake Victoria.

1910 · Chicago, USAWalter Clement Noel, a dental student from the island of Grenada, has severe anaemia. Intern Ernest Irons looks at his blood and sees red cells that are "elongated and sickle-shaped". Physician James Herrick publishes the case: the first description of the disease in Western medical literature.
1949 · A "molecular disease"Linus Pauling and colleagues run haemoglobin through an electric field: patients' haemoglobin moves differently from healthy people's. For the first time a disease is traced to a specific molecule.
1954 · Kenya and UgandaBritish doctor Anthony Allison finds the sickle trait common on the coast and around Lake Victoria, where malaria is heavy, but rare in the highlands. Children with the trait carry fewer malaria parasites. He publishes in the BMJ: the sickle gene shields against malaria.
1956–1957 · One letterVernon Ingram in Cambridge compares the haemoglobin piece by piece: just one amino acid differs. At position 6 of the beta chain, glutamic acid is replaced by valine.
Today · WorldwideIn 2021 about 515,000 babies were born with the disease, most in sub-Saharan Africa. In the US, about 1 in 365 African American babies is born with it, and about 1 in 13 carries the gene.

Source: NHGRI (genome.gov), education kit "1956: Cause of disease traced to alteration"; Herrick (1910), Archives of Internal Medicine 6: 517–521; Pauling et al. (1949), Science 110; Allison (1954), BMJ 1(4857): 290–294 and Trans R Soc Trop Med Hyg 48(4): 312–318; HHMI BioInteractive (geography, secondary source); GBD 2021, Lancet Haematology 10(8); HHS Office of Minority Health, citing CDC data (1/365, 1/13).

Who gets the credit? A debate among historians

The familiar story puts Pauling at the centre. Some medical historians, such as Feldman and Tauber, call this account "romanticised": Pauling showed the haemoglobin was different, but Ingram showed where.

The disease also existed in Africa long before 1910, with its own names in many local languages. "First described" means first in Western medical literature.

Source: Feldman and Tauber, article on Project MUSE (muse.jhu.edu/article/3977); NHGRI (genome.gov).

Part 3

How the gene works

1. The HBB gene makes the beta chain of haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in red cells. In patients, one letter of the gene is changed (codon GAG becomes GTG), so at position 6 of the beta chain glutamic acid is replaced by valine. This haemoglobin is called HbS.

2. When it releases oxygen, HbS sticks together into long fibres, pulling the red cell from a soft disc into a stiff sickle. Sickled cells jam in small blood vessels, causing severe pain, and break early, so patients are anaemic.

3. Everyone has two copies of HBB. One HbS copy (AS): a carrier, usually healthy, and less likely to get severe malaria. Two copies (SS): disease. Newborns still have fetal haemoglobin, which does not join HbS fibres, so signs usually start around 5 months of age.

Part 4

Fact check: true stories and rumours

14 things people say about sickle cell, checked against medical sources. Tap a row for the reason.

7 SOLID
4 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDA child has the disease only if they inherit the sickle gene from both parents

Autosomal recessive. If both parents are carriers, each pregnancy has a 25% chance of an affected child, 50% of a carrier, 25% of neither (MedlinePlus Genetics).

SOLIDThe disease comes from one amino acid swapped in the beta chain

Valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 (NHGRI; Ingram 1956–1957). Same HBB gene as beta-thalassemia, but a different error.

SOLIDCarriers of one copy are strongly shielded against severe malaria

2012 meta-analysis: odds ratio 0.09, about one tenth of the non-carrier risk (Taylor et al. 2012).

SOLIDBabies with the disease usually show no signs in the first months

Signs usually start around 5 months of age (MedlinePlus), as fetal haemoglobin fades. That is why many countries test at birth.

SOLIDHydroxyurea cuts pain crises by nearly half

MSH trial (299 adults): median 2.5 crises per year vs 4.5 on placebo; stopped early for benefit (Charache et al. 1995, NEJM 332(20): 1317–1322).

SOLIDGene editing freed most trial participants from severe crises for at least a year

Casgevy: 29 of 31 evaluable people per FDA (93.5%); 29 of 30 in the 2024 NEJM paper (97%). The denominators differ because of different data cut-offs.

SOLIDAbout 515,000 babies were born with the disease in 2021, most in sub-Saharan Africa

GBD 2021 estimate (Lancet Haematology 2023). A modelled estimate, but the best global source available.

CAUTION"Gene therapy has cured sickle cell disease"

Results are very good, but median follow-up was about 19 months (NEJM 2024). Recipients can still pass the gene to their children, need high-dose chemotherapy beforehand, and Lyfgenia carries a warning about blood cancer. "Potentially life-changing" is more accurate than "cured".

CAUTION"The sickle gene is a good gene"

Only for people with one copy, where malaria exists. Two copies cause severe disease. It is an evolutionary trade-off, not a gift.

CAUTION"376,000 people die of sickle cell every year"

That is total related mortality; deaths coded directly are about 34,400. Both are modelled estimates with wide uncertainty (GBD 2021).

CAUTION"Carrying the gene is completely harmless"

Usually benign. Very rarely there are complications under extreme exertion or dehydration. In 2010 the US college sports body (NCAA) required athletes to be tested; the American Society of Hematology (ASH) opposed it and urged safety measures for everyone instead of screening (via news, secondary source).

FALSE"Sickle cell disease is contagious"

It is inherited, present from conception. It does not spread through contact, blood or mosquitoes (MedlinePlus).

FALSE"Carriers will eventually develop the disease"

Carrier (AS) and disease (SS) are different genotypes and stay the same for life (MedlinePlus).

FALSE"Once the FDA approves a drug, it is surely safe for the long term"

Voxelotor got accelerated approval in 2019. On 25 Sep 2024 the company withdrew it from all markets because later studies showed more vaso-occlusive crises and deaths in the treated group. The European Medicines Agency said there was no clear evidence the drug caused the deaths, but the benefits no longer outweighed the risks.

Source: as noted in each row; full list in Part 9.

Part 5

Where things stand

From an old drug to gene editing. Each milestone has a year and a source.

1995 · HydroxyureaThe MSH trial in 299 adults was stopped early because the drug cut pain crises by nearly half (median 2.5 vs 4.5 per year). It makes the body produce more fetal haemoglobin. The FDA approved it for adults in 1998.
2019 → 2024 · A lesson about new drugsVoxelotor received FDA accelerated approval in 2019, then was withdrawn from all markets by its own maker on 25 Sep 2024 after new safety data.
16 Nov 2023 · The UK goes firstThe UK medicines regulator (MHRA) approves Casgevy, the world's first approved therapy using CRISPR.
8 Dec 2023 · FDA approves two gene therapiesCasgevy cuts a "switch" so the patient's own stem cells make fetal haemoglobin again: 29 of 31 evaluable people had no severe crisis for at least 12 months. Lyfgenia adds a corrected beta gene: 28 of 32 had complete resolution of crises 6 to 18 months after treatment, with a warning about blood cancer.
2024 · Results in the NEJM44 people received Casgevy; 29 of 30 evaluable (97%) had no severe crisis for at least 12 months, with median follow-up of 19.3 months.

Source: Charache et al. (1995), NEJM 332(20): 1317–1322; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00000586; Pfizer 25 Sep 2024 and EMA (via Pharmaceutical Journal, Sickle Cell Anemia News); MHRA 16 Nov 2023 (via Pharmaceutical Technology); FDA 8 Dec 2023 (via summaries and company releases); Frangoul et al. (2024), NEJM 390(18): 1649–1662. Drug names are for reference only, not treatment suggestions.

The price, and who gets it

In the US, Casgevy is priced at about 2.2 million USD and Lyfgenia about 3.1 million USD for a one-time treatment (press, secondary source). Patients need high-dose chemotherapy to "clear space" in the bone marrow and weeks in hospital at a major centre.

Meanwhile most babies with the disease are born in sub-Saharan Africa (GBD 2021), where many are not yet tested at birth. For them, newborn screening, infection prevention and low-cost hydroxyurea may save more lives than gene therapy for years to come. This is Mark's comment from the sources above.

Label: SOLID. One letter in a gene is both a shield and a burden, and the promise of a "cure" should be read alongside follow-up time and price.

More: why was this disease neglected for so long?

The disease was described in 1910 and its mechanism understood in the 1950s, but adults had no FDA-approved drug until 1998: 88 years after Herrick's case. Most patients live in places that receive little research investment.

2023 changed that, but a question remains: what does a treatment mean if most people who need it cannot reach it?

Part 7

Lessons: five questions when you read "cured"

There will be more news about gene therapy. Five questions help you read it more calmly.

For reading and understanding, not a substitute for medical advice. This is a way to read news, not a guide to choosing treatment.

Ask "followed for how long?""No crises for 12 months" is a real result, but not yet "for life".
Ask "how many, compared with whom?"A few dozen people and no comparison group: a notable result that needs more data.
Ask "what are the risks?"Voxelotor is the example: approved, then withdrawn when more data came in.
Ask "who can get it?"A great treatment that few people can use is only half good news.
Self-check: read a headline with five questions
  1. Does the news say "cured" or "free of symptoms for X months"?
  2. How many people were treated, and how many were followed long enough?
  3. Compared with whom: placebo, an older drug, or themselves before?
  4. Are side effects and long-term risks mentioned?
  5. What does it cost, where is it done, and is it available in my country?

The list of questions is Mark's suggestion, based on sourced examples in this article (Frangoul 2024; FDA 8 Dec 2023; Pfizer 25 Sep 2024).
