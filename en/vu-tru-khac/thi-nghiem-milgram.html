Part 1

The question

How far will an ordinary person go in harming someone, just because a man in a lab coat says "please continue"? Today's answer: further than we think, but it depends heavily on the situation, and it may not be "obedience" at all.

Key concepts

The 15-volt staircase

incremental stepsMilgram 1963 · Burger 2009

The four prods

the four prodsMilgram 1963 · Burger, Girgis and Manning 2011

1 · "Please continue"

A polite invitation, used when the teacher hesitated.

2 · "The experiment requires that you continue"

An appeal to the shared goal: science, research.

3 · "It is absolutely essential that you continue"

Stronger, still resting on the experiment's purpose.

4 · "You have no other choice, you must go on"

The only bare command. In the replication, nobody who heard it carried on.

Wording from the 1963 paper. The surprising finding (Burger, Girgis and Manning, 2011): the more direct the order, the more likely people were to stop. What kept them going was the reason "for the experiment".

The script was broken

The tapes show the experimenter sometimes pushed beyond the four prods and improvised. According to Perry, one woman was prodded 26 times.

Believers stopped more

A reanalysis of 656 post-experiment questionnaires: people who believed the learner was really in pain were about 2.5 times more likely to stop.

Debriefing fell short

According to Perry, many were at first told only that the shocks were "milder than labelled", not right away that they were fake.

If many went to the end because they guessed nobody was being hurt, then 65% measures something other than "willingness to harm".

Part 4

Evidence: how far to trust it

10 things often said about the experiment, checked against current research. Click each row for the reasoning.

3 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDMany ordinary people go much further than anyone predicts

14 Yale students predicted on average that 1.2% would reach 450V; in fact 26 of 40 did (Milgram, 1963). Partial replications also find most people continue past 150V: 70% in the US (Burger, 2009), 90% reaching the top button in Poland (Doliński et al., 2017).

SOLIDCompliance varies hugely with the situation

Within Milgram's own data, the share reaching 450V ranges from 2.5% to 92.5% depending on the condition (Haslam, Loughnan and Perry, 2014). An absent order-giver, a nearby victim and refusing peers all lower it.

SOLIDMen and women comply at similar levels

The condition with 40 women also gave 65% (Milgram, 1974, compiled in Haslam et al., 2014). Burger (2009) found no significant difference between men and women.

CAUTION"Two thirds of people will obey to the end"

65% is one condition. The average across 21 conditions is 43.6% of 740 people (Haslam, Loughnan and Perry, 2014). In 12 of the 21 conditions, most participants stopped before 450V.

CAUTIONParticipants believed the shocks were real

A sizeable share had doubts. A reanalysis of 656 of Milgram's questionnaires found that those who believed were about 2.5 times more likely to stop (Perry, Brannigan, Wanner and Stam, 2020). So the "obedience" rate may be inflated.

CAUTIONPeople did it because they were "just following orders"

The bare command ("no other choice") actually made people stop (Burger, Girgis and Manning, 2011). Haslam and Reicher's reading: people continue when they identify with the experimenter's goal. A well-grounded explanation, not yet the final word.

CAUTIONPeople today obey as much as in the 1960s

Burger (2009): 70% continued past 150V, against 82.5% in Milgram's comparable condition, a difference that was not statistically significant. But every replication stops at 150V for ethical reasons, so we cannot know how far people would go.

CAUTIONParticipants suffered no lasting harm

Milgram (1964) reported that 84% said they were glad to have taken part. But the questionnaire was sent months later, and Perry (2013) shows many were not properly debriefed straight after their session.

CRITICISEDThe experiment followed one standard script

The tapes in the Yale archive show the experimenter sometimes strayed from the four prods, repeating and adding to them (Perry, 2013). The conditions are therefore not fully comparable.

CRITICISEDThe experiment fully explains collective atrocities

Milgram himself drew the link to Nazi-era crimes. Haslam and Reicher (2012) argue that real perpetrators were usually active and believed in what they did, rather than passively "following orders". A one-hour lab session cannot explain all of history.

Strengths
  • More than 20 variations, showing which situations make people stop
  • Partial replications point the same way
  • An open archive, which is what made re-checking possible
Weaknesses
  • The script was not kept
  • Many suspected the shocks were fake
  • Severe stress for participants; cannot be repeated in full

The phenomenon is real and unsettling: ordinary people can go a long way one small step at a time. But 65% is not "human nature", and "blind obedience" may not be the right explanation.

More: research ethics after Milgram

The 1963 paper itself describes many participants under obvious strain: trembling, sweating, nervous laughter. In 1964 Diana Baumrind wrote in American Psychologist that inflicting such stress was unacceptable, and that the deception could leave participants distrustful.

Milgram replied the same year, citing his questionnaire: 84% said they were glad to have taken part. Gina Perry (2013) later listened to the tapes and interviewed former participants, showing the debriefing was less complete than Milgram described.

These debates fed into modern rules: ethics board approval in advance, the right to withdraw at any time, full debriefing right after the session. That is why Burger (2009) had to stop at 150V, screen out vulnerable people, and remind participants several times that they could withdraw.

Part 6

Lesson to apply: saying "no" to a wrong request from above

The experiment does not only show that people comply easily. It also shows which situations help people stop. Use those situations.

Write your "won't do" list in advanceAltering figures, signing for someone, hiding a mistake from a client. Decide before anyone is pushing.
Ask for time, leave the roomPressure is strongest when the person giving orders is right beside you.
One ally makes a differenceWhen two others refused, only 10% went to the end.
If you are the bossThank the person who stops you. Your instruction may be someone's "15-volt step".
Most wrongdoing at work does not start with one big order, but with a small step that is "just a bit more".Mark's paraphrase of the 15-volt staircase lesson, not a direct quote from Milgram.
