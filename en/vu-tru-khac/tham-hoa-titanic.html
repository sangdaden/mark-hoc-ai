Essence

Legal, but short of seatsThe law required boats for about 962 people. Titanic had 1,178 seats and carried 2,201 people.
A rule from 1894The lifeboat scale stopped at "10,000 tons and upwards". Titanic was 46,328 tons.
Warnings that never arrivedSeveral ice messages came in that day. The clearest one reached no one on the bridge.
Boats left half emptyThe first boat carried 27 people. A 40-seat boat left with 12.

Part 1

The question

The largest ship in the world, fully certified, carrying more boats than the law required. So why did two thirds of the people aboard die? Because the rule was written for ships a quarter of her size, and the whole industry prepared only for the usual case.

1894–1911The rule stood stillThe lifeboat scale did not change for 18 years, while ships grew from 10,000 to 45,000 tons.

The wireless room is not the bridge

Amerika's message was addressed to the US Hydrographic Office, only passed through Titanic's wireless room, and was probably set aside by the operator, Phillips.

No one put it together

Each message was a fragment. No meeting, no shared ice chart on the bridge.

The habit of the trade

British court: skilled masters kept their speed in clear weather and relied on "a sharp look-out." The event "has proved the practice to be bad."

Sources: British inquiry, sections Ice Messages Received (Phillips, Amerika message), Action that should have been taken (common practice).

The Californian

Stopped in the ice, no more than 8–10 miles away. Saw eight rockets. Her wireless operator had turned in at 23:30.

The British court

Had she gone when she saw the first rocket, "she might have saved many if not all of the lives that were lost."

The US Senate

Such conduct, "whether arising from indifference or gross carelessness, is most reprehensible," and places on her commander "a grave responsibility."

Sources: British inquiry, sections The Californian and Finding of the Court; US Senate report, The Californian's Responsibility.

Part 4

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

13 things often said about Titanic, checked against the two 1912 inquiry reports. Tap each row for the reason.

5 SOLID
6 CAUTION
2 FALSE
SOLIDThe ship sank because she hit an iceberg

British finding: "Collision with an iceberg and the subsequent foundering of the ship." Damage about 300 feet long across six spaces.

SOLIDThere were many ice warnings that day, and at least one never reached the bridge

The British report lists messages from Caronia, Baltic, Amerika, Californian and Mesaba. Mesaba's, at 21:40, "does not appear to have been delivered to the Master or to any of the officers."

SOLIDBoats for only about half the people, and that was legal

1,178 seats for 2,201 people. The rule required only about 962. A Board of Trade surveyor issued the certificate to sail on the morning of departure (US report).

SOLIDMany boats left with empty seats

Boat 7 had 27 people, boat 6 had 28, boat 1 (40 seats) had 12. British court: "At least 8 boats did not carry their full loads." US report: "Only a few of the ship's lifeboats were fully loaded."

SOLIDA nearby ship saw the rockets and did not come

Both inquiries found this of the Californian. The distance is disputed: Captain Lord said 19 miles; the British court said no more than 8–10 miles.

CAUTIONTitanic was advertised as "unsinkable"

A White Star flyer said Olympic and Titanic were, "as far as it is possible to do," "designed to be unsinkable." Trade papers in 1911 called Olympic "practically unsinkable." Hedged, not a slogan; the word became famous mostly after the sinking. (Secondary: Encyclopedia Titanica; neither inquiry report uses the word in the sections read.)

CAUTIONThe band played to the very end, and the last tune was "Nearer, My God, to Thee"

Many survivors said the band played long into the sinking, and all eight musicians died. But the last tune is unknown: some remembered the hymn, wireless operator Harold Bride told the New York Times it was "Autumn," and others heard neither. (Secondary: Snopes, summarizing the accounts.)

CAUTIONThird-class passengers were locked below

The British court found "They were not unfairly treated," explaining their lower survival by quarters at the extreme ends of the ship and reluctance to leave baggage. US report: "no distinction was made" in loading boats. But the gap is real: 25% in third class against 62% in first. The British finding has long been criticized as generous.

CAUTIONBinoculars would have saved the ship

Lookout Frederick Fleet told the US Senate that with glasses they "could have seen it a bit sooner … enough to get out of the way." That is one witness's opinion, not an inquiry finding. The British court found only that the look-out was not sufficient.

CAUTIONExactly 1,517 people died

US report: 706 saved of 2,223, so 1,517 lost. British report: 711 saved of 2,201, so 1,490 lost. Passenger lists were incomplete, so every total is an estimate.

CAUTIONWhite Star chairman Ismay pushed the captain to go faster

There is no such finding in the British report. What is on record: Ismay kept Baltic's ice warning in his pocket from about 13:42 until 19:15. The "speed up" story rests on a few contested passenger accounts.

FALSEThe captain was trying to set a crossing record

Neither inquiry says so. The British report puts speed at about 22 knots, "though not at the ship's maximum," and blames a common practice of the trade: keeping speed in clear weather.

FALSEAn aristocrat bribed the crew to row his boat away

The British court called the charge against Sir Cosmo Duff Gordon (boat 1) "unfounded." But it added that had he encouraged the men to go back, they "could have saved some lives."

Sources: British inquiry, sections Finding of the Court, Ice Messages Received, The Boats, Third-Class Passengers, Conduct of Sir C. Duff Gordon and Mr. Ismay, Speed of the Ship, The Californian; US Senate report, sections 03, 06, 07 and F. Fleet's testimony (day 4). Done right afterwards
  • Two public inquiries, full transcripts kept
  • Boats based on people carried, not on tonnage
  • Wireless watch day and night, an ice patrol
Not done right
  • The British inquiry was requested by the Board of Trade and had to judge the Board's own rules
  • No one answered for a rule left unchanged for 18 years
  • SOLAS 1914 was caught by the war; only 5 countries ratified it

Once the iceberg was seen that close, the collision may not have been avoidable. But nearly 1,500 deaths on a calm sea, with empty seats in the boats, were the result of a plan made only for good days.

More: even the fix ran late

The first International Conference on Safety of Life at Sea met in London from 23 November 1913 to 20 January 1914, with 13 countries. The convention required boats and lifejackets for everyone on board, continuous radio watches, and an international ice patrol.

Then the First World War broke out. Only 5 of the 13 countries ratified: Great Britain, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain and Sweden. The SOLAS in force today is the 1974 version, in force since 25 May 1980.

Sources: UK National Archives, "The Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea"; International Maritime Organization (IMO), SOLAS 1974 page.

Part 6

Work lesson: compliant is not the same as safe

For any contingency plan: backups, incident runbooks, the plan for when a server goes down or a key person is away.

Put a date and a scale on every standardWhen was it written, for how many users, how much data? If you have doubled, review it.
Write the "no one comes" scenarioIf the vendor, the colleague and the backup system are all unavailable, what is left?
Every warning needs an ownerWho receives it, who confirms they read it, who joins the warnings into one picture.
"Everyone does it" expires after the accidentThe British court: Titanic's mistake "would without doubt be negligence" next time. Review common practice before an accident does it for you.

Source of quotation: British inquiry, section Action that should have been taken.

What was a mistake in the case of the "Titanic" would without doubt be negligence in any similar case in the future.Lord Mersey, British Wreck Commissioner's Inquiry report (30 July 1912), "Action that should have been taken".
