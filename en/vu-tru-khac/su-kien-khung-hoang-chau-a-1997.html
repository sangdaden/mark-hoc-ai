Part 1

The question

Economies praised as "miracles" in the early 1990s: why did they fall within months into their deepest recessions in decades? Not just because of a few speculators. Because too many people borrowed short term, with other people's money, to do long-term things. When the lenders asked for it back at once, nobody had enough to pay.

Source: 1998 GDP from IMF Working Paper 99/138 (Berg, 1999); the "sudden reversal of capital flows" mechanism from the IEO (2003). The answer is Mark's view.

Part 2

Timeline

From an exchange-rate decision in Bangkok to three large IMF packages in five months.

1996 · A large deficitThailand's current account deficit was about $14.7 billion, roughly 8% of GDP: the country spent more abroad than it earned, filling the gap with borrowed money.
Early 1997 · Defending the pegThe Bank of Thailand sold dollars through forward swaps to hold up the baht. Published reserves still looked intact; forward commitments reached about $23 billion and were not disclosed.
27 Jun 1997 · 16 finance companies suspended42 more on 5 August 1997, 58 in total.
2 Jul 1997 · The baht floatsIt lost nearly 17% against the dollar in offshore trading that day.
20 Aug 1997 · IMF package for ThailandA stand-by credit of SDR 2.9 billion (about $3.9 billion), drawn over 34 months.
Nov 1997 · IndonesiaThe IMF approved SDR 7.3 billion (about $10.1 billion) on 5 November. That month 16 banks were closed.
4 Dec 1997 · KoreaThe IMF approved SDR 15.5 billion, 19 times Korea's quota. With other countries and institutions, the package was about $57 to 58 billion.
Jan 1998 · Currency lowThe rupiah had lost 81% and the baht 56% since early July 1997.
21 May 1998 · Suharto resignsAfter 32 years in power, amid riots and economic crisis. Vice President Habibie took over.
1 Sep 1998 · Malaysia goes its own wayCapital controls, a peg of 3.80 ringgit to the dollar, no IMF loan. The peg was dropped in 2005.
6 May 2000 · Chiang Mai InitiativeASEAN with China, Japan and Korea set up a currency swap network to help each other in a liquidity squeeze.

How Vietnam was affected

No currency crash like the countries with open capital markets, but a clear slowdown: GDP growth of 8.15% (1997), 5.76% (1998), 4.77% (1999); exports grew 26.6% in 1997 but only 1.9% in 1998; registered FDI fell from $4.6 billion (1997) to $1.5 billion (1999).

Where sources disagree

The 5.76% for 1998 is the General Statistics Office figure as quoted by the press. An IMF table from 1999 shows only 3.5% for 1998 (an early estimate). Mark uses the official domestic figure and notes the gap.

Part 3

Five key decisions

Seen through the eyes of the time: who decided, what they knew, what they chose, and what happened. No judging with hindsight.

1 · Holding the peg with forward contracts

forward swaps · peg defenceearly 1997 to 2 Jul 1997
Who decidedBank of Thailand
What they knewThe near-fixed rate had held for over a decade. Floating would hurt firms with dollar debts. Speculators were selling baht.
What they choseSell dollars forward to support the baht, without disclosing the commitments.
What happenedBy 2 July 1997 net reserves were almost gone, forcing a float. Markets, and the IMF, could not see the real figure.

2 · Floating the baht

the float2 Jul 1997
Who decidedBank of Thailand and Ministry of Finance
What they knewUsable reserves were nearly exhausted. Finance companies had been suspended since 27 June.
What they choseDrop the basket peg and move to a managed float.
What happenedThe baht lost nearly 17% that day and 56% by January 1998. Investors began scrutinising countries that looked like Thailand.

3 · Accepting IMF packages with tightening conditions

IMF programmesAug 1997 to Dec 1997
Who decidedThe governments of Thailand, Indonesia and Korea with the IMF
What they knewDollars were needed urgently. The IMF's familiar crisis recipe was tighter budgets, higher interest rates and cleaning up weak banks.
What they choseThailand (20 Aug), Indonesia (5 Nov) and Korea (4 Dec) took stand-by credits, drawn in tranches against conditions.
What happenedRecessions were deeper than forecast. Budget targets were eased once the output collapse was clear. Still the biggest point of debate (see Fact-check).

4 · Closing 16 banks in Indonesia

bank closures · deposit guaranteeNov 1997
Who decidedThe Indonesian government, within its IMF programme
What they knewMany banks were weak. Keeping "zombie" banks alive costs money. But there was no blanket deposit guarantee yet.
What they choseClose 16 banks; small depositors were guaranteed up to 20 million rupiah, large depositors were not.
What happenedPeople withdrew money from other banks too. In January 1998 a blanket guarantee on deposits had to be given.

Source: IMF Issues Brief 00/05 (16 banks, lesson on clear deposit guarantees); Yale Program on Financial Stability (20 million rupiah limit, spreading runs, blanket guarantee in January 1998; secondary source). IMF, "Views and Commentaries", 26 Jan 1999, rejects the claim that the IMF advised closure after "a few days of quick study".

5 · Malaysia imposes capital controls, no IMF loan

capital controls1 Sep 1998
Who decidedThe Malaysian government (Prime Minister Mahathir)
What they knewThe countries with IMF loans were in deep recession. The ringgit was falling and capital was leaving.
What they choseRestrict capital outflows, peg at 3.80 ringgit to the dollar, ban offshore ringgit trading.
What happenedInvestors objected strongly at the time. The real effect is still debated. The peg was dropped in 2005.

Source: Jomo K.S. (2004), Oxford GEG Working Paper 2004/08; The Malaysian Insight, iMoney (secondary source).

General sources for this part: IMF, Boughton (2012), chapter 11; IMF press releases 97/37, 97/50, 97/55; IMF press conference, 20 Jan 1999; CNN Money, 2 Jul 1997 (secondary source); Bank of Thailand (suspensions of 27 Jun 1997).

Part 4

What the world learned

What changed after 1998: how reserves are kept, how banks are rescued, how neighbours back each other up, and how the IMF grades itself.

Real reserves, clearly disclosedThailand's biggest lesson: reserves you can see are not reserves you can use. Subtract the commitments.
Short-term debt against reservesAt end-1997 Thailand's reserves covered only 70.4% of its short-term debt. The ratio became a watched indicator.
Say the deposit guarantee firstIndonesia 1997: closing banks before depositors feel safe sets off runs across the system.
A regional netThe Chiang Mai Initiative (2000), multilateralised into a single agreement in 2010.

Source: Bank of Thailand, "Lessons learnt from the Asian Financial Crisis"; IMF Issues Brief 00/05; ASEAN+3 joint statement, 6 May 2000; ASEAN's ASEAN+3 finance cooperation page (multilateralisation in 2010).

Part 5

Fact-check: what's true and what's myth

14 things often said about the 1997 crisis, checked against IMF documents, the Bank of Thailand and clearly cited sources. Tap each row for the reasoning.

7 SOLID
3 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDThailand floated the baht on 2 July 1997, starting the chain of crises

The IMF's official history (Boughton 2012, chapter 11): the baht lost nearly 17% against the dollar in offshore trading that day. CNN Money the same day (secondary source): a move to a managed float.

SOLIDSpeculators attacked the baht

The Bank of Thailand sold dollars forward to fight selling pressure on the baht (Boughton 2012). Speculation was real; the question is why it won (next row).

FALSE"The crisis was caused by foreign speculators"

False if meant as the main cause. A current account deficit of about 8% of GDP (CRS, UNCTAD), short-term debt at 65% of foreign debt and larger than reserves (Bank of Thailand), reserves locked in forward contracts, 58 weak finance companies. In Korea and Indonesia, lenders stopped rolling over loans (IEO 2003). The best-known blaming of speculators came from Malaysian Prime Minister Mahathir in 1997 (Jomo 2004, secondary source).

SOLIDThe rupiah lost more than 80% of its value

IMF, International Capital Markets Report 1998: by January 1998 the rupiah had lost 81% against 1 July 1997. The baht lost 56%, the won 55% (from 1 October 1997).

SOLIDThe IMF package for Korea was unprecedented relative to quota

Press release 97/55: SDR 15.5 billion, 1,939% of Korea's quota. The total package with other parties was about $57 to 58 billion (CRS $58.2 billion; PIIE about $57 billion, secondary source).

SOLIDIndonesia closed 16 banks without fully guaranteeing deposits, setting off mass withdrawals

IMF Issues Brief 00/05 cites the closure of 16 banks in November 1997 as a lesson on clear deposit guarantees. Yale YPFS (secondary source): coverage only up to 20 million rupiah, runs spread across the system, blanket guarantee from January 1998. The IMF (26 Jan 1999) rejected the claim that it advised closure "after a few days of quick study", saying it agonised because more banks were known to be weak.

SOLIDThe IMF itself concluded that initial fiscal tightening in Indonesia and Korea was not needed

IEO (2003), chapter 4: "In view of output developments, the initial tightening of fiscal policy in Indonesia and Korea was not warranted." This is the IMF's own independent evaluation office.

SOLIDSuharto resigned on 21 May 1998 after 32 years in power

Britannica, IPS 21 May 1998 (secondary source): amid riots and economic crisis; Vice President Habibie took over.

CAUTION"IMF austerity caused the collapse"

The IEO says the initial tightening was not warranted, but "mild" and "not the cause of the output collapse"; the cause was balance-sheet effects the programmes did not factor in. Many economists criticise more strongly. A fair conclusion: the medicine was partly the wrong dose, but the illness came first.

CAUTION"Malaysia refused the IMF, imposed capital controls and so recovered better"

Malaysia imposed controls on 1 September 1998, after the region's worst panic had passed. Studies still disagree; there is no clean comparison. Investors objected strongly at the time (Jomo 2004; Malaysian press, secondary source).

CAUTION"On the day of the float, Thailand's net reserves were only about $2.8 billion"

The Nukul Commission's figure, but Mark could only read it via an online copy. Other secondary sources count differently (28 of 30 billion used, or 32 of 33 billion). The direction is certain: usable reserves were nearly exhausted.

FALSE"The IMF never admitted mistakes in Asia"

IMF press conference January 1999 (wrong forecasts, budgets could have been eased sooner), Issues Brief June 2000 ("not flawless"), IEO July 2003 ("not warranted"). The IMF's admissions have limits, but they exist.

FALSE"IMF packages are free money"

They are stand-by credits: drawn in tranches, each tied to conditions and reviews, then repaid. For example Thailand could draw SDR 1.2 billion at once and Korea SDR 4.1 billion, the rest on a schedule (press releases 97/37, 97/55).

FALSE"Vietnam was not affected"

GDP growth of 8.15% (1997), 5.76% (1998), 4.77% (1999); exports grew only 1.9% in 1998; registered FDI fell sharply (General Statistics Office via the press and a paper at the 2002 IMF conference, secondary source). Vietnam was hit less because its capital market was not yet open, but it was not untouched.

The illness came from the economies themselves: borrowing short, building long, hiding the real numbers. The medicine arrived fast but partly at the wrong dose. What deserves most credit is that both sides rewrote their own lessons.

More: why the "speculators did it" myth is easy to believe

A villain from outside is a neat, comfortable story: nobody at home has to own a mistake. The truth is messier: many people at home borrowed dollars short term because they were cheap, believing the exchange rate would stand still forever.

The messy version is more useful at work: most breakdowns don't come from one blow, but from short-term commitments piling up under a long-term project.

Part 7

Work lesson: don't borrow short term with other people's money to do long-term work

At work, "money" can be people borrowed from another team, a budget granted quarter by quarter, a manager's goodwill, or a supplier you don't control.

Match maturitiesA 12-month job needs its main resources committed for 12 months. Write down the expiry date of each one.
Borrowed in which "currency"?Does your team control the resource? Borrowed people and another team's tools are "dollar debt": if they change their mind, you pay many times over.
Real buffer = buffer minus promisesThe team's free time minus the things already promised but not yet on the calendar. Make that number public.
Write the float threshold in advanceDefending a deadline or a promise? Decide now: at what cost you stop defending it and tell the truth.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. List the three main resources of your biggest project. Until what date is each committed? Does any expire before the project ends?

2. If every borrowed person and temporary budget were pulled in the same week, what percentage of the project could still run?

3. Is there a commitment the team has promised but not put on the board (like an undisclosed forward contract)?

4. What "exchange rate" are you defending: a deadline, a price, a promise to a client? What does one more month of defending cost?

5. If you must "close" a workstream, have the people involved been told clearly what is still guaranteed (the 16-bank lesson)?
