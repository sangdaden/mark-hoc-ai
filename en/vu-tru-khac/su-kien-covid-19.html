Part 1

The question

The world made vaccines faster than ever before, so why were there still almost 15 million excess deaths in the first two years? Part of the answer lies in the first weeks: while data were missing, many places waited for certainty before acting.

Source: WHO, 5 May 2022 (14.9 million excess deaths in 2020–2021); Independent Panel, May 2021 report (many countries "wait and see" after 30 Jan 2020). The answer is Mark's view.

Part 3

Key decisions

Four decisions, seen through the eyes of the time: who decided, what they knew, what they chose, what happened next.

1 · Declare a global emergency yet?22–30 Jan 2020

Who decidedThe Emergency Committee under the International Health Regulations (IHR) advised; the WHO Director-General declared.
What they knew22 Jan: cases outside China, but the extent of human-to-human spread was unclear. 29 Jan: NEJM showed cases doubling every 7.4 days.
What they chose22–23 Jan: divided views, no declaration. 30 Jan: a public health emergency of international concern, the highest alarm available then.
What happenedThe Independent Panel later said it should have been declared sooner, and that after 30 Jan "too many countries took a wait-and-see approach".

2 · Close early or wait?Feb–Mar 2020

Who decidedNational and city governments.
What they knewCases growing exponentially; no specific treatment, no vaccine; the economic cost was certain to be huge.
What they choseMany places waited through February, and only in mid to late March banned gatherings, closed schools, locked down.
What happenedOne study estimated measures in 11 European countries averted about 3.1 million deaths by 4 May 2020; the figure is disputed on method. In 2020, 8.8% of global working hours were lost.

3 · Advise the public to wear masks?Apr–Jun 2020

Who decidedThe CDC (US) and WHO, each with its own guidance.
What they knewSigns that people without symptoms could spread it; no randomised trial of community masking yet; medical masks were short for hospitals.
What they chose3 Apr: CDC advised cloth face coverings in crowded places. 6 Apr: WHO said there was "no evidence yet" and promised updates. 5 Jun: WHO encouraged masks where spread was widespread.
What happenedTwo different messages caused confusion. A large trial in Bangladesh (2022) later found a real but modest effect.

4 · Build vaccines in parallelJan–Dec 2020

Who decidedCompanies, research institutes, funding governments, medicine regulators.
What they knewThe genome since 11 Jan; the mRNA platform had been researched for years, but no mRNA vaccine had ever been authorised.
What they choseOverlap the stages, manufacture before results were known, but still run phase 3 trials on tens of thousands of people.
What happened95% efficacy (43,548 people) and 94.1% (30,420 people) in trials. But rich countries vaccinated first; WHO stressed equitable access with its very first listing.

Source: Emergency Committee statement, 30 Jan 2020; Li et al., NEJM 2020; Independent Panel, May 2021 report (quotes via UN News and Al Jazeera, secondary source); Flaxman et al., Nature 2020, and the reply by Soltesz et al., Nature 2020; ILO Monitor, 25 Jan 2021 (via press, secondary source); CDC, 3 Apr 2020 (via NPR, secondary source); WHO mask guidance, 6 Apr 2020 (quote via press, secondary source) and 5 Jun 2020; Abaluck et al., Science 2022; Moderna release, 16 Mar 2020; Polack et al., NEJM 2020; Baden et al., NEJM 2021; WHO, 31 Dec 2020.

Why not judge with today's knowledge

In 2020 no one knew for sure how far the virus spread through the air, how much people without symptoms passed it on, or whether vaccines would work. Every decision was a bet. The fair question is not "did they guess right" but "did they say clearly that they were guessing, and did they correct course as they learned more".

Part 4

What the world learned

Things that really changed after the pandemic: rules, measurement, how vaccines are made.

Rules

The International Health Regulations gained a "pandemic emergency" level (amended 2024, in force 19 Sep 2025). The Pandemic Agreement was adopted on 20 May 2025.

Measurement

"Excess deaths" became a standard measure: it counts people who died of other causes when hospitals were overwhelmed. WHO published estimates for every country.

Making vaccines

From genome (11 Jan 2020) to authorisation (2 Dec 2020): 326 days. One model estimates vaccines averted 14.4 million deaths in the first year.

Source: WHO, 19 Sep 2025; WHO, 5 May 2022 and Msemburi et al., Nature 2023; Moderna release, 16 Mar 2020; Pfizer/BioNTech release, 2 Dec 2020; Watson et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases 2022 (14.4 million based on reported deaths, 19.8 million based on excess deaths; China excluded; figures via AP, secondary source).

What is not learned yet

The Independent Panel found that countries scrambled for protective equipment and medicines instead of preparing hospitals together. The model by Watson et al. estimates about 600,000 more deaths could have been averted had WHO's target of 40% vaccination coverage been met by the end of 2021.

Source: Independent Panel, May 2021 report (via press, secondary source); Watson et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases 2022 (via AP, secondary source).

Part 5

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

14 things often said about COVID-19, checked against WHO, the CDC and peer-reviewed research. Click a line to see why.

5 SOLID
5 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDWHO received a report of an unusual pneumonia cluster on 31 Dec 2019

WHO's timeline: the Wuhan Municipal Health Commission reported the cluster on 31 Dec 2019; WHO set up an incident management team on 1 Jan 2020.

SOLIDWHO declared a global emergency on 30 Jan 2020, when there were only 83 cases outside China

Emergency Committee statement, 30 Jan 2020: 7,711 confirmed cases and 170 deaths in China; 83 cases in 18 other countries. The meeting of 22–23 Jan had been divided.

SOLIDThe virus was spreading person to person from mid-December 2019

Li et al., NEJM, 29 Jan 2020, analysing the first 425 cases: evidence of spread among close contacts since mid-December 2019; cases doubling every 7.4 days.

SOLIDThe true death toll is much higher than reported

WHO estimates 14.83 million excess deaths in 2020–2021, 2.74 times the 5.42 million reported (Msemburi et al., Nature 2023). Only 52% of countries had complete death data; the rest had to be estimated.

SOLIDmRNA vaccines were tested on tens of thousands of people before authorisation

Pfizer/BioNTech: 43,548 people, 95% efficacy (Polack et al., NEJM 2020). Moderna: 30,420 people at 99 sites, 94.1% efficacy (Baden et al., NEJM 2021).

CAUTION"Vaccines saved 20 million lives in the first year"

This is a model estimate (Watson et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases 2022): 19.8 million based on excess deaths, 14.4 million based on reported deaths; China excluded. Right direction; the exact number depends on assumptions.

CAUTION"Lockdowns saved 3.1 million lives in Europe"

Flaxman et al. (Nature 2020) estimated this for 11 countries up to 4 May 2020. Soltesz et al. (Nature 2020) replied that the model cannot separate the effect of individual measures, and results change a lot if Sweden is treated separately. The measures did slow spread, but the specific figure is disputed.

CAUTION"Masks are useless" or "masks block everything"

A randomised trial in 600 Bangladeshi villages (Abaluck et al., Science 2022): symptomatic infection with antibodies fell by about 9%, more clearly among people aged 60 and over in villages given surgical masks. A real but modest effect; other reviews remain uncertain.

CAUTION"We know for sure where the virus came from"

WHO's SAGO advisory group (27 Jun 2025): the weight of available evidence suggests zoonotic spillover, directly from bats or through an intermediate host; but much of the information needed to test every hypothesis has not been provided. The WHO Director-General: all hypotheses must remain on the table, including a lab leak. No conclusion yet.

CAUTION"WHO changed its mind on masks, so WHO cannot be trusted"

The guidance of 6 Apr 2020 said there was no evidence yet and promised updates when new evidence arrived; on 5 Jun 2020 WHO updated it. Changing with evidence is how science works. But the CDC and WHO saying different things for two months really was confusing.

FALSE"COVID is just like the flu"

France: in-hospital deaths 16.9% with COVID-19 versus 5.8% with flu (Piroth et al. 2021). US: COVID-19 was the underlying cause of 350,831 deaths in 2020 and 415,399 in 2021, the third leading cause after heart disease and cancer (CDC MMWR). The CDC estimates flu causes 6,300 to 52,000 deaths a year in the US (2010–2025). The two counts differ in method (death certificates versus modelling), but the gap is far too large.

FALSE"The vaccines were rushed, so they were not tested"

Fast because stages overlapped and the genome came early, not because testing was skipped: phase 1 from 16 Mar 2020, phase 3 with 43,548 and 30,420 people and placebo control groups (NEJM).

FALSE"mRNA vaccines change your DNA"

CDC: COVID-19 vaccines do not change or interact with your DNA; the mRNA never enters the cell nucleus, where DNA is kept, and the cell breaks it down after use (CDC "Myths and Facts" page, checked via reprints, secondary source).

FALSE"The end of the emergency in May 2023 means COVID is gone"

On 5 May 2023 the WHO Director-General said ending the emergency "does not mean COVID-19 is over as a global health threat", and WHO could declare it again if the situation changed (via press, secondary source).

More: why Mark does not score individual countries

Countries differ in population, health systems and data; quick rankings easily turn into blame. This piece scores how the world system as a whole responded: alarm, sharing, coordination, correction. Those are also things any team can score for itself.

Part 7

Work lesson: decide before the data is enough

Say how sure you are, and set a date to review. For any decision that cannot wait: a launch, a process change, an incident.

Split "known" from "unknown"Write two columns before deciding. A long "unknown" column is not a reason to wait; it is a list of things to watch.
Attach a level of certaintyLow, medium or high, with one sentence on why. Listeners know how much to lean on the decision.
Set a review dateA specific date, and the signal that would change your mind. Say it in advance, and no one feels misled when you change.
Don't wait for certainty when waiting gets expensive fastWhen the thing you worry about doubles every few days (cases in 2020 doubled every 7.4 days), each week of waiting costs more than the last.

Source: Li et al., NEJM 2020 (7.4 days). The habits are Mark's suggestions.

Check your own team: 5 questions

1. The last decision your team postponed "because there isn't enough data": what does waiting gain, and what does it cost?

2. Did the last decision announcement state a level of certainty? Could readers mistake it for "100% sure"?

3. Which running decision has no review date? Put one in the calendar today.

4. Has the team written down which signal would make it change direction?

5. The last time the team changed its mind, did anyone say "because the new evidence is…", or did it change quietly?
