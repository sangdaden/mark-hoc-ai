Part 1

The question of the case

America's biggest soft-drink company tested a new taste on nearly 200,000 people, and the new taste won. Why did the launch become one of the most famous flops ever? Because they measured an easy thing very carefully, then used it to decide something else entirely.

Source: The Coca-Cola Company, New Coke history page (nearly 200,000 tasters; the company itself calls it "the most memorable marketing blunder").

Part 4

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

13 things often said about New Coke, checked against Coca-Cola's history page and other sources. Tap a line to see why.

4 SOLID
6 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDNew taste launched 23 April 1985, old taste back 11 July 1985

Coca-Cola's history page gives both dates and calls it 79 days.

SOLIDCoca-Cola taste-tested nearly 200,000 people

Coca-Cola says "nearly 200,000 consumers". History.com (secondary source) says 190,000 blind tests: a small difference, the same point.

SOLIDIn June 1985 the hotline got about 1,500 calls a day

Coca-Cola gives 1,500 a day, versus about 400 before the change. UPI (10 June 1985) also gives 1,500 a day.

SOLIDKeough: "we are not that dumb, and we are not that smart"

President Donald Keough's words on 11 July 1985, answering those who said Coca-Cola had "planned the whole thing", quoted on Coca-Cola's history page.

CAUTION"New Coke only lived 79 days"

79 days is when the old taste came back. The new one stayed on sale alongside it, was renamed Coke II in 1990 (one source says 1992) and was discontinued in North America in 2002 (secondary sources).

CAUTION"8,000 calls a day, 400,000 letters"

Sources conflict: Coca-Cola says 1,500 a day, History.com up to 8,000, UPI about 40,000 cumulative calls. No source could be found for 400,000, so this piece drops it.

CAUTION"Coca-Cola's market research was wrong"

It correctly answered "which sip tastes better". The mistake was using that answer for a different decision: dropping the old taste altogether.

CAUTION"Customers revolted because the new taste was bad"

The new taste won blind tests. Most of the reaction was about loss and attachment: the psychiatrist listening to calls compared it to grief, and Keough said the research could not measure that bond.

CAUTION"There was no warning sign"

According to books on the case (secondary sources), about 10–12% of tasters said they would be angry or quit Coca-Cola if the old taste were gone. Schindler (1992) argues the focus groups showed how far this group's feelings could spread.

CAUTION"Coca-Cola lost hundreds of millions of dollars"

No loss figure published by Coca-Cola could be found. The numbers online cite no source, so this piece uses none.

FALSE"Coca-Cola failed on purpose as a publicity stunt"

No document shows that. Keough denied it on the day of the U-turn. Testing nearly 200,000 people and dropping your flagship product is far too big a bet for a stunt. Coca-Cola did benefit after the U-turn, but benefiting is not the same as intending.

FALSE"New Coke tasted bad"

In blind tests, the new taste was preferred (Coca-Cola history page). Taste was not the problem.

FALSE"Coca-Cola never asked its customers"

They asked nearly 200,000 people. They just did not ask the right question.

Source: The Coca-Cola Company, New Coke history page; UPI 10 June 1985; History.com; Wikipedia, Mental Floss, EBSCO (secondary sources); Schindler (1992), Marketing Research.

Done right

  • Listened and reversed within 79 days
  • Admitted the mistake in public, did not blame customers
Done wrong
  • Measured taste, then decided about attachment
  • Replaced instead of adding, no way back
  • Played down a small, strongly opposed group

A rare failure with a happy ending: nobody cheated; a good measurement was used in the wrong place. And the company dared to turn back early.

More: why call it a "business failure" when the company is fine

Other pieces in this series are about companies that collapsed. New Coke is not: Coca-Cola is still standing. But this is the most common kind of failure at work: a product decision with great numbers that still goes wrong, because the numbers answer the wrong question.

The "lucky" part is the quick U-turn. Coca-Cola did not argue with its customers. It accepted that the research had missed something, and brought the old taste back while it could still be fixed.

Source for "More": The Coca-Cola Company, New Coke history page (79 days, Keough's words); the rest is Mark's commentary.

Part 6

Work lesson: measure what customers need, not only what is easy to measure

For any change to something people already rely on: an interface, a price list, a process, a report template, a dish on the menu.

Decision question first, measure secondWrite one line: "We are about to decide ___. To decide, we need to know ___." A measure that does not fill the second blank is not enough.
Test in real lifeA sip is not a can; a demo is not a week of use. Let a small group use the new version for real, labelled, for a few days.
Measure intensity, not just shareAlso ask: "If we removed the old one, what would you do?" 10% who would leave matter more than 60% who "slightly prefer" the new one.
Add first, replace laterKeep the old version running alongside for a while. Write the turn-back threshold, such as complaints per day, before you launch.
Check your own team (5 minutes)

Take the biggest change your team is about to make. Answer three questions, one line each:

1. Which number makes the whole team confident, and what question does it answer?
2. Is that really the question behind the decision?
3. If we are wrong, how and when do we turn back?

If question 2 is "not quite" or question 3 is blank, that is your team's "New Coke".

The truth is we are not that dumb, and we are not that smart.Donald Keough, President of Coca-Cola, 11 July 1985, as quoted on The Coca-Cola Company's history page.
