Part 1

The question behind the experiment

Do unfinished tasks stay in memory longer than finished ones? Today's answer: the "better memory" part is shaky, but unfinished tasks really do pull us back, and keep the mind busy until we give them a place.

Unfinished goals intrude

Across 6 studies, unfinished goals made people's minds wander while reading and hurt performance on other tasks (Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011).

Write a specific plan

Writing down what, when and where removed those effects, even though the task was still not done.

Unfinished at the weekend

59 employees, 12 weeks: weeks with more unfinished tasks meant more weekend rumination and worse sleep (Syrek et al., 2017).

The brain does not need the task done. It needs to know the task has a place and a time.

Part 4

Evidence: how far can we trust it

SOLIDAfter an interruption, people tend to go back and finish on their own

This is the Ovsiankina effect (1928). A meta-analysis of 21 publications gives a resumption rate of about 67% (Ghibellini and Meier, 2025). The authors call it "a general tendency".

SOLIDSwitching to a new task while the old one is unfinished hurts the new task

Leroy (2009) measured this in two groups of students (84 and 78) and called it "attention residue". Leroy and Glomb (2018) extended the idea across four studies. The effect is moderate and mostly measured in the lab.

CAUTIONOriginal paper: unfinished tasks recalled about 90% more

The 1.9 comes from the first 32 people, 26 of whom recalled unfinished tasks better (Zeigarnik, 1927). The result is real in the paper, but modern statistical testing did not exist then, and the experimenter sat right next to each participant.

CAUTIONThe effect is strong or weak depending on the person and the moment

Already in the original: tired people scored 0.61 (they recalled finished tasks better), ambitious people far higher. Rosenzweig (1943) found that when the interruption feels like failure, people recall finished tasks better. Plausible, but these factors have not been tested enough to make rules.

CAUTIONWriting a specific plan stops an unfinished task from nagging you

Masicampo and Baumeister (2011), 6 studies, mostly with students. Clear results that fit research on if… then… plans, but no large independent replication yet.

CAUTIONA "ready-to-resume" note before switching improves the next task

Leroy and Glomb (2018), four studies, published in Organization Science. Worth trying because it costs almost nothing, but it comes from a single team so far.

CAUTIONUnfinished work at the weekend hurts sleep

Syrek et al. (2017): 59 employees, 357 matched Friday and Monday observations over 12 weeks. The link runs through negative rumination. It is a diary study: it shows a link, not proof of cause.

CRITICISEDUnfinished tasks are always remembered better than finished ones

A meta-analysis of 38 publications using the most common measure gives a ratio of 0.99, essentially even (Ghibellini and Meier, 2025). Many reruns since the 1960s also found no difference.

CRITICISEDCliffhangers and unfinished ads make customers remember brands better "thanks to Zeigarnik"

A popular use in marketing and social media. The original experiment measured tasks you yourself left unfinished, not stories someone else left hanging. With a shaky memory effect, this is a double leap.

CRITICISEDZeigarnik came up with the study after seeing a waiter who only remembered unpaid orders

The Berlin café story is retold often, linked to Lewin. It is an illustrative anecdote, not data, and it is not part of the results in the 1927 paper. Fine to tell for fun, not to use as evidence.

Strengths
  • Good question, simple method
  • Opened a whole line of research on unfinished tasks and motivation
  • The original already reported that the effect varies by person
Weaknesses
  • Small samples, no modern statistical testing
  • Experimenter present, could sway results
  • Many reruns found nothing; pooled, it is near zero

Robustness 5/10. The famous name is attached to the weakest part (better memory), while the solid parts (wanting to resume, a mind kept busy by open tasks) are rarely told. The office lesson still works, because it rests on the solid part.

More: why it showed up then and not now

Ghibellini and Meier (2025) suggest a few reasons. Experiments in the 1920s were often one to one, the experimenter had authority, and participants (often students or colleagues) were deeply involved in the tasks. A task has to matter to the person before an interruption leaves it "hanging".

They also split results by the atmosphere of the experiment: in a relaxed setting the ratio was 1.07; in an achievement, test-like setting only 0.88. When an interruption feels like failure, people may prefer to forget the task. That fits Rosenzweig (1943).

The broader lesson: a catchy label ("the Zeigarnik effect") can outlive its data. The less famous part, Ovsiankina's, is the part that holds up.

Part 6

Lessons to apply: stopping on purpose

Unfinished tasks will pull you back and take up a corner of your mind. Don't fight that, use it: stop in the right place, and write things down so your mind can let go.

Meetings, emails, calls that cut inAsk for 60 seconds to write a "ready-to-resume" note before switching (Leroy and Glomb, 2018).
End of a meetingSettle "who does what, by when" for each open item, so nobody takes a dangling task home.
Friday afternoon15 minutes listing open tasks with a time to resume. Writing a plan reduces intrusive thoughts (Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011).
When you manage othersAvoid pulling people out of deep work. If you must, give them a minute to note where they stopped.
Always stop while it's going well, when you know what will happen next.Paraphrase of Ernest Hemingway's writing advice in "Monologue to the Maestro" (Esquire, 1935), not an exact quote. Oyama et al. (2018) call this the "Hemingway effect".
