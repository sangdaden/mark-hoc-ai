Part 1

The paper's question

Is the way of studying that feels surest, reading again and again, the way that makes memory last longest? The paper's answer: no. Closing the book and quizzing yourself lasts longer, even though it feels worse while you do it.

Source: abstract of Roediger and Karpicke (2006). The question is Mark's restatement of the paper's core idea.

Part 2

The discovery

Not a new invention, but an old, half-forgotten finding made clear again.

1917 · RecitationArthur Gates has children spend part of their study time reciting from memory instead of only reading: recalling helps memory.
1939 · Over 3,600 sixth-gradersHerbert Spitzer in Iowa has pupils read articles and take tests at different times: testing early helps them remember longer.
Decades later · ForgottenIn schools, tests were mostly seen as a way to measure, not a way to learn.
2006 · Two papers in one yearRoediger and Karpicke at Washington University in St. Louis publish this experiment and a review, "The Power of Testing Memory", calling for tests to be used as a learning tool.
2008 and 2011 · Two papers in ScienceLearning Swahili words with flashcards: repeated testing helped recall a week later, extra rereading did not. Then retrieval practice beat concept mapping, even on inference questions.

Source: Gates (1917), Archives of Psychology 40; Spitzer (1939), Journal of Educational Psychology 30(9), 641–656 (scan of the original via search results); Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Perspectives on Psychological Science 1(3); Karpicke and Roediger (2008), Science 319, 966; Karpicke and Blunt (2011), Science 331, 772–775.

Part 3

What the paper says, in 3 sentences

  1. Right after studying (5 minutes), rereading scores higher than self-testing.
  2. But after 2 days and after 1 week, people who tested themselves remember much more, and the more tests, the better.
  3. Rereaders feel more confident, so the feeling of "I know this" is a poor gauge of learning.

Part 5

Fact check: what holds and what is myth

13 things often said about the paper and the "testing effect". Click a line to see why.

6 SOLID
4 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDAfter 1 week, self-testers remember more than rereaders

Experiment 1: 56% vs 42%. Experiment 2: 61% (tested three times) vs 40% (read four times).

SOLIDRight after studying, rereading does better

The paper itself says so: with a final test after 5 minutes, rereading won. Experiment 2: 83% (read four times) vs 71% (tested three times). That is why cramming the night before still "seems" to work.

SOLIDRereaders feel more confident, yet remember less

The abstract: restudying "increased students' confidence". In Experiment 2, the read-four-times group predicted the best recall after a week and the tested-three-times group the worst: the opposite of what happened.

SOLIDIt works even without an answer key

In the 2006 study, the tests gave no feedback. Checking answers makes the effect larger (Rowland 2014, per secondary summaries). If you recall very little, open the book and compare.

SOLIDSeveral tests beat one

Experiment 2 after 1 week: three tests 61%, one test 56%, no test 40%.

SOLIDIt works in real classrooms, not just the lab

Yang et al. (2021): 222 studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.499, medium. The size depends on the comparison, feedback and question format.

CAUTION"It only works for rote memorisation"

Karpicke and Blunt (2011): retrieval practice beat concept mapping even on inference questions. But transfer to different question types or new problems does not always happen (Pan and Rickard 2018 map the conditions).

CAUTION"With complex material the effect disappears"

van Gog and Sweller (2015) argue this, based on cognitive load theory (paper #5). Karpicke and Aue (2015) reply that nobody has measured "complexity" properly and no experiment has manipulated it. Still debated.

CAUTION"It works equally well in every subject"

A 2025 maths meta-analysis: spacing helps (g = 0.28), but evidence for the testing effect in maths is not yet enough to call it robust. A 2024 analysis across 9 introductory STEM courses: spaced retrieval practice gained only about 2 percentage points.

CAUTION"The 2006 paper is large, diverse evidence"

Two experiments, 120 and 180 students from one university, two short passages (256 and 275 words). Its strength is the clean design; the breadth comes from later studies.

FALSE"Roediger and Karpicke discovered the testing effect"

Gates (1917) and Spitzer (1939, over 3,600 sixth-graders) saw it long before. The 2006 paper's contribution was a head-to-head comparison with rereading, using real prose, a week later, which brought the effect back into education.

FALSE"It only works with real, graded exams"

In the 2006 study a "test" was simply writing down what you remembered for 7 minutes, with no grade for the learner. Small, low-stakes classroom quizzes work too (Yang et al. 2021).

FALSE"Students already know self-testing is the best way to study"

A survey of 177 students (Karpicke, Butler and Roediger 2009): about 84% reread, only about 11% self-tested, and just 1% ranked self-testing as their top strategy (figures via secondary summaries). Kuhbandner and Emmerdinger (2019) re-examined it and argued the survey question may have overstated the preference for rereading.

Source: as noted in each line; full list in the Sources section.

One of the most solid results in the science of learning. The hard part is not believing it, but putting up with the feeling of "I'm doing badly" while you test yourself.

Part 8

Apply it: close the book and quiz yourself

Usable from tomorrow with anything you need to remember: lessons, books, work documents, vocabulary.

Done reading? Close itWrite everything you remember on a blank page. Finding it hard is right: that difficulty is the learning.
Turn headings into questions"The testing effect" becomes "What is the testing effect, and what's the evidence?" Then answer without looking.
Two-sided cardsQuestion on the front, answer on the back. Recall first, then flip.
Don't trust "I know this"Feeling familiar while rereading is not the same as recalling with the book shut. Trust only what you can write down.

The rules are Mark's suggestions based on Roediger and Karpicke (2006) and the meta-analyses above.

Self-check: your own two-week experiment

1. Pick two chapters (or documents) of similar length. Chapter A: read it twice. Chapter B: read once, close it and write down what you remember, then open and compare.

2. Spend the same time on each. Note your feeling: which one feels "more solid"?

3. One week later, without the book, write down what you remember from each. Count the ideas.

4. Compare with your feeling in step 2. If chapter B wins while chapter A felt more solid, you have just replicated the 2006 paper yourself.

"Prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material."Roediger and Karpicke (2006), abstract.
