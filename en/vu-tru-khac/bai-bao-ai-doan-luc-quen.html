One-minute recap

Essence

A 130-year-old ideaMemory fades along Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve (1885). Reviewing just before you forget makes it last longer.
A word's "half-life"Each word in your head has a half-life: after that much time, your chance of recalling it is 50%.
Learned from 12.9 million tracesThe model learns to predict from real data, with 45% less error than the old box system.
Measured who came backTrials with millions of learners measured whether they returned the next day, not test scores.
Better guesses, not proven better learningThe paper shows the machine predicts better. It does not show you learn the language better.

Source: Settles and Meeder (2016), §1 (Ebbinghaus), §3.3 (half-life), §4.1 and Table 2 (12.9 million traces, error), §4.3–4.4 (user experiments). The last card is Mark's reading of the paper itself.

Part 1

The paper's question

If a machine knew exactly when you were about to forget a word, it could remind you to review right then. This 2016 paper teaches a machine to make that guess, from the data of millions of real learners.

1885The forgetting curveEbbinghaus: memory fades over time; spreading practice out saves effort.
→
1960s–1980sHand-set schedulesPimsleur, the Leitner box: review gaps fixed in advance by people.
→
2016Learned schedulesSettles and Meeder: let data set the half-life of each word, for each person.

Source: Settles and Meeder (2016), §1 (Ebbinghaus 1885, algorithms from the 1960s–80s), §3.1–3.3.

Part 2

The story of the discovery

Who did it, where, why, and what was known before.

1885 · Ebbinghaus tests himselfAs the paper retells it: after a day of cramming he could recite 12-syllable sequences; spreading practice over 3 days took half as many repetitions for comparable results. More in Paper #1: The forgetting curve.
1967 and 1972 · The first schedulesPimsleur fixed a listening schedule: 5 seconds, 25 seconds, 2 minutes, 10 minutes, 1 hour... up to 2 years. Leitner used boxes of cards: right answers move a card to a box reviewed less often, wrong answers move it back.
2012 · A language app launchesBy the time of the paper, more than 150 million people had enrolled in a course on this app. At first it used a Leitner-like variant to show which words needed review.
Learners complainThe Leitner-based "strength" meters did not reflect what they had actually learned. That is why the team started the research.
Two weeks of dataBurr Settles (Duolingo) and Brendan Meeder collected 12.9 million "one person, one word, one session" traces and compared ways of predicting recall.
8/2016 · BerlinThe paper was presented at ACL, the main conference of computational linguistics. The team released the data and code.

Source: Settles and Meeder (2016): §1 (Ebbinghaus), §2 (150 million learners), §3.1–3.2 and Appendix A.2 (Pimsleur 1967, Leitner 1972, schedules), §3.2 (learner complaints), §4.1 (2 weeks, 12.9 million); first page (ACL, Berlin, 7–12 August 2016); repository github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression.

Part 3

What the paper says, in 3 sentences

1. Each word has a "half-life"Recall of a word drops along Ebbinghaus's formula; after exactly one half-life, it is 50%.
2. Data sets the half-lifeInstead of hand-picked rules, the model learns from how often you have seen a word, got it right, got it wrong, and how hard the word is.
3. Better predictions, more learners returningError falls by more than 45% versus the boxes; the final version was rolled out to all learners.

Source: Settles and Meeder (2016), abstract, §3.3–3.4, Table 2, §4.4.

Read this table carefully: 12% compared with what?

The abstract says the model "improved daily student engagement by 12%". That number comes from experiment II: the lean version (without word-specific features) versus the full version. Not versus the old boxes.

Versus the boxes (experiment I), the share of learners returning to practice fell by 7.3%. The authors read this as good news: many people used to practise only to "keep the tree gold"; with more accurate meters they took new lessons instead of pointless practice.

And "came back the next day" measures engagement, not a language test score.

Part 5

Fact check: facts and myths

14 things often said about the paper and about "the app that knows when you forget". Tap each row for the reason.

8 SOLID
4 CAUTION
2 FALSE
SOLIDPublished at ACL 2016, pp. 1848–1858

First page of the paper and the official repository README.

SOLIDThe model builds on Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve

§3.3: "Central to the theory of memory is the Ebbinghaus model", formula p = 2^(−Δ/h).

SOLIDThe data are 12.9 million learning traces over 2 weeks

§4.1. Each trace is one person, one word, one session.

SOLIDPrediction error is over 45% lower than the Leitner boxes

Table 2: 0.128 vs 0.235. A rerun on the public data found about 46% (GitHub personal project, secondary source).

SOLIDVersus the boxes, learners returning to practice fell 7.3%

Table 4, experiment I. The authors say learners stopped practising just to "keep the tree gold".

SOLIDDropping word-specific features worked better

§4.4: word features overfitted, some words decayed very fast however much people practised; the lean version won on all 3 measures.

SOLIDSpaced practice beats cramming for retention

Meta-analysis by Cepeda et al. (2006), Psychological Bulletin: 317 experiments in 184 articles. The paper itself cites it.

SOLIDData and code are public

github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression; 361 MB data file on Harvard Dataverse.

CAUTION"The machine predicts the exact moment you forget a word"

It predicts the average recall rate well. For ranking which word is more likely forgotten, AUC is only 0.538 (0.5 is a coin flip), behind the boxes at 0.542 (Table 2).

CAUTION"The new algorithm made learners 12% more engaged than the old one"

12% is experiment II, lean versus full model. Versus the old boxes, any activity rose just 0.3% (Table 4).

CAUTION"34 hours on the app equals a university semester of Spanish"

The paper repeats this (§2). The original study by Vesselinov and Grego (2012) was commissioned by the company and not peer reviewed; only 90 of 156 people stayed to the end (TechCrunch 2013 and critiques, secondary sources).

CAUTION"Newer models are far better"

Ye et al. (KDD 2022) report 12.6% over the previous best methods on 220 million logs; the 2023 extension cuts error by 64%. But this is reported by the team behind that app, without a large independent comparison.

FALSE"The paper proves the app makes you better at languages"

The paper measured two things: prediction error and next-day return rate. There was no language test in the study.

FALSE"The app uses chatbot-style AI to guess when you forget"

The model is a regression: a few counters (seen, right, wrong) plus word features, fitted by gradient methods (§3.3–3.4, Appendix A.3). No large language model is involved.

Source: as noted in each row; full list under Sources.

Part 6

What the latest research says

2006 · The foundation holdsCepeda et al. pooled 317 experiments: spaced practice beats cramming, and the longer you need to remember, the longer the best gap between reviews.
2019 · PNAS: optimising the scheduleTabibian et al. used data from the same app and report that learners who followed the algorithm's schedule remembered better than those on rule-based schedules.
2020 · Word difficultyZaidi et al. (Cambridge): word complexity is a highly informative feature that a neural network can learn.
2022–2023 · 220 million logsYe et al. (KDD 2022, IEEE TKDE 2023) built a least-effort review schedule for a vocabulary app; they report 12.6% over earlier methods, then a 64% cut in prediction error.

Source: Cepeda et al., Psychological Bulletin 132(3):354–380 (2006); Tabibian et al., PNAS 116(10):3988–3993 (2019); Zaidi et al., arXiv 2004.11327 (2020); Ye, Su, Cao, KDD 2022, pp. 4381–4390 and IEEE TKDE 2023. Papers after 2006 were read in abstract only.

In short: machines keep getting better at predicting when you forget. Whether learners actually get better, measured by an independent test, is still rarely answered.

Part 8

Lessons: use spaced-repetition apps the right way

Usable from tomorrow, with or without an app.

Review when remindedA reminder means the half-life is running out. Review then, rather than piling it all into the weekend.
Recall before flippingSay the answer in your head before you look. Tapping multiple choice too fast makes the app misjudge you.
No app: boxes 1-2-4-8Four paper boxes. Right moves a card to a less frequent box, wrong sends it back to the 1-day box. That is Leitner.
A streak is not a test scoreStreaks help you come back. Whether you really know the words needs a check outside the app.

Source: Leitner boxes from Settles and Meeder (2016), §3.2; "review before forgetting" from Cepeda et al. (2006). The habits are Mark's suggestions.

Self-check after 1 week: 3 steps

1. Write out 20 words you learned last week, hide the meanings.

2. Without the app, write each meaning and one sentence per word. Count the correct sentences.

3. Record the number. Repeat next week with 20 new words. If the number does not rise, change how you review, not just how many minutes.

Every word has a half-life. Your job is to meet it again before the half-life runs out.Mark's summary of the paper's formula, not a quotation.
