Part 1

The question

If cystic fibrosis or PKU is caused by one broken gene, is there a gene that makes us smart, tall or good at maths? Twenty years of reading genomes answer: almost never. Knowing why helps you read "gene test for talent" ads with a clear head.

Part 2

The story

Who, where, when, and in what setting.

2004 · The human genome has "only" 20,000–25,000 genesThe Human Genome Project published a near-finished sequence. Far fewer protein-coding genes than many expected. Few genes, countless traits: they must work together.
2000s · Candidate-gene seasonGroups picked a few plausible genes (dopamine, memory: DRD2, COMT, BDNF, APOE), tested them in hundreds to a few thousand people, and reported genes "linked to intelligence".
2012 · Christopher Chabris checksChabris's team (USA) took 12 published variants and tested them in three samples, about 9,800 people in total. Power calculations predicted 10–15 significant results. Actual: 1 of 32 tests. The title says it plainly: most are probably "false positives".
2017 · Ten years of genome-wide scansPeter Visscher and colleagues (University of Queensland, Australia) reviewed 10 years of GWAS: for almost every complex trait studied, many genomic loci contribute.
2018 · Intelligence: 205 lociA meta-analysis of 269,867 people found 205 loci and 1,016 genes. Only 24 loci were known before. None is an "intelligence gene".
2022 · Height: 12,111 variantsA study of 5.4 million people found 12,111 independent variants spread over about 21% of the genome. The same year, the first truly complete human genome filled the last 8%.

Source: International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2004), Nature 431: 931–945; Chabris et al. (2012), Psychological Science 23(11): 1314–1323 (12 variants, 5,571 + 1,759 + 2,441 people, 1/32 tests, 10–15 expected); Visscher et al. (2017), AJHG 101: 5–22; Savage et al. (2018), Nature Genetics 50: 912–919 (269,867 people, 205 loci, 1,016 genes, 24 loci before); Yengo et al. (2022), Nature 610: 704–712 (5.4 million people, 12,111 variants, 21%); Nurk et al. (2022), Science 376 (the missing 8%).

Context: why did single-gene hunts so often go wrong?

With small samples and genes that each add only a little, "significant" results are often luck. Studies with pretty results got published; empty results stayed in the drawer.

Chabris himself said the 2012 results do not mean genes play no role, but that intelligence probably involves thousands of variants, each very small.

Source: Chabris et al. (2012); Chabris quoted in ScienceDaily, Oct 2012 (secondary). The explanation about luck and the "drawer" is Mark's.

Part 3

How genes work, in 3 sentences

1. Many rare diseases are like a switch: one broken gene is enough to cause disease, so reading genes can find the cause.

2. Height, intelligence and education are like thousands of small dials: each variant nudges a little up or down, and only the sum makes a difference.

3. Environment (nutrition, school, family) turns the same dials, so "heritable" describes odds across a group of people, not one person's destiny.

Source: Visscher et al. (2017); Visscher, Hill and Wray (2008), Nature Reviews Genetics 9: 255–266 (heritability is a population parameter); Smedley et al. (2021). The switch and dial images are Mark's.

Part 4

Fact check: facts and myths

13 things often said about genes and intelligence, checked against the original research. Tap a row for the reason.

5 SOLID
3 CAUTION
5 FALSE
SOLIDIntelligence is partly heritable

Decades of twin studies agree (Plomin and Deary 2015, Molecular Psychiatry 20: 98–108).

SOLIDMost complex traits involve very many genes

Ten-year GWAS review (Visscher et al. 2017).

SOLIDHeight is linked to more than 12,000 genetic variants

12,111 independent variants, 5.4 million people (Yengo et al. 2022).

SOLIDHumans have only about 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes

International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2004), Nature 431: 931–945.

SOLIDSingle-gene diseases are easier to pin down than complex ones

Whole-genome sequencing diagnosed 35% versus 11% (Smedley et al. 2021).

CAUTION"Intelligence is up to 80% heritable"

Plomin and Deary (2015): about 20% in infancy, rising to "perhaps 80%" in later adulthood, mostly from twin studies in rich countries. The figure shifts with age and population.

CAUTION"A gene score tells you how well your child will do at school"

The score explains 12–16% of the variation in years of schooling across a population; comparing siblings, the direct effect is only about half (Okbay et al. 2022).

CAUTION"Gene scores work equally well for every ancestry"

Close to false: the height score explains 40% in European-ancestry people, only about 10–20% in others (Yengo et al. 2022).

FALSE"There is an intelligence gene"

205 loci, 1,016 genes, each contributing very little (Savage et al. 2018).

FALSE"The published 'intelligence genes' were confirmed"

Rechecking 12 variants: 1 of 32 tests reached the threshold (Chabris et al. 2012).

FALSE"50% heritable means half of your intelligence comes from genes"

Heritability describes differences within a group, it does not split one person in two (Visscher, Hill and Wray 2008).

FALSE"Choosing embryos by gene score gives a much smarter child"

Simulation: on average only about 2.5 IQ points, with wide error (Karavani et al. 2019, Cell). The European Society of Human Genetics (2022) called it "unproven, unethical"; the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (2024) does not recommend it.

FALSE"Genes are destiny"

Even in a single-gene disease like PKU, the right diet from birth changes the outcome completely (GeneReviews, "Phenylalanine Hydroxylase Deficiency"). For polygenic traits, environment plays an even bigger part.

Source: as noted in each row; full list in Part 9.

Part 5

Where we are today

From gene maps to a market for "choosing children".

2019 · Simulating embryo selectionShai Carmi's group (Israel) calculated that picking the top-scoring embryo over an average one gains only about 2.5 cm of height and about 2.5 IQ points, with very wide error. In large families, the child with the top height score is usually not the tallest.
2019 · In VietnamNewspapers reported services "decoding genes to find prodigies and talents" in children, and experts warning they lack scientific basis.
2022 · Two "saturated" mapsHeight: 12,111 variants, capturing nearly all the heritability from common variants in European-ancestry people. Education: 3 million people, 3,952 variants, explaining 12–16%.
2022 · Europe speaksEuropean Society of Human Genetics: using polygenic scores to select embryos is "unproven, unethical".
2024 · The US speaksAmerican College of Medical Genetics and Genomics: insufficient evidence of clinical utility; not recommended as a clinical service.

Label: SOLID. There is no "intelligence gene"; there are thousands of small ones plus environment, and they speak of odds across a group rather than one person's fate.

More: why is this piece in a "rare diseases" series?

Because it is the other side of the coin. Rare diseases often come from one gene with a big effect, so reading genes can point straight at the cause. Common traits come from thousands of genes with small effects, so reading genes only gives odds. Understand both and you can read any genetic test result correctly.

Part 7

Lesson: genes are odds, not destiny

Four questions before trusting a "gene test for talent" ad.

For reading and understanding, not a substitute for medical advice.

Count the genesA service "reads 10 talent genes"? Intelligence involves hundreds of loci, each adding very little.
Ask for the percentage"How much of the variation does it explain?" If nobody can answer, it is advertising.
Ask "tested on whom?"Scores built from European-ancestry data work much worse for other ancestries.
Think in oddsRare single-gene disease: ask a clinical geneticist. Polygenic traits: upbringing and learning are still most of the story.
Self-check: read a gene-test ad in 5 minutes

1. Find an ad for a "talent gene test" or "sports gene test".

2. Underline how many genes they read. Fewer than a few hundred for a trait like intelligence: suspicious.

3. Look for "how much variation it explains". None: write "no answer".

4. Check which country's people the original data came from.

5. Count the promises about "one child" (your child will...). Each one overstates.

The self-check is Mark's suggestion, not a study protocol.
