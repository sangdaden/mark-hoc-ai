Part 1

The paper's question

Are the two most popular ways to study, rereading and highlighting, the most effective? The paper says no. And the two most effective methods are rarely used, because while you study they feel harder.

Source: Dunlosky et al. (2013); survey by Karpicke, Butler and Roediger (2009), Memory 17(4). The "feels harder" point follows the review by Carpenter, Pan and Butler (2022), Nature Reviews Psychology.

Part 2

The discovery story

No lab, no test tubes. This is a "read everything, then grade it" paper, written for teachers, students and parents.

Before 2013 · Hundreds of experiments, scatteredThe spacing effect and the testing effect had been measured for decades but rarely reached learners. A 2006 review of spacing alone pooled 317 experiments from 184 articles.
2009 · A student surveyKarpicke, Butler and Roediger asked students how they study. Rereading came first; self-testing far less.
January 2013 · The paper appearsFive authors at four universities: John Dunlosky and Katherine Rawson (Kent State), Elizabeth Marsh (Duke), Mitchell Nathan (Wisconsin-Madison), Daniel Willingham (Virginia). Published in the APS special-topic journal, written for the public and policy makers.
Fall 2013 · A version for teachersDunlosky wrote "Strengthening the student toolbox" in American Educator, retelling the results for classroom teachers.
2021 and 2025 · Rechecked by meta-analysisDonoghue and Hattie pooled 242 studies on the same 10 methods. Mawson and Kang re-measured spacing using only real classrooms.

Source: Cepeda et al. (2006), Psychological Bulletin 132(3) (317 experiments, 184 articles); Karpicke et al. (2009); Kent State press release, January 2013 (authors, affiliations); Dunlosky (2013), American Educator 37(3), 12–21; Donoghue and Hattie (2021); Mawson and Kang (2025).

Part 3

The paper in 3 sentences

Of 10 popular study methods, only practice testing (questions, flashcards) and spaced practice (spreading review over several days) are rated "high utility".

Asking "why", explaining to yourself and interleaving problem types are rated "moderate": useful, but not yet tested enough in real classrooms.

Summarizing, highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery while reading and rereading are rated "low", including the two methods students use most.

Why did highlighting and rereading rate low?

Highlighting: people remember what they highlighted better, but they often highlight the wrong parts, and highlighting asks the brain to do little beyond reading.

Rereading: it helps a little, especially after a gap. But compared with testing yourself for the same time, it falls far behind. That relative disadvantage is its biggest strike.

Source: Dunlosky et al. (2013), sections on highlighting and rereading, checked through secondary summaries.

Part 5

Fact check: what holds and what is myth

14 things often said about the paper, checked against the paper itself and later research. Click each row to see why.

5 SOLID
6 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDThe paper grades 10 common study methods

True: practice testing, spacing, interleaving, asking "why", self-explanation, summarizing, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading (Dunlosky et al. 2013).

SOLIDOnly 2 methods are rated "high utility"

Practice testing and spaced practice, because they help learners of many ages and abilities on many kinds of tests (the paper; APS press release, January 2013).

SOLIDHighlighting is rated "low utility"

In the same group as summarizing, keyword mnemonics, imagery for text and rereading (the paper).

SOLIDLater meta-analysis still puts these two on top

Donoghue and Hattie (2021): spacing 0.85, practice testing 0.74, the highest of the 10. Note: the 0.85 mixes in lab studies (Mawson and Kang 2025).

SOLIDPractice testing works in real classrooms, not only in the lab

Yang et al. (2021), Psychological Bulletin: 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students, effect g = 0.499.

CAUTION"Highlighting is completely useless"

Meta-analysis by Ponce, Mayer and Méndez (2022), 36 articles, 85 effects: learner-generated highlighting improves memory a little (0.36) but not clearly comprehension (0.20); it helps college students (0.39), not clearly school students (0.24). Highlighting done by the instructor helps both memory and comprehension (0.44).

CAUTION"Rereading is useless"

It rated low because it is clearly worse than other methods for the same time, not because it does nothing. Donoghue and Hattie (2021) measured 0.47. Rereading after a gap helps more than rereading straight away.

CAUTION"Almost every student studies only by rereading"

2009 survey: 84% list rereading, 55% rank it number one (Karpicke et al.; the 55% via sources quoting the survey). But Kuhbandner and Emmerdinger (2019) found students mostly reread parts they did not understand, and later switch to testing themselves.

CAUTION"Summarizing is a good way to study"

The paper rates it low: summarizing helps people who already summarize well; most need fairly long training. Donoghue and Hattie (2021): 0.44, lowest of the 10 together with underlining.

CAUTION"Interleaving works for every subject"

Brunmair and Richter (2019), 59 studies: average g = 0.42, strongest for telling paintings apart (0.67), depending on how similar the materials are. The 2013 paper also rated it only "moderate".

CAUTION"Good methods help everyone equally"

The authors say plainly these methods are not a panacea, and work best for motivated students able to use them (Kent State press release). The 2021 meta-analysis found very large differences between studies.

FALSE"It was one big experiment"

The paper ran no new experiment. It is a review: it read existing studies and graded them.

FALSE"The utility rating is a measured number"

It is the five authors' judgment, based on four groups of questions. A numeric measure (a meta-analysis of all 10 methods) only arrived in 2021.

FALSE"The paper also graded learning styles: visual, auditory, kinesthetic"

Learning styles are not among the 10 methods graded. "Everyone has a learning style" has its own piece: Everyone has a learning style.

Source: as noted in each row; full list in Part 10.

Part 6

What the latest research says

The hardest question: outside the lab, in real classrooms, does it still work?

2021 · All 10 re-measured

Donoghue and Hattie: 242 studies, average effect 0.56. Spacing and testing highest; underlining and summarizing lowest (0.44) but still positive.

2022 · Why so few use them

Carpenter, Pan and Butler (Nature Reviews Psychology): spacing and testing are underused because of wrong beliefs about learning, lack of awareness, or because they run against intuition.

2025 · Real classrooms only

Mawson and Kang: spacing still helps, d = 0.54, smaller than the 0.85 that mixed in lab work. In mathematics alone (Murray et al.): 0.28.

Source: Donoghue and Hattie (2021); Carpenter, Pan and Butler (2022), Nature Reviews Psychology 1, 496–511; Mawson and Kang (2025); Murray, Horner and Göbel (2025).

Part 8

Lessons: move time from "highlight and reread" to "quiz and space out"

No need to study more hours. Just use the same hours differently.

Read once, then close the bookWrite down on a blank page everything you remember, then open the book and compare. What you got wrong is what to study. Replace the second reading with a self-quiz.
Plan reviews from the exam dateExam in one week: review after about 2 days. Exam in one year: review after about 3–5 weeks. A study of over 1,350 people found the best gap shrinks, as a share, the further away the test.Source: Cepeda et al. (2008), Psychological Science 19(11): optimal gap about 20–40% of the time to a 1-week test, 5–10% for a 1-year test.
Mix types once you know eachDo 3 problem types mixed together instead of 10 of type A then 10 of type B. It helps most when the types are easy to confuse.
If you still want to highlightHighlight little, after reading the whole passage, then turn each highlight into a question to test yourself later.
Check your own studying: 5 questions

1. Last week, how many minutes did I spend rereading, and how many quizzing myself without looking?

2. Did I study this subject in one night, or spread it over at least 3 different days?

3. If I close the book right now, how many main points of the last lesson can I write down?

4. My most recent highlight: have I turned it into a question yet?

5. After 1 week, redo 5 old questions: how many are right? Write it down to compare next time.
