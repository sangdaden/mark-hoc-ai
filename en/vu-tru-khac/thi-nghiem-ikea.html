Part 1

The question behind the experiment

Does making something with your own hands make you value it more than an identical thing someone else made? The answer: yes, moderately, but only if you finish it. And that same "valuing it more" makes us misjudge our own work.

Why we value what we make

competence · ownership · selfMochon 2012 · Sarstedt 2017 · Marsh 2018

Feeling competent

Finishing something makes us feel capable. People whose self-worth had just been affirmed valued their creations less; people whose ego had just been threatened were keener to make things themselves (Mochon, Norton and Ariely, 2012).

Feeling it is mine

An independent team in Germany replicated the effect and found psychological ownership behind it (Sarstedt, Neubert and Barth, 2017). The meta-analysis leans the same way (Pelled, 2023).

Feeling it is me

5-year-olds already show the effect; more effort does not increase it, and ownership alone does not explain it. The authors suggest our creations become linked to our self-concept (Marsh, Kanngiesser and Hood, 2018).

The three explanations do not yet agree. What they share: what we make becomes part of "me", so we see it with different eyes.

Evidence: how far can we trust it

10 common claims about the IKEA effect, checked against current research. Click each line for the reasoning.

SOLIDPeople value what they made more than an identical item made by others

All four original experiments point the same way (Norton et al., 2012). An independent team in Germany replicated it (Sarstedt, Neubert and Barth, 2017). A meta-analysis of 55 studies and 5,454 people finds a moderate effect, d = 0.57 (Pelled, 2023).

SOLIDThe effect needs completion; building then unbuilding, or not finishing, removes it

The Lego experiment (118 people) and the unfinished-box experiment (39 people) reach the same conclusion (Norton et al., 2012). This is the most important part for the workplace.

SOLIDCreators rate their own work higher than outsiders do

Origami folders thought others would bid as much as they did (Norton et al., 2012, a side group of 14). Outside the lab, creators overestimated how well their own acts would do (Berg, 2016, 339 circus professionals).

CAUTIONThe effect is as large as in the original experiments

The original samples were small (39 to 118 students) and bids were a few dozen cents. The meta-analysis finds signs of publication bias; adjusted, it is about d = 0.38 (Pelled, 2023, dissertation).

CAUTIONThe reason is feeling competent

Mochon, Norton and Ariely (2012) have supporting evidence. But Sarstedt et al. (2017) point to ownership, and Marsh et al. (2018) argue ownership is not enough. The mechanism is still debated.

CAUTIONEveryone shows it, not only DIY fans

Experiment 3 found that liking DIY did not change the effect, but with only 39 people (Norton et al., 2012). Plausible, needs more data.

CAUTIONThe effect appears from about age 5

Marsh, Kanngiesser and Hood (2018) found it in 5-year-olds. One paper, three studies; it needs replication by other teams.

CAUTIONStaff who help design a change support it more

The classic Harwood clothing factory study (Coch and French, 1948) found that groups that took part in planning new methods adapted faster. It fits the IKEA effect, but it is an old study with a simple design that did not measure the IKEA effect directly.

CRITICISEDCake mix sold better once buyers had to add an egg

A story often used to open talks on the IKEA effect. Food historian Laura Shapiro (2004) argues the role of the "egg trick" in cake mix's success is unclear, and fresh eggs may simply have made better cakes (paraphrase, not a direct quote). No experiment ever tested it.

CRITICISEDThe harder you make people work, the more they value it

The effect disappears when people fail or do not finish (Norton et al., 2012). In 5- and 6-year-olds, more effort did not strengthen the effect (Marsh et al., 2018). The condition is finishing, not suffering.

Strengths
  • Neat design: a non-customisable box, the same item compared
  • A clever control condition (build then unbuild)
  • Replicated by independent teams and meta-analysed
Weaknesses
  • Small student samples, bids of a few dozen cents
  • The workplace story is mostly inferred, rarely measured directly
  • Signs of publication bias across the literature

A real, moderate effect with a clear condition: you have to finish. Use it so your team owns the result, and remember it is fooling you too.

More: a note on Dan Ariely

Dan Ariely, one of the three authors, is a well-known behavioural economist who has also faced data questions. A 2012 study on signing before reporting, which he co-authored, was retracted by PNAS in 2021 because of problems with its data (Shu et al., 2012).

The IKEA effect is not part of the retracted study. More importantly, it has been replicated by unrelated teams (Sarstedt et al., 2017; Marsh et al., 2018) and pooled in a meta-analysis. So Mark trusts the effect because of the replications, not because of who first reported it.

The name "IKEA effect" is just the authors' memorable label, after self-assembled furniture. This piece is not linked to or sponsored by any company.

Part 6

How to apply it

Work skill: let your team build part of it so they own the result, and do not fall for your own "baby".

In meetings

Bring a plan with two parts done and leave one for the group to fill in on the spot, for example how to split the milestones. Write contributors' names on the board.

When delegating

Hand over a whole piece the person can finish, not many scraps that never add up to a result. The effect only comes with completion.

When you must drop the team's work

Don't delete it quietly. Explain why, keep what is usable, and let them do the revision. Building and then being unbuilt is the fastest way to lose commitment.

With your own ideas

Before defending one, ask: "If another team had proposed this, would I rate it this highly?" Have a peer, not your boss or yourself, rate it first.
