Part 1

The question of the event

The world spent hundreds of billions of dollars on a single date, and then nothing happened. Was the money wasted, or was it the reason nothing happened? The short answer: mostly the second, but nobody can prove it 100%, and that is exactly what makes all prevention work hard.

The answer is Mark's assessment, based on the US Senate report (2000) and the sources in Part 9. The global "hundreds of billions" is a forecast by Gartner ($300–600 billion) and IDC ($297 billion), secondary sources.

Timeline

From a memory-saving shortcut to a New Year's Eve watched hour by hour.

2 Apr 1998 · The Senate sets up a committeeThe Special Committee on the Year 2000 Technology Problem, chaired by Robert Bennett, vice-chaired by Christopher Dodd.
19 Oct 1998 · Information-sharing lawThe Year 2000 Information and Readiness Disclosure Act (P.L. 105-271): Y2K information shared in good faith is shielded from lawsuits.

Part 3

Key decisions

Seen through the eyes of the time: nobody knew how many programs would break, only that the deadline could not move.

1 · Turning advice into deadlines with consequences

FFIEC · 1997–1999

Who decided, what they knew

The bank regulators (FFIEC), May 1997. They knew the bug was real, but nobody knew how many programs in each bank used two-digit years.

What they chose

Inventory first, then dated steps: test plan (30 Jun 1998), start testing (1 Sep 1998), finish testing (30 Jun 1999), tied to examination ratings.

Outcome

By Sep 1999, 99% of federally insured institutions had tested critical systems. On 1 Jan 2000, US banking had no major disruption.

Source: FDIC FIL-50-97, FIL-98-38, FIL-98-93; FDIC/Fed documents of Sep 1999 (99%); Senate report of 29 Feb 2000 via CBS News (no serious disruption; secondary source).

2 · One person in charge, quarterly reports

Executive Order 13073 · 4 Feb 1998

Who decided, what they knew

President Clinton, Feb 1998. Less than 23 months to go. Each department fixed things its own way, and nobody had the full picture.

What they chose

A President's Council on Year 2000 Conversion, chair John Koskinen reporting directly to the President, one representative per department, OMB publishing progress each quarter.

Outcome

OMB's 11th quarterly report: federal critical systems 99.9% compliant, total spending $8.38 billion (fiscal years 1996–2000).

Source: Executive Order 13073 (American Presidency Project, Clinton White House archive); OMB 11th quarterly report (Dec 1999). Checked through excerpts.

3 · Letting people tell the truth without fear of lawsuits

Public Law 105-271 · 19 Oct 1998

Who decided, what they knew

Congress and the White House (bill sent by OMB and Koskinen on 27 Jul 1998). Companies hesitated to disclose their readiness for fear of being sued if the information proved wrong.

What they chose

A "Good Samaritan" law: Y2K information shared in good faith is shielded; a temporary antitrust exemption until 14 Jul 2001. It does not cover systems that actually fail.

Outcome

Partners and suppliers had a legal basis to exchange test results. Its exact impact has not been measured (Mark's assessment).

Source: signing statement for S. 2392, 19 Oct 1998 (American Presidency Project); Clinton White House archive; Florida Bar Journal and FindLaw (secondary sources).

4 · Preparing for fear, not just for bugs

Federal Reserve · 1999

Who decided, what they knew

The US Federal Reserve, 1999. Banks had almost finished testing, but people and markets might pull cash out of worry, even if no machine failed.

What they chose

A bigger currency order (inventory built by Aug 1999), a special liquidity facility from 1 Oct 1999 to 7 Apr 2000, and options on overnight borrowing sold to dealers.

Outcome

A New York Fed staff report (2006) concluded that the Y2K options eased bond dealers' year-end fears.

Source: Federal Reserve, Governor Kelley's speech of 29 Jul 1999; Special Liquidity Facility documents; New York Fed staff report 266 (2006). Checked through excerpts. The article does not use the figure "$50 billion in extra cash" because no source could be found.

Part 4

What the world learned

What changed after Y2K, and one lesson the world learned backwards.

Know what you haveMany organisations inventoried all their systems and software for the first time, because the FFIEC required an inventory by 30 Sep 1997.
Contingency plansThe FFIEC issued guidance on Y2K business risk (Dec 1997); "what if it breaks" became a required part.
Public-private sharingThe 1998 law showed that a legal safe harbour can get companies talking. Mark sees it as a model for later risk-information sharing.
The next bug: 2038Unix clocks counting seconds in a signed 32-bit integer overflow at 03:14:07 UTC on 19 Jan 2038. Many systems have moved to 64 bits; old embedded systems remain a risk.

Source: FDIC FIL-50-97 (inventory by 30 Sep 1997); FFIEC statement on Y2K business risk, Dec 1997, via New York Fed circular 10958; Public Law 105-271. The 2038 date is arithmetic: 2³¹ − 1 seconds after 00:00 UTC on 1 Jan 1970. The "model for later" view is Mark's.

More: the lesson the world learned backwards

The most quoted lesson after Y2K was "don't panic next time". That is the wrong lesson: there was no need to panic this time because people panicked at the right moment, two years early.

The Senate report also noted something few remember: 32 countries, including Australia, the UK, Canada and Germany, reported "no incidents" to the International Y2K Cooperation Center, even though the press reported incidents in those very countries. Part of "nothing happened" came from under-reporting.

Source: Senate report of 29 Feb 2000, quoted in Deseret News, 29 Feb 2000 (secondary source). The "learned backwards" view is Mark's.

Part 5

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

14 things often said about Y2K, checked against US government documents and clearly labelled sources. Tap a row to see why.

5 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDUS businesses and government spent about $100 billion on Y2K

US Department of Commerce (17 Nov 1999): about $100 billion over 1995–2001, about $365 per American (per-person figure via Nextgov, secondary source). Computerworld (secondary) says it could reach $114 billion.

SOLIDThe US federal government spent about $8.4 billion

OMB 11th quarterly report (Dec 1999): $8.38 billion, fiscal years 1996–2000. GAO had a different estimate of about $8.7 billion (secondary source). Different scopes, not one wrong number.

SOLIDBanks were held to deadlines with real consequences

FFIEC: critical systems tested by 30 Jun 1999; late meant "Needs Improvement", more than 30 days late could mean "Unsatisfactory" (FDIC FIL-98-93). By Sep 1999, 99% had finished.

SOLIDThe Fed prepared cash specifically for the rollover

A special liquidity facility from 1 Oct 1999 to 7 Apr 2000, priced 150 basis points above the federal funds target; a larger currency order for 1999. The often-quoted "$50 billion" could not be sourced.

SOLIDThe US passed a law so companies would dare to share Y2K information

Public Law 105-271, signed 19 Oct 1998. It shields information shared in good faith, but not liability for systems that actually fail.

CAUTION"Italy, Russia and South Korea spent little and were fine, so the US overspent"

These countries did not see more problems (National Geographic Education; Russia spent up to 100 times less than the US per Gartner via ACM TechNews; secondary sources). But dependence on computers differed, Koskinen argued US systems were more complex, and 32 countries reported "no incidents" despite press reports. No causal study exists.

CAUTION"Programmers back then were sloppy"

Memory in 1960 cost roughly a million times more than in the late 1990s; storing two digits was a sensible saving (NYRB 1999, secondary source). The mistake was that programs lived far longer than anyone expected, and nobody fixed them once memory got cheap.

CAUTION"The world spent $300–600 billion"

That is a late-1990s Gartner forecast, not a measurement. IDC estimated about $297 billion (1995–2001). Both are secondary sources.

CAUTION"Peter de Jager was the first to warn"

"Doomsday 2000" (Computerworld, 6 Sep 1993) is the best-known warning in the press. "First" is a common label, not a proven fact.

CAUTION"2038 will be another Y2K"

Signed 32-bit Unix clocks overflow at 03:14:07 UTC on 19 Jan 2038 (arithmetic). The risk is real for old systems, but many have moved to 64 bits. It is too early to say it will be as big as Y2K.

FALSE"Y2K was a hoax, nothing happened in the end"

The bug was real and was fixed at a cost of about $100 billion in the US. Even so, the Senate report still recorded more than 250 glitches in about 75 countries. "Nothing" was the result of the fixing, not proof there was no bug.

FALSE"There were no incidents on New Year's Eve"

A ground station for a US reconnaissance satellite system failed for about 2–3 hours (Defense Department briefing, 4 Jan 2000, via UPI and NYT; secondary sources). There were also problems at a nuclear power system in Ukraine and a few 911 systems in the US (Senate report via CBS News, secondary source).

FALSE"Planes would fall and power would go out worldwide"

It did not happen. The Senate report: glitches were fixed quickly and none caused serious disruption.

FALSE"Once 1 Jan 2000 passed, Y2K was over"

2000 was a leap year (divisible by 400), so 29 Feb 2000 was a second test date. In Sep 2001, Northern General Hospital in Sheffield (UK) disclosed that about 150 prenatal screening results had been miscalculated because of a Y2K bug (BBC via Slashdot, Computer Weekly; secondary sources).

Source: as noted in each row; full list in Part 9.

Done right
  • Bank deadlines with consequences, tied to examinations
  • One person in charge, progress published each quarter
  • A law that allowed sharing, a Fed that prepared for fear
Done wrong
  • Warnings from 1993, strong action only from 1997–1998
  • Many countries under-reported glitches, hard to compare
  • Not enough record of "what was avoided", so the "hoax" story spread

Y2K was one of the most successful acts of prevention of the computer age. Its biggest weakness was not the fixing but the telling: the world could not prove its own success.

More: why the "hoax" story is easy to believe

Press coverage in late 1999 included doomsday forecasts: planes falling, wide blackouts. When those did not happen, people compared the outcome with the worst forecast, not with "what if nothing had been fixed".

Some professionals had real doubts: former Pentagon CIO Paul Strassmann said "I think we have been had" (secondary source). The useful question is not "was there a bug" but "was the money well spent": many Y2K budgets also paid to replace old systems altogether.

The "More" section is Mark's commentary; Strassmann quoted from ACM TechNews, Jan 2000 (secondary source).

Part 7

Work lesson: done well, prevention looks unnecessary, so record what you avoided

For any prevention work: security, backups, testing, maintenance, workplace safety.

Inventory before asking for moneyList what could break and which items are critical. Base the budget on the list, not on the worry.
Deadlines with consequencesEach milestone has a date, an owner, and what happens if it slips.
An "avoided" logFor each bug found: date, where, who would have been hit if it were not fixed, and a rough cost.
A post-event report, even when quietHow many bugs fixed, how many slipped through, how long they took. That is the evidence for the next budget request.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. The last prevention work your team did well: does anyone outside the team know what it avoided?

2. Does the team have a list of systems, accounts and contracts about to expire? Which ones are "critical"?

3. Does the next prevention milestone have an owner and a consequence if it slips?

4. If your manager asks "there were no incidents last year, why do you still need budget?", what number can you show?

5. What is your team's "2038": an expiring certificate, an unsupported library, the only person who knows a system about to leave?
