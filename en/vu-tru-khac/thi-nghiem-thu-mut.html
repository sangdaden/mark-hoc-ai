Part 1

The question behind the experiment

Is more choice always better? The jam study said: sometimes too much makes people walk away empty-handed. Today's evidence says: that does happen, but only under certain conditions, and on average it barely shows up.

Key concepts

Choice overload

Not just jam: essays and chocolates

studies 2 and 3same paper, 2000

Jam · supermarket

6 flavors: 30% of stoppers bought. 24 flavors: 3%.

Essays · 193 students

Choosing from 6 topics: 74% handed in the extra-credit essay. From 30 topics: 60%. Essays from the 6-topic group were also graded slightly higher.

Chocolates · 134 people

Choosing from 6: more satisfied, and 48% took a box of chocolates instead of 5 dollars. Choosing from 30: only 12%.

Three settings, one direction. That's why the paper was persuasive. But all three came from one team, with moderate samples.

Four conditions that bring overload out

moderatorsChernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, 2015

When more choice doesn't hurt

expertise · categories · alternatives2008 · 2010 · 2025

The chooser knows the field

When choosers have expertise or clear preferences, more choice tends to help (Scheibehenne et al., 2010).

Groups and labels

Sorting options into categories helps unfamiliar choosers choose and feel more satisfied (Mogilner, Rudnick and Iyengar, 2008).

Two options beat one

402 doctors: with 2 or more alternatives, 62% switched; with only 1, 44%. Going beyond 2 added nothing (Altinger et al., 2025).
Part 4

Evidence: how far can we trust it

10 common claims about the experiment, checked against current research. Tap each row to see why.

2 SOLID
5 CAUTION
3 WRONG
SOLIDMore choice hurts when options are hard to compare, the task is hard, choosers are unsure what they want, and want to be done fast

Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (2015) pooled 99 observations, 7,202 people: once these four conditions are taken into account, the effect is significant. This is the best current reading.

SOLIDPeople with expertise or clear preferences are less prone to overload

In Scheibehenne et al.'s (2010) meta-analysis, expertise or prior preferences tilted results toward "more choice helps". The 2015 analysis likewise found preference uncertainty makes overload stronger.

CAUTIONThe purchase rate at the 6-jam booth was 10 times that of the 24-jam booth (30% vs 3%)

These are the original figures (Iyengar and Lepper, 2000), but from one supermarket, two Saturdays, and very few buyers (31 vs 4). Scheibehenne (2008) repeated it in a German supermarket and found no effect.

CAUTIONFewer topics means more students write the essay (74% vs 60%)

One Stanford class, 193 students, a difference just reaching significance. Plausible but not widely replicated.

CAUTIONChoosing from fewer chocolates makes people more satisfied

134 people in the lab: 48% of the 6-chocolate group took chocolates instead of cash, vs 12% of the 30-chocolate group. Greifeneder (2008, cited in the 2010 meta-analysis) repeated it with chocolates and found no difference.

CAUTIONRetirement plans with more funds get fewer participants

Iyengar, Huberman and Jiang (2004) looked at data on nearly 800,000 employees: plans with a handful of funds had higher participation than plans with ten or more. This is observational data, not an experiment, so other explanations are possible.

CAUTIONAdding an option makes people reluctant to change

Redelmeier and Shafir (1995): doctors offered 1 drug prescribed it 72% of the time; offered 2 drugs, only 53%. But a randomized trial with 402 doctors (Altinger et al., 2025) found the opposite: with 2 or more options, more doctors switched.

CRITICISEDMore choice always stops customers from buying

A meta-analysis of 63 conditions, 50 experiments, 5,036 people: average effect D = 0.02, virtually zero (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). Newer studies found the effect less often than older ones.

CRITICISEDJust cut product lines and sales will rise

This goes beyond the data. The same meta-analysis shows that more choice often helps, especially for customers who know what they want. Cutting blindly may remove exactly what customers need.

CRITICISEDThere is an optimal number, say 6 options

Scheibehenne et al. (2010) found no clear relationship between the size of the large set and the size of the effect. The number 6 was simply one experiment's display.

Strengths
  • A field experiment with real purchases
  • Three different settings pointing one way
  • Opened a research line with clear conditions
Weaknesses
  • One store, two days, very few buyers
  • Many redos found no effect
  • Turned into the rule "less is always better"

A clever experiment with a striking result that doesn't replicate reliably. Keep the question "when does more choice hurt?", drop the slogan "less is always more".

More: why the two meta-analyses seem to disagree

In 2010, Scheibehenne's team pooled every experiment they could find, published or not, and took the average: close to zero. They also found that published studies reported the effect more often, and that the newer the study, the less often it appeared. That's a familiar sign of a first result that was too good.

In 2015, Chernev's team asked a different question: not "how big on average?" but "when?". Once the four conditions entered the model, the effect showed clearly. The two results don't contradict each other: the average is near zero because most situations lack those conditions.

For people at work, the 2015 version is more useful: it tells you when to trim options and when a long list is fine.

Part 6

Lessons to apply

Work skill: presenting options to a boss or client so they can decide. These are Mark's suggestions built on the four conditions above, not results tested in offices.

Filter first, present secondYour boss needs to decide, not to relive all your research.
Many items? Group themA 30-item catalog: split into 3–4 named groups, such as "cheap", "fast", "safe".
Experts can see it allSomeone who knows what they want may do better with the full list plus filters.
People don't mind choosing. They mind choosing without knowing how to compare.Mark's paraphrase of the 2010 and 2015 meta-analyses, not a direct quote.
