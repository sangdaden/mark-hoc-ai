Core thesis

What you believe about your own ability matters more than the ability you start with, because that belief decides whether you keep learning.

Part 4

How solid is the evidence?

The 10 most-cited claims. Tap a row to see why.

4 SOLID
4 CAUTION
2 CRITICISED
SOLIDBeliefs about intelligence differ between people and can be measured

Dweck's "implicit theories of intelligence" scale is widely used and gives stable results.

SOLIDThe brain changes with learning and practice

Neuroplasticity is mainstream neuroscience. It does not mean any skill can be trained to any level.

SOLIDA one-hour intervention slightly raises grades for struggling students

US national study (Yeager et al., Nature 2019, 12,000+ students): lower-achieving students gained about 0.1 GPA points. Real, but small.

SOLIDStudents who believe intelligence can grow score higher on PISA

OECD, PISA 2018: true in most countries. This is a correlation and does not prove cause.

CAUTIONPraising "smart" makes children avoid challenges and lie about scores

The original study (Mueller & Dweck, 1998) had small samples. Li & Bates (2019) ran three replications and found no effect of praise or mindset on responses to failure.

CAUTIONBrain recordings: fixed-mindset students ignore corrective feedback

The 2006 EEG study (Mangels et al.) had a small sample and has not been replicated at scale.

CAUTIONCEO stories: Enron and Iacocca failed; Gerstner and Mulcahy succeeded

The examples were picked after the outcomes were known. Mindset is hard to separate from markets, luck and many other factors.

CAUTIONMichael Jordan was "cut from his high-school team"

As a sophomore he was placed on the junior varsity team, not cut entirely. The spirit of the story holds, but it has been exaggerated.

CRITICISEDMindset is the key to achievement

A meta-analysis of 273 studies (Sisk et al., 2018) found a correlation of only about r = 0.10 between mindset and achievement. A study of 5,600 Czech applicants (Bahník & Vranka, 2017) found almost no link.

CRITICISEDTeaching growth mindset clearly changes academic results

Average effect d ≈ 0.08 (2018), near zero after filtering for study quality and publication bias (Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023). Dweck's group responds that the effect is real for the right students in the right settings.

  • Changes how you praise children and colleagues
  • Makes failure feel less final
Weaknesses
  • Repetitive, with cherry-picked anecdotes
  • Splits readers into "fixed" and "growth"
  • Real-world effects are smaller than the tone

Keep the way it reframes failure and praise. Don't treat it as a formula for success.

More: the scientific debate

Since around 2016, growth mindset has become a familiar example in psychology's replication crisis. Large meta-analyses show effects in schools far smaller than hoped. Dweck acknowledges that many schools apply it badly (empty effort praise, posters) and calls this a "false growth mindset". Her group's 2019 national study argues the effect is small but real and cheap, especially for students who are struggling.
