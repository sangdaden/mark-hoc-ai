Part 1

The question behind the myth

Millions of parents and teachers have "seen with their own eyes" kids go wild after candy. If blind trials find nothing, what are they seeing? The answer of this whole series fits in one line: belief comes first, and the eyes follow.

Part 2

Where the myth came from

There is no single "original quote". The myth grew through a chain of real events, each retold a little off.

1973 · Paediatric allergist Benjamin FeingoldPresents at the American Medical Association meeting: dyes, artificial flavourings and salicylates cause hyperactivity. Later publishes the bestseller Why Your Child Is Hyperactive.
Mid-1970s · Sugar gets namedPaediatrician William Crook writes that cane sugar is a leading culprit. (Secondary source: a US nutrition review citing "Crook, 1975"; the original was not available.)
1979 · The "Twinkie defense"The Dan White trial in San Francisco. The defence argued depression; junk food was cited as a symptom. The press named it as if snack cakes made a man kill.
1980 · A correlational studyPrinz and colleagues: in hyperactive children, more sugar went with more destructive behaviour. The authors called it "suggestive"; many read it as "sugar causes".
1994–1995 · Blind trials and a meta-analysisWolraich (NEJM 1994), Hoover and Milich (1994), then a meta-analysis of 23 studies (JAMA 1995).
2012 · Still here"Children are less attentive after sugary drinks and snacks": 57% of UK teachers and 55% of Dutch teachers surveyed said true.

Sources: National Research Council (1994), vol. 2 (Feingold, the 1980 study); Snopes, "The Twinkie Defense" (secondary source); Prinz, Roberts and Hantman (1980); Dekker, Lee, Howard-Jones and Jolles, Frontiers in Psychology 3:429 (2012), table 1, sample of 242 teachers.

Why this myth is so easy to believe

Sugar shows up exactly when kids are already wild: birthday parties, Halloween, holidays, days off. There are also crowds of friends, games, late nights, presents. Adults remember the cake and forget the rest.

The story has a plausible mechanism: "sugar is energy, energy has to go somewhere". It sounds tidy, but the body does not work that simply, and trials do not show that result.

It gives adults a comfortable explanation: the child is not wild because he is tired, bored, or because of how we handled things, but because of the candy. The authors of the 1995 meta-analysis themselves wrote that parents' belief may come from "expectancy and common association".

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, False

10 things people say about sugar and kids, checked against the original research. Tap a row for the reasoning.

3 SOLID
4 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDIn blind trials, kids given sugar are no more hyperactive than kids given placebo

The meta-analysis of 23 studies (JAMA 1995) and the three-weeks-per-diet trial (NEJM 1994) agree. The researchers deliberately included children whose parents believed they were "sugar-sensitive".

SOLIDExpectations make adults rate and behave differently

Hoover and Milich (1994): changing only what the mother was told made her rate her son more hyperactive and shadow him more closely. The 1995 meta-analysis also points to expectancy as a likely explanation for parents' belief.

SOLIDIt is still wise to limit sugar for children

For teeth and weight, not behaviour. WHO (2015): free sugars below 10% of daily energy is a strong recommendation; below 5% is conditional.

CAUTION"Some children are especially sensitive to sugar"

The grain of truth: the JAMA 1995 authors say an effect in a small subset cannot be ruled out, and a critical letter argued averaging could hide children who react in opposite directions. But when the very children parents called sensitive were tested (NEJM 1994), there was still no difference.

CAUTION"Food can make kids hyperactive"

For artificial food colours and the preservative sodium benzoate, there is one notable double-blind trial: McCann et al. (Lancet 2007), 153 three-year-olds and 144 eight-to-nine-year-olds, found higher hyperactivity scores in some groups. Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) called the effects "limited". This is about dyes, not sugar.

CAUTION"Kids with ADHD eat more sugar"

Some observational studies find an association (Farsad-Naeimi et al., 2020), others do not (Del-Ponte et al., 2019). Even where there is one, the causal arrow may run backwards: impulsive kids snack more.

CAUTION"A killer got a lighter sentence by blaming Twinkies"

In 1979 Dan White was convicted of voluntary manslaughter rather than murder. But the defence argued diminished capacity from depression; the switch to junk food was presented as a symptom. "Twinkie defense" was a press label. A psychiatrist did say junk food could worsen depression, so the rumour did not come from nowhere. (Secondary sources: Snopes, The Straight Dope.)

FALSE"Eating sugar causes ADHD"

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder; no blind trial shows sugar causes it. The Pelotas cohort (2,924 children) found high sugar intake did not precede new ADHD cases. If your child's behaviour worries you, ask a doctor rather than just cutting candy.

FALSE"Just look at a birthday party, sugar makes kids wild"

Parties have crowds of friends, games, presents, late nights, and adults expecting the kids to go wild. Watching like this cannot separate sugar from everything else. That is why blind trials are needed.

FALSE"Sugar doesn't make kids hyper, so any amount is fine"

This "fixes" one myth with another. Sugar does not cause hyperactivity, but high free-sugar intake is linked to tooth decay and unhealthy weight gain (WHO 2015).

Sources: Wolraich et al. (JAMA 1995; NEJM 1994); Hoover and Milich (1994); McCann et al. (Lancet 370(9598), 2007); BfR, "Hyperactivity and additives" (2007); Del-Ponte et al. (2019); Farsad-Naeimi et al. (2020); WHO (2015); Snopes (secondary source).

A myth that is almost completely wrong about behaviour, yet "accidentally right" about the advice. Its best lesson is about the observer.

More: why the 1995 evidence still hasn't won

A 2012 survey in the UK and the Netherlands found more than half of teachers surveyed still believed children are less attentive after sugary snacks (57% and 55%). The myth lives on because every party produces a fresh "eyewitness proof", while nobody gets to watch a blind trial.

This is also the common thread of the whole 10-part series: the 10% brain, left brain and right brain, learning styles, 7-38-55, 21 days, 10,000 hours, the goldfish, brainstorming, memory as a video camera. Every myth survives on a story that is easier to remember than a number.

Part 6

Work lesson: we see what we expect

The mothers in the experiment were not lying. They really saw their sons as wilder. Interviewers, KPI raters and CV screeners are the same: hear "this candidate is from a big-name company" or "this employee was weak last year", and the eyes follow.

Interviews: same questions, same scaleWrite 4–6 questions on the skills you need, with what a 1, 3 and 5 answer looks like. Score right after each question.
CVs: hide the expectation-setters in round onePhoto, age, school name, referrer. Score the work sample or test first, open the profile after.
Performance reviews: an evidence logOne line a week: what was done, what came of it. At review time, score from the log, not from impressions or last year's rating.
Panels: write scores before anyone speaksThe first speaker, especially the boss, sets the "expectation" for the whole room.
The question to ask before rating anyone: if I didn't know this about them, would I score them differently?Drawn from the Hoover and Milich experiment (1994).
