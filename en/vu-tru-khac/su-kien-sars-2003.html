Part 1

The question of the event

How did a country with limited health resources in 2003 become the first to contain SARS? Not by luck. Because people on the front line raised the alarm early, contained it at once, and the authorities chose to tell the truth.

Source: WHO, 28 Apr 2003 (first country to contain; snippet, secondary). The answer is Mark's view, based on WHO's credit to rapid detection, isolation and government commitment (UN News, Apr 2003, secondary).

Part 3

Five key decisions

Seen through the eyes of the time: the decision-makers knew only what is in the "what they knew" column. Nobody knew the cause until 16 April.

1 · Raise the alarm before knowing what it is

28 Feb 2003
Who decidedDr Carlo Urbani, WHO office in Hanoi
What they knewOne patient with severe pneumonia of unknown cause, recently in Hong Kong. Since 11 Feb WHO had posted news of a respiratory outbreak in Guangdong.
What they choseReport to WHO at once, without waiting for test results.
ConsequenceWHO had an early signal; on 12 Mar it issued a global alert.

Source: MSF (28 Feb; secondary); WHO 28 Mar 2018, WHO 11–12 Feb 2003, WHO 12 Mar 2003 (snippet, secondary).

2 · Contain the hospital

9 Mar 2003
Who decidedVietnamese health authorities, with the staff of the French Hospital
What they knewStaff caring for the patient were falling ill with fever and cough one after another. No drug, no test.
What they choseQuarantine the whole hospital. Staff stayed inside to care for patients.
ConsequenceMost cases in Viet Nam were hospital staff; those who stayed took a very large risk.

Source: Fleury et al., Médecine Tropicale 2003 (quarantine 9 Mar); VOA 28 Apr 2003 (staff stayed inside, per a WHO spokesman); MSF (a high proportion of cases were hospital staff) (all secondary).

3 · Tell the truth and ask for help

9 Mar 2003 onward
Who decidedThe Government of Viet Nam, after meeting WHO
What they knewA strange disease was spreading in a hospital in the capital. Announcing it could worry visitors and investors.
What they choseFight the outbreak openly and aggressively: a task force, centralised information, international experts, patients moved to Bach Mai Hospital.
ConsequenceWHO and foreign experts arrived early; the WHO team lead named speed, leadership and transparency as key.

Source: article "Halt of SARS in Vietnam Could Hold Lessons for Other Nations", May 2003, reprinted on the website of the Embassy of Viet Nam in the US (meeting on 9 Mar, Aileen Plant's words); MSF (Bach Mai) (all secondary).

4 · Warn the world without an answer

12 and 15 Mar 2003
Who decidedWHO
What they knewClusters of strange pneumonia in Hanoi and Hong Kong, many health workers ill. Cause unknown, no link found to bird flu.
What they chose12 Mar: global alert, advise isolating patients. 15 Mar: emergency travel advisory, name SARS.
ConsequenceCountries began looking for cases; a laboratory network set up after the alert found the virus on 16 Apr.

Source: WHO 12 Mar 2003, WHO 16 Apr 2003 (snippet, secondary); advisory of 15 Mar 2003 on ScienceDaily; Bulletin of the WHO 2003 (laboratory network set up after 12 Mar) (secondary).

5 · Declare only when the criteria are met

8 to 28 Apr 2003
Who decidedWHO, with Viet Nam
What they knewNo new case since 8 Apr. Maximum incubation estimated at about 10 days. Elsewhere the outbreak was still growing.
What they choseWait a full 20 days, twice the incubation period, before confirming; stay alert afterwards.
ConsequenceViet Nam was confirmed on 28 Apr; WHO warned a single imported case could restart an outbreak.

Source: WHO, "Viet Nam SARS-free" and outbreak news of 28 Apr 2003 (snippet, secondary).

Part 4

What the world learned

SARS was the first new infectious disease of global reach in the 21st century. What it left behind is still in use today.

Report by risk, not by listIHR 2005: report unusual events even before you know the disease. Just as Hanoi did in 2003.
Laboratories working together13 labs in 10 countries shared data almost daily and found the virus about a month after the alert.
Protect the front lineAbout 21% of cases worldwide were health workers. Hospital infection control became a priority.
A clear end criterion20 days with no new case (twice the incubation period): everyone knew where the finish line was.

Source: WHO 16 Apr 2003 (13 labs, 10 countries), WHO 28 Apr 2003 (20 days) (snippet, secondary); PMC review on the laboratory network (near-daily exchange; secondary); 1,706 of 8,096 cases per a Springer chapter citing WHO (secondary). "About a month" is Mark's count from 12 Mar to 16 Apr 2003.

More: how the Hanoi story has been told since 2003

WHO calls Urbani a hero who fought SARS and commemorated him in 2018 and 2023. The WHO team lead in Hanoi in 2003, Aileen Plant, described Viet Nam's success as "real, old-fashioned infectious disease containment", not a miracle.

When COVID-19 arrived in 2020, Vietnamese media retold the story of the French Hospital and the health workers who stayed inside.

Source: WHO 28 Mar 2018 and 29 Mar 2023 (snippet, secondary); Aileen Plant in the article "Halt of SARS in Vietnam…", May 2003 (secondary); Tuoi Tre News, 28 Mar 2020 (secondary).

Part 5

Fact-check: what is true and what is myth

14 things often said about SARS in Viet Nam, checked against WHO and clearly cited sources. Tap each line for the reason.

6 SOLID
4 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDViet Nam was the first country to contain SARS

WHO removed Viet Nam from the list of affected areas on 28 Apr 2003, "the first country to successfully contain" the outbreak (WHO 28 Apr 2003; snippet, secondary). Viet Nam was one of the first 4 countries WHO identified with local transmission on 15 Mar (UN News, secondary).

SOLIDViet Nam had 63 cases and 5 deaths

WHO 25, 26 and 28 Apr 2003: 63 cases, 5 deaths, no new case after 8 Apr (snippet, secondary). One 2003 news article said 68 cases; Mark follows WHO.

SOLIDDr Carlo Urbani alerted WHO to the new disease in Hanoi

WHO 28 Mar 2018: he alerted WHO headquarters in Geneva. MSF: he examined the patient on 28 Feb 2003 and is "widely credited" as the first WHO officer to identify the disease (secondary).

SOLIDWorldwide there were more than 8,000 cases and 774 deaths

WHO summary table (data as of 31 Dec 2003): 8,096 cases, 774 deaths, 29 countries and areas. An earlier version (CDC's MMWR) gives 8,098 cases. The two differ because of data updates.

SOLIDThe French Hospital was quarantined to stop the outbreak

Vietnamese health authorities quarantined the hospital on 9 Mar 2003 (Fleury et al., Médecine Tropicale 2003, secondary). According to a WHO spokesman, staff stayed inside (VOA 28 Apr 2003, secondary).

SOLIDMost cases in Viet Nam were health workers

MSF: before 8 Apr there were 63 cases, "a high proportion" of them French Hospital staff (secondary). The exact number conflicts: 37 (Tuoi Tre) or 39 (Luat su Viet Nam), see the chart above.

CAUTION"Dr Urbani discovered the SARS virus"

He recognised a new disease and raised the alarm. The virus was identified by WHO's network of 13 laboratories in 10 countries on 16 Apr 2003, after his death (WHO 16 Apr 2003; snippet, secondary).

CAUTION"The first patient came to Hanoi because his plane made an emergency landing"

Found only on a few summary pages with no original source. WHO 12 Mar 2003 says only that he was admitted after travelling from Shanghai and Hong Kong. Not enough basis to tell this detail.

CAUTION"SARS disappeared completely in July 2003"

WHO declared containment on 5 Jul 2003 but said clearly it was "not the end". Laboratory infections followed: Singapore Sep 2003, Taiwan Dec 2003, Beijing Mar–Apr 2004 (WHO; snippet, secondary).

CAUTION"One in ten people with SARS died"

The overall global figure is 9.6% (774/8,096, WHO). But it varied a lot by place and patient group: a study of 1,454 cases in Guangdong gives about 3.8% (secondary). One number does not fit everywhere.

FALSE"Viet Nam was just lucky"

WHO credited the quick initial reaction and the government's commitment (UN News, Apr 2003). WHO representative Pascale Brudon stressed very fast case detection and proper isolation. WHO team lead Aileen Plant: "real, old-fashioned infectious disease containment", not a miracle (secondary). There were favourable factors (the outbreak was mostly inside one hospital), but early containment is what kept it there.

FALSE"The SARS outbreak started in Hanoi"

The first case was recorded in Guangdong on 16 Nov 2002 (EID 2004, secondary); WHO received a report on 11 Feb 2003. The Hanoi case came from elsewhere (WHO 12 Mar 2003). This is epidemiology, not blame: viruses have no nationality.

FALSE"Dr Urbani died in Hanoi"

He fell ill on 11 Mar on a flight to Bangkok and died in Bangkok on 29 Mar 2003 (WHO 29 Mar 2023; snippet, secondary). Some Vietnamese articles wrongly say Hanoi.

FALSE"SARS was a form of bird flu"

WHO 12 Mar 2003: no link found to the H5N1 bird flu outbreak in Hong Kong. WHO 16 Apr 2003: the cause is a new coronavirus never before seen in humans (snippet, secondary).

Source: as given in each line; full list in Part 9.

Hanoi did not win by luck or by technology. It won with old habits: report what looks wrong, contain the cases, tell the truth. The price was paid by people on the front line.

More: why the "luck" story is easy to believe

"Luck" is a convenient explanation: you never have to ask who did what, and when. But it erases the hard decisions: a doctor who raised the alarm before he was sure, staff who stayed inside a quarantined hospital, a government that announced bad news.

For people at work, the real version is more useful: it shows things anyone can do, without waiting for luck.

The "More" note is Mark's commentary, based on the sourced facts in Parts 2 to 5.

Part 7

Work lesson: early alarms and transparency beat hiding the news

For any team facing something strange: a product defect, a customer complaint, odd numbers, a safety risk.

Report before you are sureA three-line template: "What we saw. What we don't know. What we do next." Send it the same day, don't wait for full data.
Contain firstSwitch off the feature, stop the batch, isolate the system. Stop the spread first, ask why later.
Bring in outsiders earlyCall another team, an expert, your manager as soon as something looks wrong. Asking for help early is not losing.
Write the end criterion firstWhat does "incident over" mean? For example: 2 weeks with no new error, twice the release cycle. Write it before you declare.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. The last time something odd happened, how long did it take from one person noticing to someone with authority knowing?

2. Who on the team may "contain" (stop, switch off, pull back) without waiting for approval?

3. Was the last person who brought bad news thanked, or asked "why didn't you say so sooner"?

4. Does the team have a "known / unknown / next step" report template? If not, write one today.

5. Who is on the front line of the incident, and what has the team done to protect them?

This is real, old-fashioned infectious disease containment.Aileen Plant, WHO team lead in Hanoi, 2003; in the article "Halt of SARS in Vietnam Could Hold Lessons for Other Nations" (secondary source).
