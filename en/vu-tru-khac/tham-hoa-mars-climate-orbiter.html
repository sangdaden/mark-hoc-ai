Essence

A factor of 4.45One pound-force is 4.45 newtons. The file held pounds; the receiving team read newtons.
The bug was on the groundThe flight software was metric and correct. The error sat in a file made by ground computers.
Nine months of driftA little wrong each time. By arrival the probe was nearly 170 km lower than planned.
Someone noticedNavigators saw mismatched numbers from the spring, but raised them by e-mail, never as a formal incident.
Not one person's slipThe board listed 8 contributing causes, from training to verification.

Source: MCO Mishap Investigation Board, Phase I report (10 Nov 1999), pp. 6–7, 13–17.

Part 1

The question of the case

The interface spec said metric. Navigators saw numbers that did not add up for months. So how did a unit error slip through the handoff between two teams, all the way to the Martian atmosphere?

Part 2

What happened

The milestones below come from the Phase I report of the investigation board (chaired by Arthur Stephenson, NASA).

11 Dec 1998 · LaunchA 629 kg probe whose job was to "orbit Mars as the first interplanetary weather satellite" and relay data for the Mars Polar Lander.
Dec 1998 – Apr 1999 · The data file is unusableFor the first four months, the file logging small thruster firings had format problems, so navigators did not use it. From April 1999 it could be used, but the units were still wrong.
Spring – summer 1999 · Numbers disagreeAt the working level there were concerns about orbit solutions that did not match. The Doppler-only solutions "consistently indicated" a path closer to the planet. These discrepancies "were not resolved".
15 Sep 1999 · Fourth trajectory correction (TCM-4)Aimed at a 226 km closest approach.
23 Sep 1999, about an hour before · Estimate of 110 kmA fifth correction (TCM-5) was discussed verbally, but it had never been prepared, so it was not carried out.
23 Sep 1999, 09:00:46 UTC · Orbit insertion burnAt 09:04:52 the probe passed behind Mars, 49 seconds earlier than predicted. The signal never came back.
29 Sep 1999 · The factor of 4.45 is foundSix days after the loss, the small-forces data were found to be too low by a factor of 4.45.

Source: MCO Mishap Investigation Board, Phase I report, pp. 6, 13–14, 17–20; mass of 629 kg: NASA/JPL MCO arrival press kit (Sep 1999), p. 6.

Organisational layer: two spacecraft for the price of one

faster, better, cheaperMPIAT, March 2000

New people, new craft

The navigation team was "unfamiliar with spacecraft" and had "inadequate training" (2 of the 8 contributing causes).

A botched handover

Systems engineering "did not adequately address" the move from development to operations. A recurring question: "Who's in charge?"

Nobody owned the seam

Navigators were worried but "did not communicate their trajectory concerns effectively" to the spacecraft team.

Source: MCO Mishap Investigation Board, Phase I report, pp. 7, 22; Report on Project Management in NASA (Mar 2000), p. 18.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

12 things often said about Mars Climate Orbiter, checked against the primary record. Click each row to see why.

3 SOLID
5 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDA pounds-versus-newtons error in ground software was the root cause

The board's finding (Phase I, pp. 6, 16): the "Small Forces" file did not use metric units and was low by a factor of 4.45.

SOLIDThe probe flew far lower than planned

Aimed at 226 km, final estimate about 57 km, below the 80 km survival limit (pp. 13–14).

SOLIDThe project was short of people and money

The navigation team ran 3 missions at once and MCO had a single navigator (board, p. 24). MPIAT judged Mars '98 "underfunded by at least 30 percent".

CAUTION"Lockheed used imperial, NASA used metric"

Broadly right: NASA's 30 Sep 1999 release says a team in Colorado (industrial partner Lockheed Martin) and a team in California (JPL) used different units. But it was not "NASA metric, the company imperial": the shared spec required metric, and the board does not pin the blame on one company.

CAUTIONThe probe "crashed into" Mars

Nobody knows for sure. The board wrote that it "either was destroyed in the atmosphere or re-entered heliocentric space" (p. 7).

CAUTION"$125 million lost"

This figure comes from 1999 press reports (Reuters, 1 Oct 1999); it is not in the investigation reports. NASA's official numbers cover the orbiter and lander together: $193.1M development and $42.8M operations (NASA/JPL press kit, Sep 1999).

CAUTION"Faster, better, cheaper" was the culprit

MPIAT said the slogan works "if properly applied". The culprit was applying it badly: fewer people, less money, fewer checks.

CAUTIONThe TCM-5 correction would certainly have saved it

Not doing it is one of the 8 contributing causes, and it was designed to raise the orbit to a safe altitude. But it had never been prepared, and the board does not claim it would surely have worked (pp. 19–20).

FALSE"Just one person forgot to convert units"

The board listed 8 contributing causes. NASA's Edward Weiler: "The problem here was not the error, it was the failure of NASA's systems engineering, and the checks and balances in our processes to detect the error."

FALSEThe flight software used the wrong units

"The AMD software installed on the spacecraft used metric units for the computation and was correct." "Mismodeling only occurred in the ground software" (p. 17).

FALSENobody noticed anything until the last minute

From spring 1999 there were working-level concerns about orbit solutions that did not match. They were "only informally reported" and "were not resolved" (p. 13).

FALSEMars Polar Lander was lost to the same unit error

The lander was lost on 3 Dec 1999 for a different reason: the "probable cause" was premature shutdown of the descent engines after a spurious touchdown signal at leg deployment (JPL Special Review Board, Mar 2000).

Sources: MCO Mishap Investigation Board, Phase I (Nov 1999); NASA release 99-113 (30 Sep 1999); MPIAT (Mar 2000); NASA/JPL press kit (Sep 1999); Reuters (1 Oct 1999); JPL D-18709 (22 Mar 2000).

A very ordinary error that had to pass through four layers of checks to become a disaster. That makes it far more worth studying than a joke about units.

More: two spacecraft, two failures, one root

Mars Polar Lander was lost on 3 December 1999. JPL's review board concluded the software probably mistook a spurious signal at leg deployment for touchdown, allowing engine shutdown at about 40 m altitude. A completely different bug, but the independent MPIAT team put both losses in the same picture: Mars '98 was short of money, short of people, and light on checks.

The MCO board's second report (March 2000) set out the vision "Mission Success First" and called for an environment where "problems may be raised without fear of reprisal".

Sources: NASA release 99-113 (30 Sep 1999); JPL D-18709 (22 Mar 2000); MPIAT (14 Mar 2000); Report on Project Management in NASA (13 Mar 2000), pp. 6, 37.

Part 6

Work lessons: check the handoff between two teams

Applies wherever one team passes numbers, files or an API to another: finance and sales, backend and frontend, supplier and customer.

Every seam has one ownerNot team A, not team B, but one person accountable for both ends.
Don't resolve incidents by e-mailE-mail drifts away. A ticket has a status, an owner and a deadline.
Builders stay on into operationsThe board recommends key people "remain with the project from the start to operations".
Ask "what if it's wrong?" firstDraw a fault tree before launch. The board found no such analysis for MCO.

Source: Report on Project Management in NASA (Mar 2000), p. 40; MCO board, Phase I, pp. 22–23, 30.

People sometimes make errors. The problem here was not the error, it was the failure of NASA's systems engineering, and the checks and balances in our processes to detect the error.Edward Weiler, NASA Associate Administrator for Space Science, release 99-113 (30 Sep 1999).
