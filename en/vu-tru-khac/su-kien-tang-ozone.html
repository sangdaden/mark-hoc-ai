Part 1

The question of the event

In 1987 nobody had fully proven that CFCs were punching the hole in the ozone layer. Why did almost the whole world agree to cut them anyway? Because waiting cost more than being wrong: an ozone layer, once lost, cannot be bought back.

The answer is Mark's view, based on the sourced milestones in Parts 2 and 3.

1974–1985WarningA theory on paper, then real measurements in Antarctica.
→
1985–1990DecisionA framework convention, a protocol, then step-by-step tightening.

1990–nowSlow recoveryCFCs last for decades, so the hole shrinks only very slowly.
Part 2

Timeline

The first picture is real data: mean October ozone (Antarctic spring) at the British Antarctic Survey's Halley station.

1974 · A warning on paperMario Molina and Sherwood Rowland publish in Nature: CFCs drift up to the stratosphere, ultraviolet light frees chlorine atoms, and each chlorine atom destroys ozone in a chain reaction.
1978 · Spray cans bannedThe US bans CFCs as propellants in "non-essential" aerosols (rule of 17 Mar 1978, manufacturing ban from 15 Oct 1978). Fridges, air conditioners and insulating foam still use CFCs.
1985 · Vienna ConventionCountries sign a framework convention: commitments to research and cooperate, no cuts yet.
May 1985 · The hole appearsFarman, Gardiner and Shanklin (British Antarctic Survey) publish in Nature: spring ozone over Halley down by about a third. In 1986 Nimbus-7 satellite data confirm it.
16 Sep 1987 · Montreal ProtocolFreeze CFCs and halons at 1986 levels, then cut by 50% within about 10 years. A clause to revise the cuts based on regular scientific assessments.
1 Jan 1989 · In forceCondition met: at least 11 parties representing two-thirds of 1986 consumption.
1990 · TightenedLondon Amendment: developed countries phase out CFCs by 2000; a Multilateral Fund is set up to help developing countries switch.
1995 · Nobel Prize in ChemistryAwarded to Paul Crutzen, Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland for their work on the formation and decomposition of ozone.
2006 · Largest holeThe largest seasonal average area ever measured.
16 Sep 2009 · Every country ratifies196 parties. The Vienna Convention and the Montreal Protocol become the first treaties in UN history to achieve universal ratification.
2016 · Kigali AmendmentThe Montreal machinery is used to cut HFCs, CFC substitutes that spare ozone but warm the planet.
2025 · Fifth smallest since 1992NASA/NOAA: average 18.71 million km² (7 Sep to 13 Oct), about 30% smaller than in 2006. WMO ranks it fourth to fifth smallest.
Around 2066 · Projected recoveryBack to 1980 levels: around 2066 over Antarctica, 2045 over the Arctic, 2040 for the rest of the world.

Part 3

Key decisions

Five decisions, seen through the eyes of the time: who decided, what they knew, what they chose, what followed.

1 · Ban CFCs in spray cans US, 1978

Who decided
The US FDA and EPA (with the Consumer Product Safety Commission).
What they knew
The Molina–Rowland theory and studies by the US National Academy of Sciences. Nobody had measured an ozone decline yet.
What they chose
Ban the easiest part to replace: CFC propellants in non-essential aerosols. Keep essential uses such as inhalers.
What followed
It showed CFCs could be replaced without economic collapse. But fridges, air conditioning and foam still used CFCs, and this was one country.

Source: FDA and EPA, 43 FR 11301 (17 Mar 1978); EPA release "Government Ban on Fluorocarbon Gases in Aerosol Products Begins October 15" (1978); CPSC 1977 (via search excerpts, full text not opened).

2 · Frame first, cut later Vienna, 1985

Who decided
Countries meeting under the UN Environment Programme (UNEP).
What they knew
Models predicted a slow ozone decline. The Halley paper was not yet published.
What they chose
A framework convention: commitments to research, share data and cooperate. No cuts.
What followed
Nothing was cut yet. But when news of the hole arrived a few months later, the negotiating table already existed.

Source: Vienna Convention 1985 (UNEP Ozone Secretariat); Farman et al., Nature 315 (May 1985). The "table already existed" point is Mark's view.

3 · Sign cuts before 100% certainty Montreal, 16 Sep 1987

Who decided
The negotiators: 24 countries and the European Economic Community signed on the first day, including European countries, Japan, the Soviet Union and the US.
What they knew
The hole was real (Halley, satellites). But why it appeared was still debated: chlorine, or the winds and temperatures of the polar region.
What they chose
Freeze at 1986 levels, cut 50% within about 10 years, and revise the cuts based on regular scientific assessments.
What followed
The 50% cut later proved far too small. But thanks to the review clause, the parties could tighten as soon as the evidence grew stronger.

Source: Montreal Protocol 1987; number of signatories per Nature and UNEP (some sources such as Britannica say 46, counting countries that signed later); memorandum to President Reagan, FRUS 1981–88 vol. XLI, doc. 375 (Europe, Japan, the Soviet Union signed); four-yearly assessments per UNEP/WMO 2022.

4 · Tighten to a phase-out, and pay the slow London, 1990

Who decided
The parties to the Protocol, at their second meeting.
What they knew
In 1988 a NASA assessment confirmed ozone loss beyond Antarctica and tied it to CFCs. At Halley, October 1987 and October 1989 were both down to 150 DU.
What they chose
Developed countries phase out CFCs entirely by 2000. A Multilateral Fund to help developing countries, which had already been given a 10-year delay in 1987.
What followed
Large developing countries joined. This laid the ground for universal ratification in 2009.

Source: London Amendment 1990 and Article 5 of the Protocol (UNEP Ozone Secretariat); BAS, October ozone at Halley (Shanklin 2001); UNEP Decision XXI/1 (2009).

5 · Reuse the machinery for climate Kigali, 2016

Who decided
The parties to the Montreal Protocol.
What they knew
HFCs, the CFC substitutes, do not destroy ozone but are very strong greenhouse gases.
What they chose
Add HFCs to the list of substances to be cut on a schedule.
What followed
The 2022 scientific assessment estimates 0.3–0.5 °C of warming avoided by 2100.

Source: Kigali Amendment 2016 (UNEP); WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment 2022, the 0.3–0.5 °C figure per Al Jazeera/AP 9 Jan 2023 and IIFIIR (secondary sources).

Part 4

What the world learned

What changed after the event: laws, institutions, habits.

Law: cuts on a schedule

About 99% of controlled ozone-depleting substances have been phased out (WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment 2022, per UNEP).

Institutions: science has a seat

The four-yearly assessment is a required input for every adjustment. The 2026 edition is in preparation, with the Executive Summary expected late 2026 (NOAA CSL).

Habit: monitor to catch cheating

Atmospheric networks detected an unexpected rise in CFC-11 emissions after 2012; once traced, emissions fell again from 2019 (Montzka et al., Nature 2018 and 2021).
Part 5

Fact check: truth and myths

14 things often said about the ozone layer, checked against measurements and scientific reports. Tap each line for the reasoning.

6 SOLID
3 CAUTION
5 FALSE
SOLIDScientists warned in 1974 that CFCs destroy ozone

Molina and Rowland, Nature 249, 810–812 (1974): CFCs are long-lived, rise to the stratosphere, ultraviolet light frees chlorine, and chlorine destroys ozone in a chain. In 1995 both, with Paul Crutzen, won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

SOLIDThe hole was found by a ground instrument, and satellites confirmed it later

Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin used a Dobson spectrophotometer at Halley (Nature, May 1985). Stolarski et al. (1986) confirmed it with TOMS and SBUV data from the Nimbus-7 satellite.

SOLIDMontreal was signed before the cause of the hole was fully proven

By September 1987 the hole had been measured, but the chlorine explanation still competed with hypotheses about polar meteorology. A NASA assessment in 1988, after the signing, clearly tied the loss to CFCs.

SOLIDIt was the first treaty ratified by every country

On 16 Sep 2009, with 196 parties, the Vienna Convention and the Montreal Protocol became the first treaties in UN history to achieve universal ratification (UNEP, Decision XXI/1).

SOLIDAlmost all controlled ozone-depleting substances have been phased out

About 99%, according to the WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment 2022 (as summarised by UNEP).

SOLIDMontreal did more for the climate than Kyoto's first-period target

CFCs are also strong greenhouse gases. Velders et al. (PNAS, 2007): Montreal's climate benefit is "far larger" than Kyoto's first commitment target; the authors presented it as about 5–6 times in 2010.

CAUTION"Montreal saved hundreds of millions from skin cancer"

The EPA's 443 million cases is a model estimate, only for people born in the US between 1890 and 2100, against a no-controls scenario. The large benefit is real, but it is not a count and not global.

CAUTION"Without Montreal, two-thirds of the ozone layer would be gone"

This is the "world avoided" model scenario of Newman et al. (ACP, 2009): if CFCs had grown 3% a year, 17% of global ozone would be lost by 2020 and 67% by 2065. Useful to see the scale, not a certainty.

CAUTION"The ozone hole causes global warming"

Two different problems: the ozone hole is about ultraviolet light, warming is about greenhouse gases. But they are linked: CFCs and HFCs are greenhouse gases, so cutting them also helps the climate (Velders 2007; Kigali Amendment).

FALSE"The ozone hole has healed itself"

Not healed, and not "by itself": the ozone layer is recovering because people cut CFCs. In 2025 the hole still averaged 18.71 million km² (NASA/NOAA). Return to 1980 levels is projected around 2066 over Antarctica (2022 Assessment).

FALSE"The ozone hole is made up"

Continuous measurements at Halley since 1956: October mean 300 DU (1957–72), down to 150 DU in 1987 and 122 DU in 1993. Satellites confirmed it independently. The chemistry earned a Nobel Prize in 1995.

FALSE"The hole shrinks steadily every year"

At Halley: 150 DU (1987), 216 DU (1988), 150 DU (1989). WMO says year-to-year swings are mainly meteorological; the long-term trend is what shows recovery.

FALSE"CFCs are heavier than air, so they cannot reach the stratosphere"

Winds and convection mix the atmosphere, so heavy and light gases alike are carried up. CFCs have been measured directly in the stratosphere (WMO/UNEP, "Twenty Questions and Answers About the Ozone Layer").

FALSE"Volcanoes put more chlorine into the stratosphere than people do"

Volcanic chlorine mostly dissolves in water and is rained out in the lower atmosphere. Stratospheric chlorine comes mainly from human-made substances (WMO/UNEP, "Twenty Questions and Answers About the Ozone Layer").

How well did the world respond?

5/5
Done right
  • Signed cuts in 1987 when evidence was strong enough, not certain
  • Built in a clause to revise based on science
  • Paid to help developing countries instead of only pressuring them
Not done right
  • 13 years from warning (1974) to treaty (1987)
  • The initial 50% cut was too small and had to be tightened
  • Illegal emissions still happened, caught only by monitoring the air

One of the rare times the whole world got it right: it did not wait for 100% certainty, and it did not sign once and stop. The minus is speed, and that slowness is paid for with more than half a century of waiting to heal.

More: why did it work this time, when many other problems did not?

Some conditions helped: few chemicals to cut, few producing companies, substitutes already available, and a danger (skin cancer) that is easy to picture. This is Mark's view, not the finding of a study.

But helpful conditions do not turn into a treaty by themselves. What made the difference was the design: frame first, cut small first, review regularly, and pay the slow. That design works at work too.

Part 7

Work lesson: act when the evidence is strong enough, don't wait for 100% certainty

For any team that has to decide before the data is complete: a spreading bug, a customer about to leave, a risk that keeps growing.

Compare the two costsWrite it down: if we wait 3 more months and the risk is real, what do we lose? If we act now and the risk is not real, what do we lose? Which one can't be undone?
Decide small, set a review dateDon't demand a perfect decision. Take the smallest step that works and write down the date the team will look at the data again.
Write the threshold now"If errors exceed X next week, we stop the release." Write it while calm, not while on fire.
Bring the slow alongWhich group will find the change hardest? What do they need: time, tools, a helper? Give them that instead of just a deadline.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. The last decision your team postponed "to wait for more data": what data, and when will it arrive?

2. When the team reports a risk, does it state a confidence level (low, medium, high), or just "not sure yet"?

3. Which running decision has no review date? Write one today.

4. At what threshold does the team tighten, and at what threshold does it relax? Is it written down?

5. Who in the team (or which partner team) will be slowest to change, and what have you done to help them?
