Part 1

The question

If a teacher believes a pupil is about to improve, does the pupil really improve? Today's answer: a little, mostly when the teacher knows nothing else about the child, and far less than the legend of "believe and they become geniuses".

Part 3

Key concepts

The self-fulfilling prophecy

self-fulfilling prophecyMerton, 1948

Four channels of expectation

climate · feedback · input · outputRosenthal, 1973 · Harris and Rosenthal, 1985

Climate

Warmer: smiles, eye contact, nods, a softer voice for people we expect a lot from.

Input

More teaching, harder tasks, more opportunities passed their way.

Output

Asking them more, waiting longer for their answer, giving hints when they are stuck.

Feedback

More specific praise and critique, instead of vague praise or silence.

Harris and Rosenthal (1985) pooled studies of these behaviours: expectations do not travel by telepathy, but through small gestures repeated every day. Climate and input are the clearest channels.

Pygmalion, Golem and Galatea

high · low · self-expectationBabad, Inbar and Rosenthal, 1982

Pygmalion

Others expect a lot, we do better. Named after the sculptor in Greek myth who fell in love with his own statue.

Golem

Others expect little, we do worse. Bias-prone student teachers treated pupils they saw as weak more negatively (26 student teachers, 202 pupils).

Galatea

Our expectations of ourselves. At work, Eden argues this is the route by which a boss's expectations reach results.

Expectations only "take" when the teacher is new to the pupil

credibility of inductionRaudenbush, 1984

Pygmalion at work

Pygmalion in managementEden and Shani, 1982 · McNatt, 2000 · Eden et al., 2000

Boot camp

Israeli army instructors were told (at random) that some trainees had high command potential. That group scored higher (Eden and Shani, 1982).

17 studies pooled: large

Mean d of 1.13 across 2,874 people, strongest in the military, with men, and with people previously expected to do poorly (McNatt, 2000).

Training bosses to expect more: weak

7 workshop experiments for managers: small mean effect, r = 0.13, little change in bosses or staff (Eden et al., 2000).

Fooling a boss with a fake list works; teaching a boss to "expect more" is much harder. That is why the lessons in Part 6 focus on concrete behaviour, not belief.

Part 4

Evidence: how far can we trust it

10 common claims about the Pygmalion effect, checked against current research. Click each line for the reason.

3 SOLID
5 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDOther people's expectations can affect our results

Rosenthal and Rubin (1978) pooled 345 experiments on interpersonal expectancy effects. Even the strongest critics (Jussim and Harber, 2005) conclude the effect is real. The debate is about size, not existence.

SOLIDExpectations are passed on through everyday behaviour

Harris and Rosenthal (1985) pooled studies on how people with expectations behave: warmer, more teaching, more chances to answer. The mechanism is fairly consistent.

SOLIDTeacher expectations predict grades mainly because teachers read pupils correctly

Expectations correlate with achievement at about 0.4–0.8, while experimental effects are about 0.1–0.2. Jussim and Harber (2005) estimate about 75% accuracy, about 25% self-fulfilling prophecy.

CAUTION"Bloomers" gained more IQ (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968)

The overall gap was only about 4 IQ points (12 vs 8), almost all from grade 1 (about 15 points) and grade 2 (about 10 points); other grades showed no difference or the reverse (per Jussim and Harber, 2005). Thorndike (1968) pointed out that one whole class had a mean pre-test IQ in the intellectually disabled range. Snow (1995) found about 35% of scores outside the test's norm range, and the effect vanished without 5 extreme pupils (one went from 17 to 110).

CAUTIONExpectations hit low achievers and stigmatised groups harder

Madon, Jussim and Eccles (1997): effects of about 0.26 for low achievers, 0.08 for high achievers. Jussim and Harber (2005) report similar findings for poor and African American pupils, but only about 2 studies have tested this.

CAUTIONAt work, the Pygmalion effect is very large

McNatt (2000) pooled 17 studies (2,874 people): mean d of 1.13. But most were training courses, many in the military, and effects varied widely. There is little data from ordinary, running companies.

CAUTIONFeedback with "I believe you can meet a high bar" gets people to revise

Yeager et al. (2014), US 7th graders: among African American pupils, 64% turned in a revision with this note vs 27% with standard feedback. Clear result, but in a school, small sample, little tested at work.

CAUTIONLow expectations do harm (the Golem effect)

Babad, Inbar and Rosenthal (1982): bias-prone student teachers treated pupils they saw as weak more negatively, and the gap showed in task performance. Only 26 student teachers, but consistent with later studies.

CRITICISEDIf the teacher believes in you, your IQ shoots up

Raudenbush (1984) pooled 18 IQ experiments: mean d = 0.11; Snow (1995) put the median at just 0.035. The effect is near zero once teachers have known pupils for more than 2 weeks. The "believe and they become geniuses" version in self-help books is an exaggeration.

CRITICISEDJust train bosses to expect more and productivity rises

Eden et al. (2000) ran 7 experiments training managers in a "Pygmalion leadership style": small mean effect (r = 0.13) and little evidence of change in bosses or staff. Eden, the pioneer of workplace Pygmalion research, published this himself.

Strengths
  • Random assignment: a real experiment in real classrooms
  • Opened a whole field on expectations and bias
  • The mechanism (4 channels) is easy to understand, observe and fix
Weaknesses
  • The IQ test gave absurd scores for young children
  • Results came only from grades 1 and 2 and a few extreme pupils
  • Later experiments found much smaller effects

A real but small effect, strongest when people are still strangers. Keep the lesson about behaviour, drop the promise of magic.

More: why a weak study became so famous

Pygmalion arrived just as the US was fiercely debating poor and Black pupils' education. The story "if teachers believe, children will thrive" was hopeful and simple, so it spread through the press, education books and later management books.

Thorndike (1968), a measurement specialist, wrote a scathing review: some IQ data could not possibly be right, so every conclusion drawn from them was doubtful. His famous line, paraphrased: when the clock strikes thirteen, you doubt more than the last stroke (paraphrase, not an exact quote). Rosenthal replied that poor measurement makes differences harder to find, so the differences found still mean something.

Dozens of later experiments give a modest picture: the effect is real, usually small, larger for disadvantaged people, and most of the predictive power of expectations comes from people guessing right. It is an early example of what is now called the replication crisis.

Part 6

Lessons: expectations and feedback with colleagues and reports

You do not need to "believe harder". You need to check how you treat people differently across the 4 channels, then even it out.

In meetings

Ask the quiet person first, wait a few more seconds for their answer, do not jump to "the one with ideas". That is the "output" channel.

When assigning work

Rotate the hard tasks, the client meetings, the chance to present to senior leaders. Keep a note of who got what this quarter.

In feedback emails

"I'm giving detailed comments because this needs to meet a high bar, and I'm confident you can. Three things to fix: …" High bar, belief, concrete guidance.
Audit your silent labelsWho on your team have you boxed in as weak? On what evidence, since when?
Even-handed, not blind faithTreat everyone evenly; update expectations on real results.
Give feedback with a good reasonSay why you are commenting: a high bar and belief in them.
