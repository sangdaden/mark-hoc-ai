Part 2

The story

Who, where, when: from a street performance to a lab in Cambridge.

Before 2006 · Lahore, PakistanA boy performed in the street: he pushed knives through his arms and walked on burning coals, feeling no pain. He often came to hospital to have his wounds patched up after a show. Doctors took notice.
About age 14 · An accidentHe died after a fall from a roof, before the research team could examine him. Not feeling pain does not mean not getting hurt.
The years after · Three familiesThe team of geneticist Geoffrey Woods (Cambridge Institute for Medical Research, UK) found relatives of the boy, aged 4 to 14, who had also never felt pain. They belonged to three families in northern Pakistan where relatives had married each other.
What the children could still feelTouch, warmth, cold, tickle, pressure and limb position were all normal. Parents and teachers judged their intelligence normal. But in their first 4 years they bit their lips and tongues until injured; one needed plastic surgery on the lip.
14/12/2006 · The Nature paperCox, Woods and colleagues showed that each family carried a different mutation, but all three broke the same gene, SCN9A. Conclusion: this gene is "essential and non-redundant" for pain in humans.

Source: Cox et al. (2006), Nature 444: 894–898 (three families in northern Pakistan, three mutations, conclusion); OMIM #243000 summarising the paper (remaining senses, intelligence, lip and tongue injuries in the first 4 years); Nature News 13/12/2006, HealthDay, The Naked Scientists (the boy in Lahore, his age at death, relatives aged 4–14, Woods's team; secondary sources).

Why did marriage between relatives help find the gene?

The condition is recessive: a child must inherit a broken copy from both parents to be affected. When parents are related, a child's two copies are more likely to be identical in the same stretch of DNA. The team searched for that shared stretch in the affected children and narrowed it to a region on chromosome 2 that contains SCN9A.

This is about how genes are inherited, not a judgement on any community's customs.

Source: Cox et al. (2006) (recessive, region 2q24.3).

Part 3

How the gene works, in 3 sentences

1. The SCN9A gene makes the Nav1.7 sodium channel, a small "gate" in the membrane of pain-sensing nerves.

2. When skin is burned or cut, Nav1.7 amplifies a small signal enough for the nerve to fire up to the spinal cord and brain, and we feel "pain".

3. With both copies broken the gate cannot open: the signal dies at the start. Mutations that make the gate open too easily do the opposite: severe burning pain.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is rumour

13 things often said about people who feel no pain, checked against original sources. Tap a row to see why.

6 SOLID
3 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDA broken SCN9A gene means no pain from birth

Three families, three different mutations, the same gene (Cox et al. 2006).

SOLIDThey still sense touch, warmth, cold and pressure

Only pain is missing; other sensations are normal (OMIM #243000, summarising Cox 2006).

SOLIDMany also lose their sense of smell

Nav1.7 is also in smell cells; people with loss-of-function SCN9A cannot smell (Weiss et al. 2011; MedlinePlus Genetics).

SOLID"Turned-up" mutations in the same gene cause severe burning pain

Erythromelalgia (Yang et al. 2004) and paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (Fertleman et al. 2006).

SOLIDFeeling no pain leads to more injuries and may shorten life

Lips and tongues bitten in early years (OMIM); repeated injuries, possibly shorter life expectancy (MedlinePlus Genetics).

SOLIDIn 2025 the FDA approved a new class of non-opioid painkiller

Suzetrigine, 30/1/2025, for moderate-to-severe acute pain in adults (FDA, via trade press).

CAUTION"The boy who walked on coals was the one studied"

He died before the team could examine him. The paper studied his relatives (Nature News 2006, secondary source).

CAUTION"Finding SCN9A would quickly give us new painkillers"

Nearly 20 years on, no Nav1.7 blocker has been approved. A major candidate stopped in 2015 after a failed phase 2 trial (Nature Reviews Drug Discovery 2019); a 2025 review shows mouse experiments differ sharply from human trials (Pain 2025).

CAUTION"Blocking Nav1.7 in adults would mimic being born without it"

People born without the channel lack it for life, and the body may adapt differently. One study (peer-reviewed preprint, eLife 2025) found pain-sensing cells can use other sodium channels instead.

FALSE"Feeling no pain is lucky, a superpower"

Pain is an alarm. Without it, children hurt themselves without knowing; the boy in Lahore died after a fall (OMIM; MedlinePlus; Nature News 2006).

FALSE"The new 2025 painkiller targets Nav1.7 itself"

Suzetrigine blocks Nav1.8, a different sodium channel on pain-sensing nerves (FDA 2025).

FALSE"Everyone who feels no pain has an SCN9A mutation"

Insensitivity to pain with inability to sweat (CIPA) is caused by the NTRK1 gene (MedlinePlus Genetics). A woman in Scotland feels little pain because of changes in FAAH and FAAH-OUT (British Journal of Anaesthesia 2019, read via news).

FALSE"Feeling no pain comes with intellectual disability"

With SCN9A, parents and teachers judged the children's intelligence normal (OMIM, summarising Cox 2006). Intellectual disability is common in CIPA, a different disease.

Source: as given in each row; full list in Part 9.

Part 5

Where things stand today

From one gene to the hunt for non-addictive painkillers.

2011 · Smell is lost tooPeople with loss-of-function SCN9A cannot smell; Nav1.7 is needed for smell cells to "talk" to the brain.
2015 · Nav1.7 blockers stumbleThe first selective Nav1.7 blocker to reach large trials was stopped after phase 2 in painful diabetic neuropathy. Many other programmes stopped too.
2019 · Another gene, another storyA woman in Scotland feels little pain because of a change in FAAH-OUT. Pain has several "switches".
2021 · Switching the gene down in miceA form of CRISPR that does not cut DNA (dCas9) temporarily silenced the Nav1.7 gene in mouse spinal nerves: pain relief lasting weeks to months. Mice only.
30/1/2025 · A drug via the "sibling channel"The FDA approved suzetrigine, a Nav1.8 blocker, for moderate-to-severe acute pain in adults, based on two phase 3 trials after surgery. The FDA called it the first drug in a new class of non-opioid painkillers.
2025–2027 · Still being testedSuzetrigine is in phase 3 trials for painful diabetic neuropathy, expected to finish in 2027. No results yet (checked 16/10/2026).

Source: Weiss et al. (2011), Nature 472: 186–190; Yang et al. (2025), Pain (PubMed 39928833); Habib et al. (2019), British Journal of Anaesthesia (via news, secondary source); Moreno et al. (2021), Science Translational Medicine (mice; duration of relief per UC San Diego and GEN News, secondary sources); FDA 30/1/2025; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06628908, NCT07231419.

More: why is the gene clear but the drug hard?

People born without Nav1.7 lack it in every cell, all their lives. A pill blocks only part of it, for a few hours, and must avoid very similar sodium channels in the heart and brain. Mouse experiments often use young male mice, inflammatory pain and a single dose; human trials are usually in nerve pain with daily doses.

Source: Yang et al. (2025), Pain (mouse vs human mismatch); eLife preprint 2025 (other channels compensate). The comments are Mark's.

Part 7

Takeaway: pain is a signal, don't just try to switch it off

People who feel no pain show that pain is an alarm system. Knowing how to read the alarm helps you ask your doctor better questions.

For reading and understanding, not a substitute for medical advice.

Note down the painWhere, since when, how strong on a 0–10 scale, what makes it worse. That is exactly what a doctor needs.
Ask "why", not only "what to take"Lasting or unusual pain is a signal worth tracing to its cause. Don't silence it with medicine on your own.
Read "new painkiller" news closelyApproved for which pain, in whom, tested after what surgery? Don't extend it to every kind of pain.
Doubt "instant pain relief"A product that promises to stop pain at once without naming a cause is promising to switch off the fire alarm.
Self-check: your most recent pain

1. Think of the last pain that lasted more than a few days.

2. Could you say where it was, since when, how strong out of 10, and what made it worse?

3. Did you ask (or learn) the cause, or only ask for a medicine?

4. Missing two or more: next time, write it down before the appointment.

The self-check is Mark's suggestion, not a procedure from a study.
