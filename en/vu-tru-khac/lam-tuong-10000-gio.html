Essentials

It is an average~10,000 hours was the average of the best violin students at age 20, not a finish line.
An 8-fold spreadChess players reached master level after anywhere from about 3,000 to over 23,000 hours.
The kind of hour mattersDeliberate practice: a narrow goal, fast feedback, just beyond your comfort zone.
Part of the storyPractice explains roughly 1/5 to 1/3 of the differences in chess, music and sport. Far less in office work.
Don't swap one myth for anotherThe rest is not automatically "innate talent". Practice still matters.

Sources: Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (Psychological Review, 1993); Ericsson and Pool, Peak (2016); Campitelli and Gobet (2011); Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (Psychological Science, 2014); Hambrick et al. (Intelligence, 2014).

Part 1

The question behind the myth

A small study of violin students became the "10,000-hour rule" for every profession. The real question is not "how many hours" but "what kind of hours, and how much do hours explain".

1993A findingThe best violin students had practised alone for far more hours than weaker ones.
→
2008A ruleOutliers names it the "10,000-Hour Rule", with stories about the Beatles and Bill Gates.
→
2014–2019A correctionMeta-analyses and a replication: practice matters, but less than people think.
Part 2

Where the myth came from

Every retelling dropped one more condition.

1973 · Simon and Chase, chessA master needs about a decade. A master was estimated to have spent 10,000 to 50,000 hours "staring at chess positions". This is the distant root of the "ten years" idea.
1993 · Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, Berlin30 violin students, 3 groups. The best group had practised alone the most. The paper called this "deliberate practice".
2006 · Daniel LevitinIn This Is Your Brain on Music: "ten thousand hours of practice" are required for world-class mastery, "in anything".
2008 · Malcolm Gladwell, OutliersChapter 2 is titled "The 10,000-Hour Rule". The Beatles playing in Hamburg, Bill Gates on a computer as a schoolboy.
2013 · Gladwell answers his criticsIn The New Yorker: the rule is about complex fields and does not promise that anyone can become an expert.
2016 · Ericsson and Pool, PeakThe author of the original study says the "10,000-hour rule" gets his work wrong in several ways.

Sources: Simon and Chase, American Scientist 61(4), 1973 (the 10,000–50,000 hours figure as quoted in Gladwell, The New Yorker, 2013, secondary source); Ericsson et al. (1993); Levitin (2006); Gladwell (2008, 2013); Ericsson and Pool (2016).

What the evidence says

The original study: hours rise with skill level

Meta-analysis: how much of the difference do hours explain

variance explainedMacnamara et al. 2014

Higher estimates

Correcting for measurement error, practice explains about 34% in chess and 30% in music (Hambrick et al., Intelligence, 2014).

Pushback

Ericsson argued that Macnamara's team included activities that are not deliberate practice, pulling the figure down. Platz et al. (2014) found a correlation of 0.61 in music.

Elite sport

18% of differences across sport overall, but among world-class athletes practice explains almost nothing (about 1%).

Sources: Hambrick, Oswald, Altmann, Meinz, Gobet and Campitelli, Intelligence 45, 2014; Platz, Kopiez, Lehmann and Wolf, Frontiers in Psychology, 2014; Macnamara, Moreau and Hambrick, Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(3), 2016 (18%); the ~1% figure for elite athletes per BPS Research Digest and Fox News (2016), secondary sources.

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, Wrong

11 common versions of the myth, checked against the original research. Click a line to see why.

3 SOLID
4 CAUTION
4 WRONG
SOLIDThe very best have usually practised for many years

In violin (Ericsson 1993) and chess (Gobet and Campitelli 2007), nobody reached the top with very little practice. The fastest of 104 Argentinian players still needed about 3,000 hours.

SOLIDThe kind of practice matters more than the hours that pass

This is Ericsson's central point from 1993 to Peak (2016): narrow goals, feedback, slightly beyond your ability. The review of doctors (Choudhry 2005) shows that years on the job do not automatically make people better.

SOLIDPractice explains a sizeable share of differences in chess, music and sport

About 18–26% according to Macnamara (2014), 30–34% when corrected for measurement error (Hambrick 2014). Not small, but not everything.

CAUTION"It takes about 10,000 hours to become an expert"

The number has a real origin: the average of the best violin group at age 20. But it is an average, recalled from memory, and at that age they were not yet masters. In chess, hours to master level differ almost 8-fold (3,016 to 23,608).

CAUTION"Practice explains only 12%, so it hardly matters"

The 12% is an average across many domains and measures. Ericsson disputed the definition used in that analysis; correcting for measurement error gives higher shares. The sources still disagree, but nobody says practice is useless.

CAUTION"The Beatles succeeded thanks to 10,000 hours in Hamburg"

They did play a great deal. But Ericsson argues that nightly performing is not deliberate practice, and that Gladwell did not distinguish the two. An anecdote cannot prove a rule.

CAUTION"So innate talent decides everything"

That swaps one myth for another. The unexplained part includes starting age, working memory, personality, quality of teaching, environment and measurement error, not just genes. Gobet and Campitelli: practice is "necessary but not sufficient".

WRONG"Anyone who puts in 10,000 hours becomes an expert"

In the Argentinian chess sample, some players had practised more than 25,000 hours without reaching master level (Campitelli and Gobet 2011). The 2019 replication also found the best group did not practise more than the good group.

WRONG"The 10,000 figure holds for every field"

Ericsson says the number varies from field to field. In professions, practice explained under 1% of differences, in education 4% (Macnamara 2014).

WRONG"Ericsson came up with the 10,000-hour rule"

The name is Gladwell's (2008). Ericsson later wrote that the rule misrepresents his research in several ways (Peak, 2016; Salon, 2016).

WRONG"Doing a job for 10 years means 10,000 hours of practice"

Routine hours on the job are not deliberate practice. 32 of 62 evaluations of doctors found quality declined with years in practice (Choudhry 2005).

Sources: as in Part 3 and the list in Part 8. Ericsson's point that "performing is not deliberate practice" read via interviews and secondary quotations (Wharton Knowledge, Salon 2016).

The grain of truth
  • The top takes many years of practice
  • Encourages persistence instead of blaming "natural gifts"
  • Brought deliberate practice to a wide audience
What's wrong and harmful
  • Turns an average into a finish line
  • Makes people count hours instead of measuring progress
  • Easy to blame "not enough hours" when someone stalls

Half right: the right practice is necessary, but there is no magic number, and not every hour is equal.

More: why this myth lives on

A round number, a fair-sounding promise ("anyone can do it"), and great stories about the Beatles and Bill Gates. It is easier to remember than "deliberate practice explains a sizeable but not complete share of differences in performance, depending on the field".

Both sides of the debate agree on one thing: practice helps. They differ on how big its share is and how to measure it.

Part 6

Work lesson: measure progress, don't count hours

For any skill at work: presenting, writing emails, Excel, client interviews, coding.

Break the skill downDon't practise "being good at Excel". Practise "building a summary table from raw data in 10 minutes".
Shorten the feedback loopAt work, feedback comes late or never. Make your own: record yourself, compare with a model, ask someone to score.
A stretch, not an overloadIf it feels easy, you've stopped improving. If it feels hopeless, it's too hard. Aim for "only with effort".
Compare with yourselfHours don't explain all the differences. Don't beat yourself up because someone else improves faster; check whether you beat last week.
