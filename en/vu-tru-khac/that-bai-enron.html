Part 1

The question of the case

Fortune named it "America's most innovative company" six years in a row. Why was it bankrupt a few months later? Because a large part of its profit came from deals where almost nobody outside, and very few people inside, could explain where the real money came from.

Source: Enron press release, 6 Feb 2001 (Fortune, sixth year in a row); bankruptcy court notice, case 01-16034 (2 Dec 2001).

Part 2

What happened

To the top and down again in about 15 months. Dates come from Enron's SEC filings, court records and testimony to Congress.

30/1/1992 · Allowed to book profit up frontThe SEC sends a no-objection letter on Enron Gas Services using mark-to-market accounting for gas contracts.
28/6/1999 and 10/1999 · The board approves LJMCFO Andrew Fastow may run the LJM1 fund, then LJM2, funds that will buy and sell assets with Enron itself.
2000 · The peakReported revenue of $100.8 billion (1999: $40.1 billion). In February 2001 Fortune names it "most innovative" for the sixth year running.
14/8/2001 · The CEO quits, a letter is sentJeffrey Skilling resigns suddenly. The same day Sherron Watkins sends an anonymous letter to chairman Ken Lay; on 22/8 she meets Lay.
16/10/2001 · Third-quarter loss$1.01 billion after-tax charges, a net loss of $618 million.
8/11/2001 · "Don't rely on the old reports"Enron restates 1997–2000 profits and says the financial statements for those years should no longer be relied on.
2/12/2001 · BankruptcyChapter 11 filing in the Southern District of New York. About $63 billion in assets, the largest in the US at the time.
2002 · InvestigationsPowers Report (2/2002), Senate (7/2002). Andersen convicted of obstruction of justice (6/2002), reversed by the Supreme Court in 2005.
2004–2013 · The courtsFastow pleads guilty (1/2004). Lay and Skilling convicted (5/2006). Lay dies before sentencing. Skilling gets 24 years 4 months, resentenced to 14 years (2013).

Why: four layers of causes

Strategy layer: book the profit today, the cash comes over years

mark-to-market accountingSEC 1992 · Powers 2002

Structure layer: private vehicles, and one man on both sides of the table

special purpose entities · related-party dealsPowers 2002 · SEC LR 18776

Organisation layer: the board heard the warning, then approved anyway

board oversightSenate 2002 · Powers 2002

Gatekeeper layer: auditors, banks, lawyers

gatekeepersSEC 2003 · Supreme Court 2005

Auditor Arthur Andersen

Signed off accounts that later had to be restated. Staff destroyed Enron documents from 10/2001; the firm was convicted of obstruction of justice in 2002. In 2005 the Supreme Court reversed 9–0 because the jury instructions were wrong.

Banks

The SEC made J.P. Morgan Chase pay $135 million and Citigroup $101 million for the Enron-related part, for helping deals that dressed up the accounts.

Lawyers

After receiving Watkins's letter, Ken Lay handed the questions to the company's own outside law firm, Vinson & Elkins.

Source: Arthur Andersen LLP v. United States, 544 U.S. 696 (31 May 2005); SEC press release 2003-87 (28 July 2003); Sherron Watkins's testimony to the House (2/2002), per CNN (secondary source).

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

14 things often said about Enron, checked against Enron's SEC filings, the Powers Report, the Senate report and court records. Tap each line for the reason.

4 SOLID
3 CAUTION
7 FALSE
SOLIDEnron's top leaders were convicted

25 May 2006: the jury convicted Lay on all 6 counts and Skilling on 19 of 28 (acquitted on 9 insider trading counts). Lay was also convicted on 4 more counts by the judge in a separate bench trial. Fastow pleaded guilty on 14 Jan 2004 and got 6 years in prison (DOJ press releases of 25 May 2006 and 26 Sep 2006).

SOLIDThe CFO made more than $30 million from funds dealing with Enron itself

The Powers Report and Enron's disclosures in late 2001 put Fastow's take from LJM at more than $30 million (per CNN Money, 2 Feb 2002, secondary source; the original page could not be checked because the network was blocked).

SOLIDSomeone inside raised the alarm

Sherron Watkins sent an anonymous letter to Ken Lay on 14 Aug 2001 (many reports say 15 Aug; her testimony says 14 Aug), then met Lay on 22 Aug 2001. The letter feared the company would "implode in a wave of accounting scandals".

SOLIDEmployees lost retirement money because the plan was heavy in Enron stock

The US Department of Labor sued Lay, Skilling, former outside directors and the plan committee in 2003; 2004 settlements returned at least $66.5 million to the 401(k) and ESOP plans. At the end of 2000 about 62% of the 401(k) plan's assets were Enron stock (CNN citing Reuters, secondary source).

CAUTION"Skilling served 24 years"

The original sentence was 292 months (24 years 4 months, 23 Oct 2006). The appeals court upheld the convictions but vacated the sentence; in 2013 he was resentenced to 168 months (14 years), with about $42 million going to victims (DOJ, 21 Jun 2013).

CAUTION"The largest bankruptcy in US history"

True when filed (12/2001), with about $63 billion in assets. WorldCom overtook it the following year (Oil & Gas Journal, secondary source). Some reports use "$63 billion" for both assets and debts.

CAUTION"70% of Enron's profits were made up"

The figure appears in press accounts of Powers's testimony (for July 2000 to October 2001). This article could not find the sentence in the original documents, so it is not used as fact.

FALSE"Enron fell because of a few bad people"

Powers: "failures at many levels and by many people". The Senate: the board "knowingly allowed" high-risk accounting. The FBI: 22 people convicted. The SEC fined two banks. The auditor was prosecuted. The crooks were real, but the system around them let it run for years.

FALSE"The board was fooled and knew nothing"

Andersen told the Audit Committee on 7 Feb 1999 that the accounting was "pushing limits". The board itself let the CFO run LJM1 (6/1999) and LJM2 (10/1999). The Senate concluded the board "knowingly allowed" it. Some directors dispute details, for example the word "waived" for the ethics code.

FALSE"Ken Lay went to prison"

Lay died on 5 Jul 2006, before sentencing. On 17 Oct 2006 the judge vacated the conviction and dismissed the indictment under the "abatement" rule: a defendant who dies before exhausting appeals (NBC News, Jurist, secondary sources).

FALSE"Enron was a shell with no real business"

Enron had gas pipelines, power plants and a real energy trading arm (Form 10-K for 2000). The problem was dressed-up profit and hidden debt, not that the whole company was fake.

FALSE"Mark-to-market accounting was illegal"

The SEC did not object to it from 30 Jan 1992 (secondary source). The wrong lay in inflated estimates and deals built to avoid booking losses.

FALSE"Watkins blew the whistle to regulators"

She wrote to the company's chairman, internally. The letter reached the public when Congress released it in early 2002. The press called her a "whistleblower", but what she did was warn upward.

FALSE"Andersen was convicted of accounting fraud, and it stuck"

The charge was obstruction of justice (destroying documents), not accounting fraud. On 31 May 2005 the Supreme Court reversed 9–0 because of faulty jury instructions (544 U.S. 696).

Not a few bad people. A system where everyone was rewarded when the numbers looked good, and nobody was made to explain where the numbers came from.

More: what changed in law after Enron

In 2002 the US passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act. One of its provisions (section 308(a), "Fair Fund") is what the SEC used to send $236 million of the two banks' settlement money to Enron victims.

But laws only patch the holes already seen. The "great profits, nobody can explain where the money is" failure returns later in this series: Lehman Brothers (2008) and FTX (2022).

Source for "More": SEC press release 2003-87 (Sarbanes-Oxley section 308(a), $236 million); the rest is Mark's comment.

Part 6

Work lesson: be able to explain where the money comes from

Use it for any number that makes you happy: revenue from a new channel, a project with a "surprise profit", a metric that suddenly jumps on the dashboard.

The two-sentence testFor every large revenue line: "who pays this, and why do they pay". Explain it to a new colleague. If you can't, mark it red.
Two columns: booked and collectedEvery report puts "profit on the books" next to "cash received". A growing gap is a question, not an achievement.
Nobody on both sides of the tableWhoever approves a contract must not benefit from the other side. If there is a conflict, declare it and let someone else approve.
Warnings with an answer deadlineWhoever raises a concern gets a written answer by a clear deadline, from someone not involved in what is being questioned.
Check your own team: 5 questions for the next meeting
  1. What is your team's best number this month? Who can explain in two sentences where it comes from?
  2. How much of it has turned into cash (or a real result), and how much is still an estimate?
  3. Is there a contract, supplier or partner where the approver has a personal connection? Has it been declared?
  4. The last time someone said "this number looks odd", did they get an answer, and from whom?
  5. If an outsider asked "how are you making so much", would you want them to see the books?
The tragic consequences were the result of failures at many levels and by many people.William Powers, testimony to the US House, 2/2002, per the PBS NewsHour transcript.
