One-minute recap

Essence

James never said 10%In 1907 he wrote that we use "only a small part" of our resources. No number at all.
The number came laterA 1936 preface pinned "10%" on James. An ad turned "ability" into "brain-power".
2% of weight, 20% of energyEvolution is unlikely to keep such an expensive organ and leave 90% of it idle.
Sparse, not spareOnly a small share of neurons fire hard at any moment. They take turns; the whole brain is used.
Lesson: go to the sourceA sentence passed through three people has already changed meaning. Find the original before you quote.

Sources: W. James, "The Energies of Men" (1907); Lowell Thomas's preface to Dale Carnegie's book (1936), per Beyerstein (Scientific American); Raichle and Gusnard, PNAS 2002 (2% and 20%); Lennie, Current Biology 2003 (sparse neural activity).

Part 1

The question

A claim nobody ever proved, rejected by scientists themselves, has survived for nearly 100 years. Why? Because it comes with a famous name, a round number and a pleasant promise: you still have 90% left to use.

1907The original ideaJames: we use "only a small part" of our mental and physical resources.
→
1936A number is addedPreface to Carnegie's book: "the average man develops only ten per cent of his latent mental ability".
→
TodayThe subject changes"Ability" becomes "brain". A point about motivation becomes an "anatomical fact".

Sources: James (1907), Philosophical Review 16(1); Lowell Thomas's preface (1936) per Beyerstein, Scientific American, and The Psychologist (BPS).

Part 2

Where the myth came from

The sentence changed shape with every retelling. Each milestone below comes with its source.

1890s · "Reserve energy"William James and Boris Sidis argued that people have reserves of mental energy, and tested the idea by educating the child prodigy William Sidis early. (Secondary source.)
1907 · "The Energies of Men"James: "We are making use of only a small part of our possible mental and physical resources." He meant motivation and habit, not a percentage of brain tissue.
1936 · Preface "A Short-Cut to Distinction"Journalist Lowell Thomas, in How to Win Friends and Influence People: "Professor William James of Harvard used to say that the average man develops only ten per cent of his latent mental ability." No source given.
1944 · A self-improvement course adThe Pelman Institute in London: "as Science says, you are only using one-tenth of your real brain-power!" (Secondary source.)
1970s onward · Cell countsTextbooks repeat "100 billion neurons and ten times as many glial cells". That is easy to misread as "neurons are only a tenth of the brain". The figure was never measured.
Later · Credited to Einstein"Einstein said we only use 10% of our brains" spread widely. Beyerstein searched the Albert Einstein Archives and found nothing.
2014 · The film LucyIts whole plot rests on a woman gradually "unlocking" 100% of her brain.

Sources: James (1907), Philosophical Review 16(1) and Science 25(635), p. 323; Lowell Thomas's preface (1936) per Beyerstein, Scientific American, and Chudler, Neuroscience for Kids; Pelman 1944 per Wanjek (2002) via The Psychologist; Sidis per myth round-ups (secondary source); Azevedo et al. (2009) on the unmeasured "100 billion, ten times as many" figure; Beyerstein (1999) on Einstein.

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, False

11 things people say about "10% of the brain", checked against the original sources. Tap each line to see why.

4 SOLID
3 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDThe brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of its energy

Raichle and Gusnard (PNAS 2002). This rate barely changes whether we are doing maths or resting.

SOLIDWilliam James never gave a 10% figure

"The Energies of Men" (1907) says only "a small part of our resources". Beyerstein found no percentage anywhere in James's writing.

SOLIDThe 10% figure was pinned on James in a 1936 preface

Lowell Thomas wrote it in the preface to Dale Carnegie's book, gave no source, and still spoke of "latent ability", not the "brain" (Beyerstein; The Psychologist).

SOLIDNo brain region lies "dormant" waiting to be woken

Imaging, lesion studies and electrode recordings all show that every region has work to do (Beyerstein 1999).

CAUTION"People don't reach their full potential"

This was James's actual point, and it is reasonable: practice, motivation and sleep all change results. But potential cannot be measured in percentages, and it does not sit in an "unused" part of the brain.

CAUTION"Only a small share of neurons are active at a time"

True under sparse coding: Lennie (2003) estimates the ceiling may be under 1% of neurons firing hard at once. But every group gets its turn. Think of a building that never has every light on at once, not one with 90% of its rooms abandoned.

CAUTION"A 1944 ad is where the sentence moved to the brain"

The Pelman Institute ad is cited by Christopher Wanjek in Bad Medicine (2002). Mark has only seen it through a secondary source, not the original newspaper. The sentence probably mutated in several places at once.

FALSE"Humans only use 10% of their brains"

No study has ever produced this figure. All the evidence in Part 3 points the other way.

FALSE"Einstein said so"

Beyerstein searched the Albert Einstein Archives and found no such quote. Pinning a line on a famous person is the fastest way to make it spread.

FALSE"Neurons are only 10% of brain cells, the rest are useless"

The 2009 count: about 86 billion neurons and 85 billion other cells, close to 1:1 (Azevedo et al.). Glia also feed, clean and protect neurons. Careful: don't correct this myth with another "1:10" figure.

FALSE"Unlock 100% of your brain and get superpowers" (Lucy)

There is no locked part to open. The way to get better is still learning, practice and enough sleep, not "activating" the rest of the brain.

Sources: Raichle and Gusnard (2002); James (1907); Beyerstein (1999; Scientific American); The Psychologist (BPS); Lennie (2003); Wanjek (2002) via The Psychologist (secondary); Azevedo et al. (2009).

Why it is easy to believe
  • Attached to big names: James, Einstein
  • A round, memorable number
  • A pleasant promise: 90% still unused
  • A grain of truth: sparse neural firing
Where it does harm
  • Sells courses and "brain-activating" pills
  • Hides the real ways to improve: learn, practise, sleep
  • Teaches people to trust a line with no source

Almost entirely wrong about the brain, but a perfect lesson in how a sentence mutates when nobody goes back to the original.

More: why Mark gives "harm" only 2/5

Believing you use only 10% of your brain rarely hurts anyone. The harm is elsewhere: it is bait for products promising to unlock the rest, and it gets people used to quoting a "fact" without knowing who said it.

Beyerstein called it one of those hopeful shibboleths that refuses to die. Precisely because it looks harmless, few people bother to check it.

Part 6

Work lesson: trace the source before you quote

Use it whenever you are about to put a number or a quote into a slide, report, post or email to your boss.

Four red flagsA famous name with no source; "science says"; a round number; "everybody knows". Any one of them means: trace it.
Two steps backFrom what you read, go at least two steps back to where the number first appeared. By the second step the sentence has usually changed.
One source line per numberOn a slide: "Source: name, year". If you can't find it in five minutes, drop the number or label it "source unclear".
Don't fix one error with anotherWhen you debunk a myth, check the numbers you debunk it with. "Ten times as many glia" was never measured either.
We are making use of only a small part of our possible mental and physical resources.William James, "The Energies of Men" (1907). The real quote, with no number in it.
