Part 4

How solid is the evidence

The 10 most-quoted claims. Tap a row to see why.

3 SOLID
4 CAREFUL
3 WRONG
SOLIDAdults need 7–9 hours of sleep

Matches the recommendations of the major sleep medicine societies.

SOLIDAdenosine + the body clock drive sleepiness

The classic model from the 1980s. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors.

SOLIDSleeping after learning helps you remember

Replicated by many groups; the size of the effect is still debated.

CAREFUL"10 days of 7 hours = 1 all-nighter"

Cumulative decline is real, but this exact conversion doesn't match the original data (Van Dongen et al., 2003).

CAREFULREM is "overnight emotional therapy"

Evidence is thin, and much of it comes from Walker's own lab.

CAREFUL4 hours of sleep cuts natural killer cells by 70%

A small 1990s study with short-term measures. It says nothing about cancer risk.

CAREFULDaylight saving time raises heart attacks by 24%

Up on the Monday, but the weekly total barely changes.

CRITICIZED"The shorter your sleep, the shorter your life"

The data is U-shaped: both too little and too much sleep go with higher mortality. That is correlation, not causation.

WRONGThe WHO declared a "sleep loss epidemic"

No such WHO declaration can be found (Guzey, 2019).

WRONGThe young athletes injury chart

The book's version drops a data column from the original study, making the link look smoother than it is.

Strengths
  • The clearest explanation of the mechanisms
  • Actually gets readers to change habits
  • Brings in a social policy view
Weaknesses
  • Alarmist tone that can cause anxiety
  • Often turns correlation into causation
  • Has data errors that have been pointed out

Keep the mechanisms and the habits. Check the source before quoting a number.

More: published critiques

In 2019, independent researcher Alexey Guzey published a long analysis listing the book's errors; statistician Andrew Gelman and several scientists followed. Walker responded in part. The overall framework and practical advice remain widely accepted by experts.
