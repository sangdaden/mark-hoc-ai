Part 1

The question

Does a pill with no active ingredient make people healthier? Today's answer: yes, but far less than the legend says, mostly in what patients feel themselves (pain, discomfort), and most of the "effect" seen in placebo groups is really people getting better on their own.

The pricey pill "works" better

82 healthy people got mild electric shocks and the same dummy pill. Told it cost $2.50: 85% felt less pain. Told $0.10: 61% (Waber et al., 2008).

The brain releases its own painkillers

After wisdom-tooth extraction, patients were given naloxone, which blocks opioid painkillers. Those who had felt relief from a placebo got their pain back (Levine et al., 1978).

The point

For pain, expectation has a real biological pathway. This is the most solid part of the placebo effect.

Part 4

Evidence: how far to trust it

10 things often said about placebos, checked against the original studies and reviews. Tap each line to see why.

4 SOLID
4 CAUTION
2 WRONG
SOLIDPeople in placebo groups often improve noticeably

True, but that is the "response", which includes natural recovery and regression to the mean. In Kaptchuk's trial, even the group that only waited had 28% reporting relief (Kaptchuk et al., 2008).

SOLIDPlacebos reduce felt pain, by a small to moderate amount

A review of 114 trials comparing placebo with no treatment: a small benefit on continuous outcomes (standardised mean difference −0.28), concentrated in pain and self-reported outcomes (Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche, 2001). Naloxone removed placebo pain relief in responders, pointing to a biological pathway (Levine et al., 1978).

SOLIDNocebo is real: worrying about side effects can create symptoms

SAMSON trial: symptom score 15.4 in dummy-pill months, 16.3 on statins, 8.0 with no pills; nocebo ratio about 90% (Wood et al., 2020; Howard et al., 2021). Only 60 people, but double-blind and each person compared with themselves.

SOLIDTo know whether a treatment works, compare it with a placebo

Sham surgery for knee osteoarthritis: arthroscopy relieved pain no better than sham at 1 and 2 years (Moseley et al., 2002, 180 people). Without the sham group, the operation looked very effective.

CAUTIONA warm practitioner makes treatment work better (28% → 44% → 62%)

One trial, 262 people, one condition (irritable bowel), self-reported outcomes, and participants cannot be blind to how they are treated. The direction is plausible; the size needs more studies (Kaptchuk et al., 2008).

CAUTIONExpensive dummy pills work better than cheap ones (85% vs 61%)

A short research letter, 82 healthy people, lab pain from electric shocks (Waber et al., 2008). The lead author called it a small, simplified experiment. Interesting, not yet something to apply to real patients.

CAUTIONPlacebos work even when people know they are placebos

Open-label placebo for 80 people with irritable bowel: about 60% reported adequate relief versus about 35–37% with no treatment (Kaptchuk et al., 2010; figures via secondary sources). Small sample, self-reports, no blinding possible, only 3 weeks of follow-up.

CAUTIONThe placebo effect is getting stronger over time

84 neuropathic-pain drug trials, 1990–2013: placebo responses rose, but only in US trials, which also grew bigger and longer (Tuttle et al., 2015). It may be about how trials are run rather than placebos getting "stronger".

WRONG"35% of patients are cured by placebo"

Beecher's (1955) average of 35.2% came from 15 studies and 1,082 people. Kienle and Kiene (1997) re-read each study and found no evidence of a placebo effect in any of them: the improvement came from natural recovery, regression to the mean, parallel treatments and misquotation.

WRONGBelieve and you'll heal: placebos cure real diseases like tumours or infections

For "recovered or not" (binary) outcomes, placebo had no significant effect, whether reported by patients or measured objectively (Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche, 2001: relative risk 0.95, confidence interval 0.88–1.02). The 2010 Cochrane review of 234 trials concluded that placebos have no important clinical effects in general.

Strengths
  • Hundreds of trials with a no-treatment group to compare
  • A biological mechanism for pain (naloxone)
  • Nocebo measured with a double-blind design
Weaknesses
  • Most of the effect sits in self-reported outcomes, which are easily biased
  • Many famous experiments had only dozens to a few hundred people
  • Only 16 of 234 trials in the Cochrane review had low risk of bias

Robustness 6/10. Placebos do not cure disease, but they change how people feel, especially pain. The bigger lesson lies elsewhere: much of the "improvement" we see would have happened anyway.

More: Beecher's battlefield story

The often-told story: in World War II, out of morphine, a nurse injected a wounded soldier with salt water, and he still felt relief. Beecher is said to have seen this and turned to studying placebos.

Some critics say the story is hard to verify: Mark could not find Beecher's own original account of it, only retellings. Treat it as an anecdote.

What is certain is the 1955 JAMA paper "The Powerful Placebo", where the 35% figure was born and then quoted for decades.

Part 6

Lesson to apply: measure whether a change really worked

Work skill: before praising an initiative, a tool or a training course, ask "compared with what?", and use the "warmth" part deliberately.

SituationEasy wrong conclusionCheck it like a trial
A new training course"After the course everyone says they feel more confident. It works!"Compare two months of errors or sales for the trained team versus an untrained one.
Changing a process after a bad month"March had record errors, we changed the process in April, errors halved."Look at the months before March: April may simply be back to normal.
Announcing a big changeListing only risks: "it may be slow, may break, may lose data".Give risks with real odds and what has been prepared, so worry doesn't create its own "symptoms".
Ask "compared with what?"A rising number means little without a group to compare.
Beware the worst monthAny change made right after it looks effective.
Warmth is realHow you roll something out makes people feel better. Use it, but measure separately.
Watch for noceboReport risks in balance; don't plant groundless worry.
The question is not "did it get better?", but "did it get better compared with doing nothing?"A paraphrase of the spirit of the placebo-versus-no-treatment reviews (Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche, 2001), not a direct quote.
