Part 1

The question of the case

An old-line planemaker, the world's most powerful certification authority, and a system so small it was left out of the manual. How did an unchecked assumption pass through every layer of review, and why, after the first crash, did the second one still happen?

Part 3

Why: five layers of cause

The House committee wrote that the crashes "were not the result of a singular failure", but "the horrific culmination" of faulty technical assumptions by Boeing's engineers, a lack of transparency by Boeing's management, and "grossly insufficient oversight" by the FAA.

Source: US House (2020), p. 6.

Technical layer: MCAS and one sensor

Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation SystemFAA 2020, pp. 9–23

Assumption layer: 4 seconds and more than 10

pilot response assumptionHouse, pp. 13–14, 24–25; JATR, p. 14

Information layer: who knew what

culture of concealmentHouse, pp. 20–25; DOJ 2021

Boeing knew

March 2016: MCAS redesigned, stronger at low speed (p. 20). 2015: an engineer asked whether MCAS was "vulnerable to single AOA sensor failures" (p. 21). August 2017: found the AOA DISAGREE alert was inoperable on more than 80% of the fleet, deferred the fix to 2020 (pp. 22–24).

The FAA knew part of it

JATR: the FAA "had inadequate awareness of the MCAS function", and MCAS "was not evaluated as a complete and integrated function" (pp. VII–VIII). Boeing admitted two of its technical pilots deceived the FAA's training evaluation group about MCAS (DOJ).

Pilots did not know

References to MCAS were removed from the flight crew operations manual, with FAA approval (p. 21). The Lion Air 610 crew did not know about the night before, because the tech log did not record the trim cutout (KNKT, p. xviii).

Source: US House (2020), pp. 20–25; JATR (2019), pp. VII–VIII; US Department of Justice press release, 7 Jan 2021; KNKT (2019), p. xviii.

Pressure layer: "no simulator needed"

production pressureHouse, pp. 16–17, 24, 26

The Indonesian report says it plainly: the ninth of its nine contributing factors is the crew's manual handling, non-normal checklist execution and cockpit communication, issues that "had previously been identified during training and reappeared during the accident flight". The other eight are about design, certification, the sensor, maintenance and information.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is myth

13 things often said about the 737 MAX, checked against the original records. Tap a row to see why.

4 CAUTION
3 FALSE
SOLIDMCAS relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor

Confirmed in the FAA's return-to-service summary (p. 20). KNKT lists it as a contributing factor. The fix uses both sensors.

SOLIDPilots were not told MCAS existed

MCAS was removed from the flight crew operations manual with FAA approval; the committee says pilots were "largely unaware that the system existed" (House, pp. 13, 21). Boeing sent MCAS information to all operators only on 10 Nov 2018, after Lion Air (NTSB, Jan 2023, p. 7).

SOLIDThe "AOA disagree" alert did not work on most aircraft

It was wrongly tied to an optional extra, so it was "inoperable on more than 80 percent of the MAX aircraft". Boeing found this in August 2017 and deferred the fix to 2020 (House, pp. 22–24). Neither Lion Air nor Ethiopian had it (KNKT; EAIB, p. 55).

SOLIDThe flight the night before hit the same fault and survived

The crew cut out electric trim on a deadheading pilot's suggestion but did not record it in the tech log (KNKT, pp. xviii, 36; House, pp. 8–9). The situation was survivable, but the information never reached the next morning's crew.

SOLIDBoeing knew pilots could take more than 10 seconds to respond

From a November 2012 simulator test, recorded in at least six internal documents from 2015 to 2018 (House, pp. 24–25).

SOLIDAvoiding simulator training was a commercial goal

2011 Southwest contract: at least $1 million off each aircraft if the FAA required simulator training; a July 2014 Boeing press release said none would be needed (House, pp. 24, 26).

CAUTIONThe pilots did nothing wrong

KNKT names the Lion Air crew's manual handling, checklist execution and communication as a factor. For ET302 the US team points to thrust left at full power leading to overspeed, and to the crew switching electric trim back on against the procedure (pp. 6–7). The ET302 first officer had 207 hours on the 737 (House, p. 10). The errors were real, but made amid simultaneous alarms, with a system they had never been taught.

CAUTION"It was a software bug"

The software did what it was designed to do. The flaw was in the design and its assumptions: one sensor, repeated activation, pilots not told, and a system "not evaluated as a complete and integrated function" (JATR, p. VIII).

CAUTIONA bird broke the ET302 sensor

The NTSB concluded the vane separated after hitting a foreign object, "most likely a bird", and says this evidence was left out of the final report. The EAIB blames an electrical fault. The two agencies disagree; unresolved.

CAUTIONThe FAA predicted 15 more crashes and let the plane fly

An internal FAA risk analysis dated 3 Dec 2018 estimated up to 15 fatal crashes over the fleet's lifetime without an MCAS fix. It was released at a House hearing in Dec 2019; I could only read it through press reports (CNBC), so it is marked secondary.

FALSE"It was just bad foreign pilots; US pilots would not have crashed"

Boeing's own test pilot took more than 10 seconds. The two Lion Air pilots had over 5,100 and 4,200 hours on the 737. The president of the American Airlines pilots' union told Congress that claiming this would not happen to US-trained pilots "is presumptuous and not supported by fact" (House, pp. 10–11, 24–25). After the crashes, the FAA changed the design, training and manuals for every pilot, US pilots included.

FALSEAfter Lion Air, pilots were fully trained on MCAS

They received an emergency directive and a manual bulletin (Nov 2018). The flap position needed for MCAS to activate was not in Boeing's bulletin or the FAA directive (NTSB, Jan 2023, p. 6). Mandatory MCAS training came only in Nov 2020 (FAA, pp. 11, 14).

FALSE"Boeing paid a $2.5 billion fine"

The 7 Jan 2021 deferred prosecution agreement was worth over $2.5 billion, but the criminal penalty was $243.6 million. $1.77 billion went to airline customers and $500 million to a fund for victims' families (US Department of Justice).

Source: FAA (Nov 2020), pp. 11, 14, 24; NTSB investigation DCA24MA063 (Alaska 1282, 5 Jan 2024): probable cause Boeing's failure to provide adequate training, guidance and oversight; the four bolts securing the plug were not located.

Not one mistake, but an assumption carried through every layer of review without anyone checking it again, while the people who most needed to know were not told.

More: why the second crash still happened

After Lion Air, Boeing and the FAA chose a procedural patch: remind pilots of the existing "runaway stabilizer" procedure. Meanwhile, the FAA's own internal analysis estimated the risk remained high (secondary source). The aircraft kept flying while Boeing worked on a software fix.

On ET302 the crew did cut out electric trim, but by then the aircraft was so fast and so far out of trim that the wheel would not turn. The procedure was written for a "cleaner" situation than the real one. Another untested assumption.

Source: EAIB (2022), pp. 26–30; US comments (2022), pp. 6–7; CNBC, 11 Dec 2019, on the FAA's TARAM analysis.

Part 6

Work lesson: write your assumptions down, and ask who needs to know

For any design you hand to others: a product feature, an internal process, an AI model, a plan.

Find the single point of failureAsk: if just this one data source is wrong, what happens? One sensor, one API, one approver.
Don't hide things to avoid teaching themIf "no training needed" is a goal, make sure it never becomes a reason not to tell.
A mid-course change is a new designEach time scope grows, re-check the whole list of assumptions, not just the part you changed.
Log the near missThe night-before flight was saved, but no one wrote down how. Record the save, too.
These crashes are demonstrable evidence that our current system of aircraft design and certification has failed us.Captain Chesley Sullenberger, testimony to the US House (June 2019), quoted in the committee report, pp. 11–12.
