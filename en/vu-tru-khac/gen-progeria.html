Essence

A very rare diseaseBabies look healthy at birth, then grow slowly, lose hair, and their blood vessels stiffen. Intelligence is not affected.
A family that did not waitIn 1999, two doctors whose son had the disease set up a research foundation, when almost no one studied it.
18 of 20 casesIn 2003 the gene was found: the same single letter changed in LMNA, newly arisen, not passed down by parents.
First drug, 2020The FDA approved lonafarnib. It lowers mortality and extends life, but does not fix the gene.
No cure yetGene editing helped mice live more than twice as long. Not yet in people.

Part 2

The story

Who, where, when, and why one family had to go looking for answers themselves.

1886 and 1897 · The disease gets a nameTwo English doctors, Jonathan Hutchinson and then Hastings Gilford, described children who aged early. "Progeria" comes from Greek: "pro", before, and "gēras", old age. Today's full name: Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome.
Summer 1998 · A diagnosis in the USSam, son of two doctors, Leslie Gordon and Scott Berns, was diagnosed with progeria at about 22 months old. His parents searched the literature and found almost no research, no diagnostic test and no funding.
Early 1999 · A foundation of their ownThe family gathered relatives, friends and colleagues and founded the Progeria Research Foundation (PRF). The goal: find the cause, then a treatment.
16/4/2003 · The gene is foundA team at the US National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI) led by Francis Collins, together with PRF, announced that progeria is caused by mutations in the LMNA gene. Leslie Gordon was a co-author. The paper appeared in Nature in May 2003.
2013 · Sam talks about a happy lifeAt 16, Sam spoke at TEDxMidAtlantic about how he lived: be OK with what you can't do, because there is so much you can do. A documentary about him aired on HBO in October 2013.
10/1/2014 · Sam dies at 17The foundation carried on. By 30 June 2026, PRF had identified 223 children and young adults with progeria or related conditions in 55 countries.

Source: PRF "Our story" (1998, 22 months, 1999, date of death); NHGRI press release 16/4/2003; Eriksson et al. (2003); PRF Quick Facts 30/6/2026 (223 people, 55 countries); Radiopaedia and PRF (1886, 1897, origin of the name, secondary source); TEDxMidAtlantic 2013 talk and the film "Life According to Sam" (secondary sources).

Why is a rare disease hard to study?

The rarer the disease, the fewer patients to study, the fewer cell samples, the less money. Progeria is estimated to affect about 1 in 4 to 8 million births (estimate, secondary source). In 2008, a study that examined 15 children in depth covered nearly half of all known cases at the time.

A family-run foundation can do what a single lab struggles to: find patients worldwide, collect samples, connect researchers.

Source: The Scientist and an ISPOR 2010 conference abstract (estimated rate, secondary sources); Merideth et al. (2008), NEJM (15 children, nearly half of known cases then).

Part 3

How the gene works, in 3 sentences

1. The LMNA gene makes lamin A, a mesh lining the inside of the nuclear envelope that keeps the cell nucleus round and sturdy.

2. Newly made lamin A carries a "fatty tail" (a farnesyl group); normally an enzyme acts like scissors and cuts off the end piece that holds this tail.

3. In progeria, one letter C becomes T, so the cell cuts out 50 amino acids, including the spot where the scissors cut; the faulty protein (progerin) keeps its fatty tail, sticks to the nuclear envelope and slowly warps the nucleus.

Part 4

Fact check: what is true and what is rumour

13 things often said about progeria, checked against original sources. Tap a row to see why.

5 SOLID
4 CAUTION
4 FALSE
SOLIDProgeria is caused by a point mutation in the LMNA gene

18 of 20 classic cases had the same single-letter change (GGC to GGT); one more case had a different change in the same codon (Eriksson et al. 2003).

SOLIDChildren with progeria have normal intelligence

Motor development and intelligence are not affected (Mayo Clinic, secondary source). That is one reason not to describe them as "old people in children's bodies".

SOLIDThe main cause of death is cardiovascular disease

Heart attack or stroke (Merideth et al. 2008; GeneReviews).

SOLIDLonafarnib is the first FDA-approved drug for progeria

On 20/11/2020, for patients aged 1 and older; the European Union followed in July 2022 (FDA; EMA).

SOLIDA family-founded charity helped find the gene

PRF and NHGRI announced the gene in 2003; Leslie Gordon co-authored the Nature paper (NHGRI 2003; Eriksson 2003).

CAUTION"The drug adds 2.5 years of life"

That is the average at the longest follow-up (up to 11 years). Over the first 3 years the gain was about 3 months. The comparison was with untreated children from another study, not a randomised trial (FDA, via Contemporary Pediatrics and Fierce Pharma, secondary sources).

CAUTION"Progeria is normal ageing on fast-forward"

There are similarities (stiff vessels, hair loss) but not a full match. Right after the 2008 NEJM paper, three gerontologists wrote a letter objecting to equating the two (letter by Ershler, Ferrucci and Longo).

CAUTION"Progerin is the key to everyone's ageing"

Skin cells from people aged 81–96 carry a little progerin and nuclear defects like progeria (Scaffidi and Misteli 2006). But those were cultured cells; it does not show that progerin drives whole-body ageing.

CAUTION"One in 4 million children has it"

That is an estimate from reviews (about 1 in 4 to 8 million births; secondary sources). The counted number is clearer: PRF has identified 163 people with classic progeria (30/6/2026).

FALSE"Lonafarnib cures progeria"

The drug stops the protein from getting its fatty tail and lowers mortality, but does not fix the gene. Average lifespan with the drug is about 18.7 years (GeneReviews).

FALSE"Parents pass progeria to their children"

The mutation usually arises new in the child, and usually no one else in the family is affected (Eriksson 2003; MedlinePlus Genetics). It is no one's fault.

FALSE"There is already a gene-editing cure for people"

Gene editing worked in mice in 2021 (Koblan et al.). No human gene-editing trial for progeria has been published (checked 16/10/2026).

FALSE"Benjamin Button had progeria"

That is fiction by F. Scott Fitzgerald (1922): the character is born old and grows younger. Progeria does not reverse age and no one "grows young".

Source: as given in each row; full list in Part 9.

Part 5

Where things stand today

From understanding the gene to the first drug, then to gene editing in mice.

2005 · Block the fatty tail, the nucleus looks betterIn mouse cells carrying the progeria mutation, a drug that blocks the enzyme adding the fatty (farnesyl) tail moved progerin off the envelope, and the nuclei blebbed less.
2006 · A little progerin in older peopleSkin cells from people aged 81–96 sometimes use the same faulty splice site as progeria. An intriguing hint, so far in cultured cells.
2018 · Evidence of longer life27 children on lonafarnib were compared with 27 similar untreated children: after about 2.2 years, 1 child (3.7%) versus 9 children (33.3%) had died. Observational, not randomised.
20/11/2020 · FDA approves the first drugLonafarnib, based on 62 patients in two single-arm trials. Europe approved it in July 2022.
2021 · Gene editing in miceOne injection of a "base editor" changed the T back to C; median mouse lifespan rose from 215 to 510 days.
2025–2026 · A second drug in testingProgerinin, an oral drug aimed at progerin, has been in a phase 2 trial since January 2025. No published results, not approved (checked 16/10/2026).

Source: Yang et al. (2005), PNAS; Scaffidi and Misteli (2006), Science 312: 1059–1063; Gordon et al. (2018), JAMA; FDA 20/11/2020; EMA, Zokinvy EPAR; Koblan et al. (2021), Nature 589: 608–614 (215 and 510 days); ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06775041 (progerinin); Pharmaceutical Technology 3/2026 (secondary source).

More: why does Mark give "treatment" only 2/5?

The drug is real progress: for the first time something helps children live longer. But average lifespan on the drug is still under 20 years, and it has side effects such as nausea, diarrhoea and fatigue. A real cure means fixing the gene, and that has only been done in mice.

Source: GeneReviews (about 18.7 years); FDA 20/11/2020 (common side effects, via AJMC, secondary source); Koblan et al. (2021). The comments are Mark's.

Part 7

Takeaway: reading "this drug helps people live longer" correctly

When you read news about a new drug, three questions help you understand the number.

For reading and understanding, not a substitute for medical advice.

Ask "compared with whom"A randomised trial is more reliable than a comparison with a group taken from another study.
Ask "after how long"The number changes with follow-up time. News often picks the nicest number.
Remember "average"A group average promises nothing to one person. Ask the doctor about the specific case.
"Newly arisen" is nobody's faultFor a disease caused by a new mutation, ask a genetics doctor about the risk for a future pregnancy rather than guessing.
Self-check: try it on a health story you read this week

1. Find a story saying something like "drug X helps people live longer / cuts Y by Z%".

2. Does it say what group it was compared with? Write it down.

3. Does it say how long people were followed, and how many? Write it down.

4. Does it say "cures", or "lowers", "extends"? Which word fits the number?

5. Missing two or more of these: treat the story as "needs more reading", not ready to share.

The self-check is Mark's suggestion, not a procedure from a study.
