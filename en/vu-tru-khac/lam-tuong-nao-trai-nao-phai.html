Essence

The halves do specialiseLanguage usually sits on the left, spatial attention leans right. This part is true.
Born in split brainsThe findings came from epilepsy patients whose bridge between hemispheres was cut.
1,011 brains, no "types"Nobody's whole brain leans to one side. Lateralisation is a region-by-region matter.

Part 1

The question behind the myth

How did a Nobel-winning finding about surgically divided brains turn into a way of sorting ordinary people, and where does it go wrong? The short answer: the hemispheres divide the work, but people do not divide into two kinds.

1962–1981A real findingIn patients with a severed corpus callosum, each hemisphere processes information on its own.

Part 2

Where the myth came from

Each retelling dropped one important condition.

1861–1865 · Paul BrocaPatients with damage to the left hemisphere lost the ability to speak. Broca wrote: "we speak with the left hemisphere". The first widely accepted evidence of specialisation.
1962 · The first reported split-brain caseGazzaniga, Bogen and Sperry tested a 48-year-old war veteran whose corpus callosum had just been cut to treat severe epilepsy (PNAS).
1972 and 1979 · Two bestsellersRobert Ornstein, The Psychology of Consciousness (1972), appeared in the middle of the "left/right fad"; Betty Edwards, Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain (1979), is called by Corballis the epitome of the "right brain does creativity" view.
8 Dec 1981 · Sperry wins the Nobel and warns"The left-right dichotomy in cognitive mode is an idea with which it is very easy to run wild."
2012 · The myth in the classroom91% of surveyed UK teachers and 86% of Dutch teachers believed "left brain, right brain" explains differences between learners.
2013 · 1,011 brains scannedA University of Utah team found no "left-brained" or "right-brained" people (PLOS ONE).

Sources: Gazzaniga, Bogen and Sperry, PNAS 1962; Sperry, Nobel lecture 8 Dec 1981, printed in Science 217 (1982), p. 1226; Dekker et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2012, table 1; Nielsen et al., PLOS ONE 2013. The description of Ornstein's book as part of the "left/right fad" is a secondary source.

Fact check: Solid, Caution, False

SOLIDThe hemispheres specialise, for example language is usually on the left

From Broca in the 1860s to modern measurements. Among healthy strong right-handers, only about 4% have language mainly on the right (Knecht et al., Brain 2000).

SOLIDCutting the corpus callosum makes the hemispheres process information separately

The research line began with Gazzaniga, Bogen and Sperry (PNAS 1962) and earned Sperry the 1981 Nobel. These are results in surgical patients, not in ordinary people.

SOLIDThe myth is very widespread, even among teachers

In a survey of 242 teachers interested in brain science, 91% in the UK and 86% in the Netherlands agreed with the "hemispheric dominance" statement; only 3% and 4% answered correctly (Dekker et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2012, table 1).

CAUTIONThe right hemisphere has a slight edge on some creative tasks

The meta-analysis by Mihov et al. (2010) found a relative right advantage. The review by Dietrich and Kanso (2010) concluded creativity is not tied to one region or one hemisphere. Corballis (2014): creative thought activates a widespread network. "A slight edge on some tasks" is far from "creativity lives in the right brain".

CAUTIONSpatial attention and face recognition "belong" to the right hemisphere

They do lean right in many people (Anderson, University of Utah press release 2013: attention more on the right). But "leaning" is not "owning": the left side still takes part.

CAUTIONSplit-brain patients have "two minds" in one head

That is the classic reading. Pinto et al. (Brain 2017) tested 2 patients who could respond to stimuli on either side and proposed "split perception, undivided consciousness". Volz and Gazzaniga (2017) disagreed. The question is open.

FALSEEach person has a dominant hemisphere that sets their personality and talents

Nielsen et al. (2013) analysed 1,011 people: lateralised connections did not go together within individuals, and there was no whole-brain "left-brained" or "right-brained" type.

FALSELogic and maths live in the left brain

Calculation relies on fronto-parietal networks on both sides (Arsalidou and Taylor, NeuroImage 2011); number sense uses parietal regions in both hemispheres (Dehaene et al., 2003). The verbal side of a maths problem leans left, like anything involving language.

FALSELeft-handers are "right-brained" and therefore more creative

Even among strong left-handers, only about 27% have language mainly on the right; about 3/4 still have it on the left (Knecht et al., 2000). Handedness says nothing about a "brain type".

FALSEAn online quiz can tell you whether you are "left-brained" or "right-brained"

If no such types exist in the brain (Nielsen 2013), the quiz only measures self-reported preferences and gives them a scientific-sounding name. "Hemisphere balancing" exercise programmes are also listed by Dekker et al. among educational neuromyths.

The true core
  • The hemispheres really do specialise
  • Language leans left in almost everyone
  • Split-brain research is first-rate, Nobel-winning science
Where it was inflated
  • From "per function" to "per person"
  • From surgically divided brains to normal brains
  • From "a slight edge on some tasks" to "creativity is on the right"

A half-truth about the brain turned into a falsehood about people.

More: Sperry himself warned about it

In his 1981 Nobel lecture, Sperry wrote about the growing wave of "semipopular extrapolations and speculations" on left-brain and right-brain functions, and cautioned: "The left-right dichotomy in cognitive mode is an idea with which it is very easy to run wild."

The myth survives because it is handy: two boxes, one quiz, one label. It also sounds scientific, because there really is a Nobel Prize behind it. Corballis (2014) calls most of these speculations "exaggerated or ill-founded".

Sources: Sperry, "Some Effects of Disconnecting the Cerebral Hemispheres", Nobel lecture 8 Dec 1981, Science 217 (1982), p. 1226; Corballis, PLOS Biology 12(1), 2014.

Part 6

Work lessons: don't label colleagues as "types"

Applies when you hand out work, form a project team, write a year-end review, or hear someone say "she's just not the creative type".

Turn adjectives into verbs"Creative" → "proposed 3 new approaches for the Q3 campaign". Only assign work on a claim you can write as a verb.
Run a small trial before judgingA two-hour sketch, a sample spreadsheet. A real result beats the impression "she isn't a numbers person".
Teams need tasks, not "types"Build the team from a list of tasks and people who have done them, not "one left-brain, one right-brain".
Watch for self-applied labels"I'm right-brained, I don't do Excel" is a label too. Ask: how long did you try to learn it, and did anyone show you?
The left-right dichotomy in cognitive mode is an idea with which it is very easy to run wild.Roger Sperry, Nobel lecture, 8 December 1981 (printed in Science, 1982).
