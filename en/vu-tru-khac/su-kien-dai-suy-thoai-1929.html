Part 1

The question of the event

Why did a stock market fall and an ordinary recession stretch into a decade of hardship? Because how the world responded in the first three years mattered more than the first shock.

The answer is Mark's reading, based on Federal Reserve History and Friedman and Schwartz (1963). Economists still debate the causes: see Part 3 and Part 5.

→
1930–1933The spiralBanks fail, money shrinks, prices fall, debts grow heavier, more banks fail.

1933–1939New rulesBanks closed, gold abandoned, deposits insured. Recovery, then a relapse in 1937.

Part 3

Key decisions

Five decisions, seen with the eyes of the time: who decided, what they knew, what they chose, what happened next. Many choices followed the "rules of the game" of that era.

1 · After the crash, October 1929: pump in money

New York Fed · George Harrison

What they knew

Stocks fell nearly 13% on 28 October. New York banks were carrying loans to stock market players.

What they chose

Buy government bonds, speed up discount-window lending, cut the rate, promise all the reserves banks needed.

What happened

The banking system got through the stock market shock. This response is judged a good one.

Source: Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of 1929" (via search-result excerpts).

2 · The 1930 banking panic: let the market sort it out

Reserve banks, Federal Reserve Board

What they knew

In late 1930 banks failed in droves; 1,350 suspended over the year. Many small banks were not Fed members. A common belief then: weak banks failing "cleans out" the system.

What they chose

No broad emergency lending. For the Bank of United States, the New York Fed and big banks tried a merger; talks broke down and the bank closed on 11 December 1930.

What happened

The news spread nationwide and people withdrew money. Friedman and Schwartz saw the 1930 panic as a major factor turning an ordinary recession into the Great Depression. Some scholars think that bank was already insolvent.

Source: Federal Reserve History, "Banking Panics of 1930–31"; FDIC history page 1930–1939 (merger attempt, debate over solvency); Friedman and Schwartz (1963), via secondary summaries.

3 · Britain leaves gold, October 1931: raise rates to keep gold

New York Fed · gold standard

What they knew

On 21 September 1931 Britain left the gold standard. Gold flowed out of the US. By the central-banking "book" of the time, you stop a gold drain with high rates.

What they chose

In early October (9 October per the FDIC), raise the discount rate from 1.5% to 3.5%.

What happened

Gold was kept, but money was tightened while banks were failing. A new panic ran on into 1933.

Source: Federal Reserve History, "Banking Panics of 1931–33"; FDIC history page 1930–1939 (9 Oct 1931); Marriner Eccles memo on FRASER (1.5% to 3.5%).

4 · March 1933: close to reopen, then leave gold

President Roosevelt, Congress, Treasury, Fed

What they knew

A new panic spread across the country in early 1933; many states had already closed their banks.

What they chose

Close every bank for about a week from 6 March; the Emergency Banking Act (9 March) let only sound banks reopen. April 1933 gold must be handed in; January 1934 gold goes to $35 an ounce.

What happened

The banking spiral stopped. In 1934, the first year of deposit insurance, only 9 insured banks failed.

Source: Federal Reserve History, "Bank Holiday of 1933" and "Emergency Banking Act of 1933"; FDIC (9 failures in 1934); Executive Order 6102 and the Gold Reserve Act from Wikipedia and EBSCO (secondary sources).

5 · 1936–37: support withdrawn too early?

Federal Reserve Board · reserve requirements

What they knew

Banks' excess reserves rose from $859 million (December 1933) to over $3.3 billion (December 1935). The Fed feared an "injurious credit expansion".

What they chose

Double reserve requirement ratios, in several steps in 1936–37.

What happened

Recession again in 1937–38. Whether this caused it is still debated: Friedman and Schwartz say yes; Calomiris, Mason and Wheelock say the effect was small.

Source: Federal Reserve History, "Recession of 1937–38"; Calomiris, Mason and Wheelock, NBER Working Paper 16688.

What do economists still debate about the causes?

Money (Friedman and Schwartz, 1963): banking panics and the Fed letting the money stock shrink by about a third (1929–1933) were the main cause.

Spending (Temin, 1976): spending fell first; the 1930 panic was not a major deflationary shock.

The gold standard (Eichengreen, 1992; Temin, 1989): the rules of gold tied central banks' hands worldwide and carried the shock across borders.

The credit channel (Bernanke, 1983): collapsing banks cut the lending channel; falling prices made debts heavier.

Hsieh and Romer (2006) found that the $1 billion of purchases in 1932 led almost no one to expect a dollar devaluation, meaning gold did not bind the Fed as tightly as often thought, at least that year. A 1995 survey of economic historians (Whaples) showed they still disagree a lot about the causes. The closest thing to a consensus: an ordinary downturn made much worse by the Fed, the gold standard and the collapse of banking. There is no single settled answer.

Source: Friedman and Schwartz (1963); Temin (1976, 1989); Eichengreen (1992); Bernanke (1983); Hsieh and Romer (2006), Journal of Economic History 66(1); Whaples (1995), Journal of Economic History 55(1). Via summaries and excerpts; "a third" is Friedman and Schwartz's figure as quoted in secondary summaries.

Part 4

What the world learned

Things we take for granted today were born in this decade.

Deposit insuranceThe Banking Act of 16 June 1933 created the FDIC; coverage ran from 1 January 1934.
Lender of last resortThe central-bank lesson: in a panic, lend freely instead of letting the system sort itself out.
Securities oversightThe Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the SEC.
A safety netThe Social Security Act was signed on 14 August 1935.
Don't let gold tie your handsCountries that left gold early tended to recover sooner (Eichengreen and Sachs, 1985).
Don't withdraw support too earlyThe 1937–38 lesson, even though its cause is still debated.

Part 5

Fact check: what's true and what's myth

14 things often said about the Great Depression, checked against the Fed, FDIC, CDC and peer-reviewed research. Tap each line for the reason.

SOLIDStocks lost nearly 90% from the 1929 peak to the 1932 bottom

Dow Jones 381.17 (3 Sep 1929) down to 41.22 (8 Jul 1932), a fall of 89% (Mark's calculation). Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of 1929".

SOLIDAbout 9,000 banks suspended

FDIC: from autumn 1929 to the end of 1933, depositors lost about $1.3 billion. Another FDIC publication counts "more than 9,000" up to the March 1933 bank holiday. State the time window when quoting it.

SOLIDThe Fed let the money supply shrink sharply

Federal Reserve History: nearly 30% from autumn 1930 to winter 1933, the Fed's most serious "sin of omission". Friedman and Schwartz: about a third, 1929–1933 (different window).

SOLIDThe suicide rate rose during the Depression

CDC (2011), Luo et al.: 18.0 (1928) up to 22.1 per 100,000 (1932), the highest ever recorded. The biggest rise was among working-age adults.

SOLIDCountries that left gold early recovered sooner

Eichengreen and Sachs (1985), Journal of Economic History: early devaluers had better industrial output and exports. Note: a single country's devaluation hurt its neighbours.

CAUTION"The Fed alone caused the Great Depression"

The Fed made big mistakes (Federal Reserve History says so itself), but there was also the gold standard (Eichengreen), a fall in spending (Temin), and the collapse of the credit channel (Bernanke 1983). No single settled answer (Whaples 1995).

CAUTION"The Fed officially apologised"

The 2002 apology was a remark by Ben Bernanke, then one governor, at a celebration for Milton Friedman. Not a Fed resolution. The Fed's own Federal Reserve History site does say the Fed made errors.

CAUTION"The Smoot-Hawley tariff caused the Great Depression"

The law came in 1930, after the downturn had begun; 1,028 economists signed a letter against it beforehand (5 May 1930, secondary source). Eichengreen (1986): small direct macro effect. Irwin (2011): it harmed world trade. A contributor, not the main cause.

CAUTION"Treasury Secretary Mellon advised liquidating everything"

The line "liquidate labor, liquidate stocks, liquidate the farmers" appears only in Hoover's memoirs (1951), with no independent source. Hoover presented it as the view of a camp he did not side with (secondary source).

CAUTION"The New Deal prolonged the Depression"

Whaples survey (1995): about half of economists and 27% of historians agreed wholly or partly (via secondary sources). Researchers are split.

CAUTION"Doubling reserve requirements caused the 1937–38 recession"

Friedman and Schwartz say yes. Calomiris, Mason and Wheelock (NBER) find the requirements did not bind banks, so the effect was small. Federal Reserve History gives both views.

FALSE"The day of the stock market crash caused the Great Depression"

The downturn began in August 1929, before the crash. The crash was a big shock, but the worst damage came later: three banking panics in 1930–1933 and policy decisions (Federal Reserve History; Friedman and Schwartz).

FALSE"Investors jumped out of windows en masse"

Manhattan: 44 suicides in the 4 weeks after the crash, fewer than 53 in the same period of 1928 (Norris, via History.com, secondary source). Galbraith called it a legend. There were a few real individual cases.

FALSE"It was America's problem alone"

Federal Reserve History describes a series of national and international financial crises in 1931–1933. Britain left gold in September 1931; the shock travelled through the gold standard system (Eichengreen).

  • Deposit insurance, the SEC, Social Security
Got wrong
  • Let banks fail in a chain through 1930–1933
  • Raised rates to keep gold in the middle of a panic (1931)
  • Pumped in 1932 then stopped; tightened again in 1936–37

The 1929 shock was not the worst of it. The worst was three years of letting the spiral run, in the belief that the system would clean itself and the gold rule had to be kept at any cost.

More: being fair to people at the time

In 1930 there was no theory of a lender of last resort for a system of thousands of small banks, no monthly unemployment data, and keeping gold was seen as duty. Raising rates in 1931 was by the book of the time.

But not everyone thought so: the New York Fed pumped in money in 1929 and wanted to do more; in 1932 the Fed did pump, then stopped on its own. Other choices were on the table, just not chosen.

Mark's commentary, based on Federal Reserve History and Hsieh and Romer (2006).

Part 7

Work lesson: how you respond matters more than the first shock

For any team facing a problem that is spreading: customers leaving, projects slipping in a chain, rumours inside the company.

Cut the loop first, find fault laterWhile a problem is spreading, the first job is to stop it: more people, more money, call the customers. The "who was wrong" meeting can wait a week.
What is this rule for?Every hard rule ("we never discount", "nobody skips a level") must answer: which goal does it protect, and does it still protect it now?
Withdraw support by numbers, not by calendarWrite it down in advance: which metric must stay stable for how long before you pull people or budget away from what you just rescued.
Say clearly what is guaranteedIn a crisis people run because they are unsure. One clear sentence ("this month's salaries are paid in full", "orders already placed will ship") stops many runs.
Check your own team: 5 questions

1. At your last incident, how long did the team take from noticing to stopping the spread? What was most of that time spent on?

2. Does the team have a "gold standard": a rule nobody dares break even while it does harm?

3. Who is allowed to add people or money within 24 hours without waiting for a meeting?

4. The last time the team withdrew support from something it had just rescued, which number did it rely on?

5. If customers or colleagues are worried, what guarantee can the team state today?
