Part 1

The question

If 21 days is enough, why does day 22 still take willpower? Because the number was never measured for habits. When someone finally measured it, the answer was three times longer and varied enormously from person to person.

Part 2

Where the myth came from

The sentence was distorted in two steps: it lost the word "minimum", and "self-image" turned into "habit".

1960 · Maxwell Maltz, Psycho-CyberneticsA plastic surgeon. He saw patients take about 21 days to get used to a new face, amputees feel a "phantom limb" for about as long, and people need about three weeks in a new house before it feels like home.
The following decades · Self-help books and coursesThe line was retold countless times, getting shorter, until it became the "21-day challenge".
2009–2010 · Phillippa Lally and colleagues, UCLThe first day-by-day measurement of habit formation in real life. Median 66 days.
2012 · UCL's behavioural science blogTraces Maltz's sentence and calls its use for habits "unfounded".
2021–2024 · Later studiesA randomised trial, millions of gym visits, a systematic review: all find weeks to months, with no single number.

Sources: UCL Health Behaviour Research blog (2012); UCL press release (8/2009); Keller et al. (2021); Buyalskaya et al. (PNAS, 2023); Singh et al. (Healthcare, 2024).

Round and memorable

Three weeks. Short enough that anyone dares to start, long enough to sound serious.

A doctor's name on it

A real doctor, a real book. Few people open the preface to read the original sentence.

Sometimes it's true

Some people, with simple behaviours, really do find it easier after three weeks. They tell the story, and it spreads.

Part 4

Fact check: Solid, Caution, Wrong

10 things people say about habits, including variants of "21 days". Tap each line to see why.

3 SOLID
4 CAUTION
3 WRONG
SOLIDRepeating a behaviour in the same context makes it gradually automatic

That is how Lally (2010) designed and measured it: same behaviour, same context, every day. Keller (2021) found that tying it to an existing routine or to a fixed time worked equally well; what matters is repeating it at the planned moment.

SOLIDThe first repetitions help the most

Lally describes automaticity rising along an asymptotic curve: steep at first, slower later. The "it's getting easier" feeling after a few weeks is real.

SOLIDA missed day doesn't ruin the process

Lally (2010) abstract: missing one opportunity "did not materially affect" it. But that is evidence about a single miss, not about long breaks or frequent dropping out.

CAUTION"Actually, it's 66 days"

66 is the median of the 39 people whose data fitted well, not a figure for everyone. The range was 18–254 days. Turning "66 days" into a new slogan repeats exactly the mistake of "21 days".

CAUTION"For simple things, 21 days is enough"

Possibly, for some people: the fastest person in Lally needed 18 days, and hospital handwashing became habitual within weeks (PNAS 2023). But no study shows 21 days as a general mark.

CAUTION"Go to the gym 4 times a week for 6 weeks and it's a habit"

Kaushal and Rhodes (2015) called this the minimum threshold among 111 new gym members in a 12-week observational study. A floor, not a promise.

CAUTIONThe 66-day figure is firmly established

Small sample, self-ratings, everyone chose a different behaviour, and no exact replication yet. Later studies (Keller 2021: median 59; Singh 2024: medians 59–66) find numbers of the same size, so the general direction is fairly solid; the exact figure is not.

WRONG"Dr Maltz proved that 21 days forms a habit"

Maltz wrote about adjusting to a self-image, based on watching patients, and wrote "a minimum of about 21 days". There is no habit experiment here. (Original sentence quoted via the UCL blog 2012, secondary source.)

WRONG"After 21 days the habit runs itself, no more effort"

At the median, day 21 is still on the rising part of the curve. Many people quit right here, thinking it "isn't for them".

WRONG"Miss one day and you have to start over"

The opposite of Lally (2010). Streak counting makes a single miss look like failure, while the curve barely changes.

Sources: Lally et al. (2010); Keller et al. (2021); Kaushal and Rhodes (2015); Buyalskaya et al. (2023); Singh et al. (2024); UCL Health Behaviour Research blog (2012).

How harmful

The true core
  • Regular repetition makes things easier
  • The first few weeks improve the most
  • For a few people and behaviours, three weeks really is enough
What's wrong
  • The number was never measured for habits
  • It promises one mark for everyone and everything
  • It makes people give up on day 22

A gentle myth: nobody is badly hurt, but it sets the wrong expectation, so many people quit just as the habit is about to form.

More: why harm is only 2/5

"21 days" still encourages people to start and repeat, and that is the true part. The harm is in the expectation: when day 22 is still hard, people conclude "I lack willpower" or "this isn't for me", when they are simply in the middle of the curve.

The second harm comes from streak counting: miss a day and the "21-day challenge" makes you start over, so many people stop altogether. The research says a single miss barely matters.

Part 6

Work lesson: plan for 2–3 months, tie it to a fixed context

For a new habit or skill at work: keeping a work log, learning a new tool for a few minutes a day, preparing before each meeting.

Tie it to something already fixed"After I make my morning coffee, I open my plan file for 5 minutes." Same place, same time, every day.
Small enough for your worst day5 minutes with the new tool, 3 lines of log. Raise the bar later, once it runs itself.
Missed a day: carry on the nextDon't restart the count, don't double up. Avoid missing two days in a row.
For a team: don't promise "21 days"When rolling out a new process, say up front "about a quarter to get used to it". Fewer people lose heart in week four.
Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process.Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle, abstract, European Journal of Social Psychology (2010).
