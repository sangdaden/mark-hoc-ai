Under the hood: the chart rules

Compare groups: bars. Over time: a line. How the mix changes: stacked bars by week. Avoid pies with many slices or two dates to compare; eyes are bad at comparing angles. A bar chart axis must start at 0.

Under the hood: precision and recall

Precision: of the customers who got a voucher, how many really left. Recall: of the customers who really left, how many got one. Lower the threshold and you catch more leavers (recall up) but waste more vouchers (precision down).

Probabilities come from the LogisticRegression model in the video's notebook (176 customers to learn, 59 to test). At 0.5 and 0.3 the numbers match the video exactly. Voucher cost and customer value are assumptions.

Station 3 · Videos 348, 42, 241

Grow the tree deeper: when does the model start memorizing?

A decision tree asks yes/no questions to guess whether a customer will leave. Deeper trees ask more questions. The train score is on customers it has seen; the test score is on new ones.

Under the hood: why the test score drops

There are only 235 customers and 2 months of data. A deeper tree memorizes random details of single customers (some leaves hold just 1 customer), so it guesses wrong on new ones. Tip: always print the train score next to the test score; the bigger the gap, the more you should suspect overfitting.

Every score was really computed with DecisionTreeClassifier in the video’s notebook, depth 1 to 13 (13 is the unlimited tree).

Under the hood: the motor model

This page runs the exact model from the video: a motor τ·dω/dt = K·u − ω − load (K = 2, τ = 0.15 s), voltage arriving 30 ms late, ±12 V limit, a 360-tick encoder averaged over 5 readings. The I term stops adding up when the voltage hits the limit.

Under the hood: what the EKF does each step

Every 50 ms the EKF predicts the new position from the encoders and its uncertainty circle grows. The upward camera looks 10 times a second; any marker within 1 m corrects the position and the circle shrinks. The more accurate source gets more weight.

This page re-runs the video's simulation on your device (right wheel 0.5% bigger, about 1% random slip per wheel). Slip is random, so each run differs a little from the video: odometry 2.9 m off, EKF 1.8 cm average error.

Under the hood: how A* scores a cell

Each cell scores f = g + h: g is the distance already travelled, h is the estimated distance left to the table (8 directions, no corner cutting). A* always opens the lowest f, so it heads for the goal. Dijkstra drops h and spreads evenly in every direction: same shortest path, many more cells opened.

The map is a 5 cm grid, 160 x 100 cells, taken from the video's 43-second survey run. The video's challenge: move the pillar and compare the new route with the old one.
